# South Africa

## FILE ROLE & METHOD

This file is a single national staging brief: one TCCC hero beverage per
scene (chosen per brief, never a hardcoded default — see HERO PRODUCT SLOT),
staged against real South African dishes, vessels, and settings, with the
full iconic-beverage landscape documented as cultural context even where a
specific beverage (alcohol) is never itself staged.

**Scope** (inherited from the scaffold, not re-litigated): lunch, dinner
(widely called "supper"), and snacks are in scope. Breakfast is out of
scope except via the opt-in Morning Module. Other beverages are documented
in ICONIC BEVERAGES but not staged, except the chosen hero product and,
where a brief explicitly allows it, one named non-alcoholic companion.

### Structural decision: one file, five internal zones — decided, not re-litigated

**One `south-africa.md` with five labeled internal zones** (Western Cape,
KwaZulu-Natal, Gauteng & Highveld, Eastern Cape, Interior & North), rather
than a US-style national-index-plus-regional-files split. This mirrors the
single-file-with-internal-zones pattern already established for Germany and
Spain, and is an ordinary structural call this project makes unilaterally
per `country-file-schema.md` §1.1 — it is **not** the part of this file's
structure that needs human sign-off (see below).

The scaffold's own swap test (§1.1), summarized: food mostly travels
nationally (the braai, pap and chakalaka, vetkoek, biltong, fried and
peri-peri chicken, the garage pie, curry and rice, the Sunday plate, the
kota and boerie roll read correctly almost anywhere), while a few
serving-format dishes are genuinely zone-coded (a Gatsby on paper reads
Cape Town; a bunny chow with its bread plug reads Durban) and are handled
as zone-coded variants rather than triggering a full split. This resolves
the same way Germany's regional dish variation resolved: real, sourced,
dish-level variation handled via §4.6 disclosure and zone callouts, not a
file split.

### Settlement-register framing — PENDING HUMAN SIGN-OFF, not resolved by this pass

The scaffold additionally proposes four cross-cutting **settlement
registers** — suburban, township, urban-flat, rural/farm — as the primary
axis for staging environment scenes (ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES below),
used instead of, or alongside, the five geographic zones, on the finding
that a township street and a walled suburban house differ more visually
than Pretoria and Durban do.

**This framing is preserved exactly as the scaffold built it, and this
pass explicitly declines to resolve it — it is a different kind of open
question than the "one file vs. split" call above.** The scaffold itself
flagged why: settlement type in South Africa is inseparable from race,
class, and apartheid-era spatial planning (the geography of townships,
"matchbox" and RDP housing, and the suburb/township divide is a direct,
documented legacy of forced removals and group-areas planning, not a
neutral housing-style choice the way "urban flat vs. farmhouse" is in
Germany or Spain). Deciding how to frame — or whether to frame at all —
scenes built around that divide is an editorial and brand-safety decision
for a human reviewer, not a research question this pass can settle with
sources. **Status: PENDING HUMAN SIGN-OFF.** The four registers are kept
in the file, described factually and without a poverty-only or
sanitized-suburb caricature in either direction (see Caricature avoidance
below), so the file remains usable while sign-off is pending — but no
scene should be generated from this file's settlement-register content
until a human reviewer has confirmed the framing.

### HERO PRODUCT SLOT

Every scene carries one TCCC hero product, chosen by the brief — **this
file never defaults to a fixed "the classic red Coca-Cola can."** This is
the user's own explicit, deliberate design feature for this file (distinct
from the UK/Germany/Spain files' red-can default, which is out of scope
for this pass), preserved exactly as built and verified for internal
consistency this pass rather than redesigned.

**Template:**
> [HERO PRODUCT]: {brand and variant exactly as named on pack}, in
> {format and size}, {dominant pack colour and material cue},
> {negated lookalikes}. {Position: standing upright on the surface,
> label facing camera or turned slightly}. Pack text will be composited
> in post.

**Rules:**
- **Name the variant and flavour; negate the closest lookalike.**
  Testing showed an unspecified "Coca-Cola can" rendered as the wrong
  variant in 2 of 3 generations (`coca-cola-guidelines.md` §1). The lesson
  applies to every brand: Original vs. Zero Sugar vs. Light; Fanta's
  several flavours; Sparletta's several flavours.
- **Match format to register**, unless the brief overrides:
  - on the go, forecourt, street: a can or small PET
  - shisa nyama, spaza counter, township yard: a returnable glass bottle
    or PET
  - Sunday family lunch, braai with guests: a multi-serve PET in the
    midground, one filled glass per place setting
  - restaurant: a glass bottle or can, optionally poured into plain,
    unbranded glassware with ice
- **Use the chosen format's real dimensions as the scale anchor** (see
  SCALE REFERENCE — South Africa's local formats are now substantially
  clarified by this pass, see below).
- **One hero product per scene** unless the brief asks for several; don't
  let a second TCCC product appear by default.
- **Brand-to-dish fit is the brief's decision.** Notable local
  associations (e.g. ginger beer at braais) are context, not rules.

### SCALE REFERENCE — SOUTH AFRICA

**Hero-product anchors — substantially clarified this pass.** The
scaffold flagged South Africa's local can/bottle formats as entirely
unconfirmed, suspecting a 300 mL can might be a local standard. This pass
found real, current, dated confirmation:

- **The 300 mL can is real, current, and genuinely distinct from — not a
  replacement for — the 330 mL can.** South Africa's beverage industry
  introduced a 300 mL can in **September 2018** as a lower-sugar-conscious
  alternative sold alongside the existing 330 mL can at the same price;
  current South African retail and CCBA-operated listings (e.g. CCBA's own
  myccba.africa product catalogue, and multiple South African online
  beverage retailers) show **both 300 mL and 330 mL cans on sale today**,
  alongside a 440 mL can. [HIGH for both sizes being real and currently
  sold — corroborated by CCBA's own current product listings and multiple
  independent SA retail sources; MEDIUM for the September 2018
  introduction date and its "reduced-sugar" framing — corroborated by two
  independent SA business-press sources (bizcommunity.com, timeslive.co.za)
  though one is older reporting from 2017 discussing a related
  "shrinkflation" trend and should be read as background, not the primary
  date] [SOURCE: bizcommunity.com "Things go smaller with Coca-Cola";
  timeslive.co.za "Smaller soft drink sizes leave bitter taste of
  shrinkflation"; myccba.africa product listings for "Coke 300ML 01X24"]
  **Practical guidance: use 300 mL as the reasonable current default for a
  generic "a can of Coca-Cola" South African brief; use 330 mL only if the
  brief specifically calls for a "traditional/standard" can register** —
  both are correct and current, so this is a register choice, not a
  correction of one to the other.
  - **Note on a plausible but unconfirmed causal link**: the September 2018
    timing sits roughly five months after South Africa's Health Promotion
    Levy (a sugar-sweetened-beverage tax) took effect on 1 April 2018. This

### A. Braai and fire

#### Boerewors (and the boerie roll)
- **Category:** Everyday braai centrepiece; roll as a snack.
- **Lineage:** Afrikaner; coarse beef-and-pork sausage with coriander.

  then cut into lengths; **boerie roll**: a length in a soft hot-dog roll
  with tomato-onion relish or chutney.

  grill marks.
- **Confidence:** [MEDIUM] core dish, not independently re-checked this
  pass beyond well-established common knowledge.

#### Ostrich steak — new entry, added this pass (gap #3)
- **Category:** Everyday-to-occasion; a distinctively South African red
  meat, not a novelty item.
- **Lineage:** Farmed ostrich meat, centred on the Klein Karoo around

  described as the world's largest ostrich processor, producing roughly
  65% of internationally-traded ostrich product. [HIGH — corroborated by
  South Africa's own official tourism site, Oudtshoorn's own tourism
  board, and multiple independent industry sources] [SOURCE:
  southafrica.net "Oudtshoorn and surrounds... Ostrich Capital of the

  southafrica.co.za "Ostrich Meat, South Africa"]
- **Why it needs its own entry, not a beef-steak variant:** ostrich is a
  different animal and reads differently on camera — a dark, deep
  red-to-maroon, very lean, fine-grained meat, closer in colour to beef
  fillet but with almost no visible fat marbling or fat cap, commonly
  served as a fillet or steak in South African restaurants and endorsed by
  the Heart and Stroke Foundation for its low fat/cholesterol profile.
  [MEDIUM — corroborated by the same industry sources; visual/texture
  description is this pass's own reasonable rendering of the sourced facts
  about leanness and colour, not itself independently photographed or
  confirmed]
- **Texture & finish:** dark maroon-red, fine-grained, almost no visible
  fat marbling; a seared, evenly browned crust; a pink-to-red medium-rare
  centre when cut, notably leaner-looking than a beef steak of the same
  cut.
- **Scale:** a fillet portion ~2–3 cm thick, ~10–12 cm long — similar
  footprint to a beef fillet steak, on a 26–28 cm plate with sides
  covering roughly a third to half the plate. [LOW — this pass's own
  reasonable estimate by analogy to a beef fillet steak, not independently

- **Model failure:** a pale, poultry-like "ostrich" render (ostrich meat is

### B. Pap, steamed bread and traditional staples

#### Pap (stywe pap, krummelpap/phutu) + chakalaka / tomato-onion sous
- **Variants (§4.6), verified this pass:** stywe pap; krummelpap/phutu
  (with amasi in Zulu homes); pap en sous. See GENERAL NORMS above for the
  full HIGH-confidence sourcing on these three forms.
- **Texture:** stywe pap: smooth, dense, matte bright-white mound, faintly
  glossy, spoon- or hand-shaped ridges, steam. Krummelpap: dry grainy

  flecks, carrot shreds and pale beans.
- **Scale:** stiff pap portion roughly fist-sized, ~8–10 cm across,
  beside meat on a 26–28 cm plate or tray; chakalaka a spoonful or a
  12–15 cm side bowl.
- **Eating:** by hand or fork. [LOW-MEDIUM]
- **Model failure:** mashed potato; grits; polenta.

#### Pap tert (pap bake)
- **Form:** layered pap with tomato-onion sauce and melted cheese, baked;

#### Ujeqe / dombolo (steamed bread and dumplings)
- **Form:** ujeqe — a whole loaf steamed in a bowl or pot; dombolo —

- **Scale:** ujeqe loaf ~15–20 cm dome; dombolo ~8–10 cm each, several on

- **Model failure:** a baked, crusty loaf; a brioche.

#### Umngqusho (samp and beans)
- **Texture:** pale, chunky broken maize kernels and speckled sugar beans,

- **Scale:** a mound ~10–12 cm on a plate or in a 15–18 cm bowl.

#### Dikgobe
- **Form:** a Setswana/Sotho dish of samp or maize kernels with beans,

#### Ting

### D. Street and township

#### Kota (spatlo)
- **Form, verified this pass:** hollowed quarter loaf, originally filled
  with mince and mashed potato, evolved into today's standard fill of
  **slap chips, polony, atchar, and cheese**, commonly extended with a
  fried egg, a Russian sausage, or a beef patty; the removed bread on top
  or beside. [HIGH — multiple independent South African food-media sources
  (Wikipedia's Spatlo entry, Sandwich Tribunal, Daily News, MyKitchen)
  converge closely on both the historical mince/mash origin and the
  modern chips/polony/atchar/cheese standard] [SOURCE:
  en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatlo; sandwichtribunal.com "Spatlo or Kota:
  Stuffed Bread of South Africa"; livekindly.com "The South African Kota
  Sandwich Is a Story of Apartheid"]
- **Texture:** soft pale bread, thin golden crust, inner walls soaked
  orange-red; soft pale chips piled above the rim; glossy pink curled
  polony; oily chilli-red atchar; melting cheese.
- **Scale:** quarter loaf ~11 × 11 × 8 cm, overflowing; in paper or a
  cardboard tray.
- **Model failure:** a sub or burger; a bunny chow.

#### Bunny chow (KZN / Durban)
- **Variants (§4.6):** mutton, chicken, beans; quarter, half or full loaf.
- **Origin, verified this pass — genuinely disputed, disclose explicitly.**
  Created in Durban among the city's large Indian community, dated to
  around the 1940s, but its precise origin is contested across at least
  three separate accounts: (1) a Bania-caste restaurateur at Kapitan's
  Restaurant coined "Bania's Chow," later corrupted to "bunny chow" — a
  claim itself disputed by rival restaurant Patel's Vegetarian Refreshment
  Room; (2) apartheid-era Indian golf caddies, barred from carrying
  cutlery, had curry delivered in a hollowed loaf as an improvised,
  cutlery-free container; (3) a practical-solution account in which
  proprietors switched from a fragile roti wrapper to a hollowed bread
  loaf as a sturdier takeaway container. [HIGH for the origin genuinely
  being disputed across multiple named, credible competing accounts — this
  matches and slightly sharpens the scaffold's own already-correct
  disclosure] [SOURCE: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunny_chow; theculturetrip.com
  "The Mystery Of Durban's Signature Dish Bunny Chow"; food52.com "The
  Origins of Bunny Chow"]
- **Form:** hollowed loaf filled with curry, bread plug set on top or
  beside, carrot-chilli-onion sambal alongside; eaten by hand.
- **Texture:** loose, oily, deep red-orange gravy, red oil slick, whole
  spices, potato chunks, bone-in meat; bread walls soaked at the inside
  edges.
- **Scale:** quarter ~11 × 11 × 8 cm; half ~14–18 cm long; on a plate or
  paper.
- **Model failure:** sourdough bowl with creamy soup; yellow korma.

#### Trinchado (Portuguese-influenced) — new entry, added this pass (gap #1)
- **Category:** Everyday-to-occasion; a bar-snack/starter register as much
  as a main.
- **Lineage, verified this pass:** Portuguese-South African, brought by
  Portuguese settlers who migrated from Mozambique and Angola; the name
  derives from the Portuguese for "cut up" or "chopped." Notably, sources
  are explicit that **trinchado is not found in Portugal itself** — it is
  a genuinely South African-Portuguese invention, originally a
  restaurant/bar dish made from off-cut meat left over from the day's main
  service. [HIGH — multiple independent culinary-history sources converge
  closely, including the specific "not found in Portugal" claim]
  [SOURCE: leitesculinaria.com "Trinchado ~ Spicy South African Beef";
  finglobal.com "Trinchado – A Taste Sensation from South Africa"]
- **Form:** cubed beef (or, less commonly, chicken or chicken livers)
  braised in a spicy, garlicky red sauce built on chilli, paprika, bay
  leaf, and often red wine and olives; served in the sauce as a starter
  or bar snack, with bread for dipping, or with a heap of **batatas
  fritas** (fries) as a more substantial plate. [HIGH — corroborated by
  multiple independent recipe/culinary-history sources]
- **Texture & finish:** small, glossy, deep red-brown cubes of meat in a
  thick, glossy, chilli-red-brown sauce, flecked with garlic and coarse
  chilli, sometimes with visible whole or halved olives; served bubbling
  in a small cast-iron skillet or shallow bowl, with bread torn and
  dipped, or fries piled alongside.
- **Scale:** served in a 15–18 cm cast-iron skillet or bowl as a starter
  (shared, with bread on the side); as a fries-topped main, the sauce and
  meat cover roughly half of a 26–28 cm plate beside a mound of fries.
  [LOW — this pass's own reasonable estimate by analogy to the
  similarly-staged chicken-livers entry above and standard side-dish
  vessel sizing, not independently sourced]
- **Eating:** bread or a fork for dipping; not hand-eaten.
- **Model failure:** a plain beef stew or goulash (trinchado's sauce is
  distinctly glossier, redder, and chilli-forward, and the dish is
  typically served in a much smaller, more concentrated portion than a
  full stew); confusing it with a Spanish or generic "Portuguese" tapa —
  trinchado is specifically South African-Portuguese and not found on
  menus in Portugal itself.

#### "Russian and chips" — new entry, added this pass (gap #5)
- **Category:** Everyday takeaway; a standalone fish-and-chip-shop and
  spaza/corner-café order in its own right, not only a kota filling.
- **Lineage, verified this pass:** the "Russian" is a South African
  polony-style processed sausage with no actual Russian origin — one
  culinary-history source traces the naming, and notes the closest real
  international relative is the Slovenian kransky/German Krainer sausage,
  not anything Russian. It is firmer than polony or a vienna (which are
  pure fine-meat-paste products), described as an emulsified sausage with
  visible "showpiece" meat chunks in a fine pink-cured paste, typically
  filled in 22–28 mm collagen casing or 20–24 mm sheep casing. [HIGH —
  corroborated by an independent South African sausage-manufacturing trade
  source and Freddy Hirsch's own South African "how to make Russians"
  guide] [SOURCE: earthwormexpress.com "Origins of the South African
  Sausage, Called a Russian"; freddyhirsch.co.za "How to Make Russians"]
- **Form and serving, verified this pass:** the Russian is slit to keep it
  from bursting, then deep-fried alongside the chips, and served with salt
  and vinegar (and often tomato sauce) — this is described directly as a
  standalone, common order ("if you want to indulge in the great South
  African classic of a Russian and chips") at fish-and-chip shops,
  corner cafés, and spaza shops, distinct from its role as one kota
  filling among several. [HIGH — corroborated by multiple independent
  South African food-culture sources] [SOURCE:
  easysouthafricanrecipes.com "Easy Traditional Russian and Chips";
  biltongmakers.com "Russian and Chips Sausage"]
- **Texture & finish:** plump, taut, deep pink-to-red casing with a few
  shallow diagonal frying slits; a firmer, coarser bite than a smooth
  vienna or polony, with visible small chunks of coarser meat inside a
  fine pink emulsion; served whole or halved lengthwise on a bed of soft,
  pale, oily chips, with a scatter of salt and vinegar and often a stripe
  of tomato sauce.
- **Scale:** sausage ~15–18 cm long, ~2.5–3 cm diameter (comparable to a
  large frankfurter, but plumper); served on a ~20–25 cm paper wrap or
  polystyrene tray with the chips, the same footprint as the existing
  slap-chips entry above. [LOW — this pass's own reasonable estimate by
  analogy to a standard large frankfurter and the existing slap-chips
  vessel sizing, not independently sourced]
- **Model failure:** a plain hot-dog frankfurter (too smooth/uniform, no
  visible meat-chunk texture, and usually the wrong colour — a Russian's
  casing reads distinctly pink-red rather than a hot dog's tan-brown);
  confusing it with polony sliced into rounds (the Russian is a whole
  sausage, fried and slit, not sliced cold).

#### West Coast mussels — new entry, added this pass (gap #6)
- **Category:** Occasion/coastal specialty; a genuine Western Cape dish,
  not just a restaurant novelty.
- **Lineage, verified this pass:** South Africa's mussel farming is
  concentrated entirely in **Saldanha Bay** on the Western Cape's west
  coast, where the cold, nutrient-rich Benguela current upwelling makes
  the bay unusually productive; the country's black-mussel harvest runs
  roughly 2,000–2,500 tonnes a year across a handful of farms. [HIGH —
  corroborated by an academic food-security journal article, an industry
  trade publication, and South Africa's own tourism/industry sites]
  [SOURCE: farmersweekly.co.za "Mussel farming on the West Coast";
  Springer Nature's "Mussel and oyster culture in Saldanha Bay, South
  Africa"]
- **Form:** most commonly served as a **mussel pot** — mussels steamed
  open in a pot with white wine, garlic, onion, and sometimes cream, then
  served in the pot or a large bowl with the cooking liquid, alongside
  crusty bread for dipping. [MEDIUM — corroborated by multiple current
  South African recipe sources converging on the same white-wine/garlic
  base and bread-for-dipping serving style, though this is a recipe-level
  consensus rather than a single canonical "the" West Coast preparation]
  [SOURCE: taste.co.za "West Coast Mussel Pot"; theinsidersa.co.za "West
  Coast Creamy Mussel Pot"]
- **Texture & finish:** dark blue-black shells, opened to reveal glossy
  orange-to-cream flesh, pooled in a pale, glossy, herb-flecked wine-cream
  broth; a scatter of chopped parsley or thyme; torn crusty bread beside
  the pot, some pieces dipped and soaked.

#### Bobotie

#### Bredie (tomato; waterblommetjie)

#### Denningvleis — new entry, added this pass (gap #2)
- **Category:** Occasion/Sunday-table dish; one of Cape Malay cuisine's
  oldest recorded recipes.
- **Lineage, verified this pass:** the name traces to the Javanese word
  **dendeng** (originally water-buffalo meat), combined with the Afrikaans
  **vleis** (meat) — a linguistic fusion reflecting the dish's actual
  history: brought to the Cape from Java along with a supply of tamarind,
  with the water-buffalo meat substituted locally for mutton or lamb.
  Cape Malay spicing for this dish draws on turmeric, star anise, fennel,
  coriander, nutmeg, and tamarind. [HIGH — multiple independent
  culinary-history sources converge closely on both the Javanese
  etymology and the tamarind-and-spice profile] [SOURCE:
  greedygourmet.com "Denningvleis: BEST South African Lamb Stew";
  foodwinegarden.com "Tamarind Lamb Stew or Denningvleis"]
- **Form:** a braised lamb or mutton stew in a sweet-and-sour sauce built
  on tamarind, vinegar, and sugar, with the characteristic Cape Malay
  spice blend above. [HIGH]
- **Texture & finish:** bone-in or cubed lamb, deep glossy brown-amber
  sauce (darker and more amber than the tomato-red of a bredie, and
  glossier/thinner than a Durban curry's oil-slicked red), a visible sheen
  from the tamarind-vinegar reduction, flecked with whole spices; usually
  served over plain or yellow rice.
- **Scale:** over rice, filling roughly two-thirds of a 26–28 cm plate —
  the same vessel-fill convention as the neighbouring bredie entry. [LOW —
  this pass's own reasonable estimate by analogy to the bredie and curry
  entries above, not independently sourced]
- **Model failure:** a plain brown beef stew (denningvleis's sauce reads
  distinctly glossier and more amber-toned, with a sweet-sour tamarind
  character rather than a savoury brown-gravy one); a bredie (denningvleis
  is sweet-sour and tamarind-based, not tomato- or waterblommetjie-based).

#### Breyani (Cape Malay and Durban — §4.6)
- **Variants, verified this pass:** Cape Malay breyani (includes lentils —
  a genuine point of difference from Indian biryani, milder, layered with
  potato and egg) vs. Durban breyani (spicier, redder). Sources are
  explicit that lentils and potatoes are a South African/Cape Malay
  addition **not** found in traditional Indian biryani, and that the dish
  is assembled by layering rice and lentils with the spiced meat in a
  single pot (a genuine construction difference from biryani's own
  layering conventions). [HIGH — multiple independent South African
  culinary-culture sources converge closely] [SOURCE:
  capecolouredculture.co.za "4 Tasty Differences Between Breyani And
  Akni"; quintorecipes.com "What is the Difference Between Biryani and
  Breyani?"]
- **Texture:** layered rice streaked yellow-orange and white, whole spices,
  meat pieces, fried onion, boiled egg halves, visible lentils.
- **Scale:** served from a 30 cm pot; a mound filling most of a 26–28 cm
  plate, with a small bowl of sambal or dhal.

#### Akni
- A spiced rice-and-meat dish lighter in colour than breyani, cooked in one
  pot, and distinguished from breyani specifically by the *absence* of
  lentils (per the breyani-vs-akni sourcing above). **Scale:** as breyani.
  [LOW-MEDIUM]

#### Cape Malay roti and salomie
- **Form:** soft, layered, flaky flatbread; the **salomie** is a roti
  rolled around curry as a wrap. [LOW-MEDIUM]
- **Texture:** golden blistered patches, flaky visible layers, a buttery
  sheen; curry glossy at the open end.
- **Scale:** roti ~20–25 cm diameter; salomie ~20 cm long × ~6–7 cm thick.
- **Model failure:** a Mexican tortilla wrap (thin, pale, no layers).

#### Koeksisters vs. koesisters (§4.6 — two different sweets) — cross-reference note added this pass (gap #7)
- **Afrikaner koeksister, verified this pass:** a tight, braided/plaited
  dough, deep-fried, then dunked in ice-cold syrup — glossy, chewy,
  intensely sweet, crisp-shelled, usually served chilled. **Scale:**
  ~8–10 cm long. [HIGH — multiple independent South African food-media
  sources converge closely on the plait shape and cold-syrup-dunk method]
  [SOURCE: iol.co.za "What is the difference between koeksisters and
  koesisters?"; foodandhome.co.za "The difference between koesisters and
  koeksisters"]
  **Cross-reference note, per gap #7:** this entry sits in section F
  ("Cape Malay, Western Cape") for direct visual comparison with the
  similarly-named Cape Malay koesister immediately below, but **the
  Afrikaner koeksister is a nationwide Afrikaner tradition** — genuinely at
  home in the Free State, Gauteng, and everywhere else Afrikaner food
  culture is present, not a Western-Cape-specific dish the way the Cape
  Malay koesister actually is. A Highveld, Free State, or other non-Western-
  Cape brief calling for a koeksister should **not** be staged with
  Western Cape environment/settlement cues just because this entry is
  catalogued here — see the matching notes in the Gauteng & Highveld and
  Interior & North ZONE CALLOUTS above.
- **Cape Malay koesister, verified this pass:** an oval-shaped, spiced
  dough (cinnamon, cardamom, aniseed, sometimes naartjie/tangerine peel),
  deep-fried, dipped in hot syrup, then rolled in desiccated coconut —
  soft, spiced, cake-like, distinctly different in shape, spicing, and
  finish from the Afrikaner koeksister. **Scale:** ~8–10 cm. [HIGH — same
  sourcing as above] **This is genuinely Western-Cape/Cape-Malay-specific**
  and correctly placed in this section.
- **Model failure (both):** confusing the two — the plaited, glassy,
  syrup-soaked Afrikaner koeksister and the oval, coconut-rolled, spiced
  Cape Malay koesister are visually and texturally distinct, not regional
  spelling variants of the same sweet.
