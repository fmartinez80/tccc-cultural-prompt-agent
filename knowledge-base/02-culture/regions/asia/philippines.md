# Philippines

## FILE ROLE & METHOD

This file is the Philippines' country file: a single national staging
brief with seven labeled internal zones. One TCCC hero beverage per scene
(named by the brief — see HERO PRODUCT SLOT), staged against real
Filipino dishes, vessels and settings, with the full drinks landscape —
calamansi juice, sago't gulaman, buko juice, kapeng barako, tsokolate,
and alcohol — documented as context even where a drink is never itself
staged.

**Scope.** Lunch, dinner, merienda (the mid-morning and mid-afternoon
snack meals) and street food are in scope (project default). **The silog
plates (tapsilog, longsilog, tocilog) are in scope** because they are
eaten at any hour — at 24-hour silogan stalls, as a carinderia lunch, as
a late-night meal — not only at breakfast. **Morning-only items
(pandesal, champorado, taho) sit in an OPTIONAL MORNING MODULE, off by
default**, the same pattern as `india.md` and `brazil.md`. Both are scope
calls **flagged for Fernando's confirmation** (§1.2; log in
`DECISIONS.md`). No beverage other than the hero TCCC product is staged;
the others are documented in ICONIC BEVERAGES.

### Hard staging rules specific to the Philippines (read before any scene)

1. **Rice is on the table at every meal.** A Filipino lunch or dinner
   without a mound of plain white rice reads as a snack, not a meal.
   Every ulam (main dish) scene carries steamed white rice — on each
   plate, or in a bowl or a rice pot at the centre — unless the dish is
   itself a noodle, bread or merienda item. [HIGH that rice is the
   everyday staple — uncontested; MEDIUM for the "every meal" norm, not
   independently re-checked this pass beyond meal-pattern sources]
2. **Spoon and fork, not knife and fork.** The Filipino place setting is
   a **spoon in the right hand and a fork in the left**; the fork pushes
   food onto the spoon, the spoon cuts soft meat and carries rice to the
   mouth. **No table knife at a home or carinderia table.** Hand eating
   (**kamayan**) is real — at a boodle fight, a seaside grill, in the
   provinces — but **nothing is held in a hand in frame**: show kamayan
   by implication (banana leaves, no cutlery, rice pressed into small
   mounds). [MEDIUM — etiquette sources (Etiquette Scholar,
   factsanddetails) via search; uncontested]
3. **Pork is everywhere in Christian Filipino food — and absent from
   Muslim tables.** Lechon, adobo, sisig, liempo, longganisa and bagnet
   are national favourites; but the **Muslim minority (6.4% of the
   population, 2020 census; Roman Catholic 78.8%)** [HIGH — PSA 2020
   Census of Population and Housing] lives mainly in the **Bangsamoro
   region (BARMM) and western Mindanao — Maguindanao, Lanao, Sulu,
   Basilan, Tawi-Tawi** — where food is **halal and pork-free**. Rules:
   - **A zone 7 (Bangsamoro / Muslim Mindanao) scene carries no pork
     and no alcohol at all** — not in the background, not as "a meat".
     Stage beef, chicken, goat or fish dishes (satti, pastil, tiyula
     itum, beef rendang, piaparan). [EDITORIAL rule built on the HIGH
     census figure]
   - **Never put a pork dish beside a Muslim-coded setting** (a
     headscarfed family, a mosque in the background, a halal carinderia).
     [EDITORIAL]
   - **When a brief names no zone or community, pork is not a problem**
     in zones 1–6 — but prefer chicken or fish as the hero ulam when a
     brief is national and unspecified, so the scene is acceptable to
     every viewer. [EDITORIAL fallback, same logic as `india.md`'s veg
     fallback, much weaker here]
4. **No alcohol staged — and the Philippines has a strong drinking
   culture, including TCCC-branded RTDs.** Beer (San Miguel Pale
   Pilsen, Red Horse), gin (Ginebra San Miguel), rum (Tanduay), brandy,
   tuba and lambanog are real and everyday; the **inuman / tagay**
   (drinking session with pulutan bar snacks) is a fixture of evenings
   and fiestas. **Rum-and-Coke is a real mixed-drink habit**, and
   **ready-to-drink "Jack Daniel's & Coca-Cola" and "Absolut & Sprite"
   cans have been sold in the Philippines** (2023 and 2024 launches)
   [MEDIUM — Manila Millennial, Inquirer, Ilonggo Tech Blog via search
   titles]. **Never stage a TCCC product as a mixer with alcohol (non-alcoholic TCCC-brand mixes are allowed, schema §5.5), never beside a beer
   bottle or a gin bottle, never at an inuman, and never stage a
   pulutan spread (sisig, chicharon, kropek on a sizzling plate at
   night) in a way that reads as a drinking session.**
5. **Holy Week and religious imagery.** No TCCC product on or beside a
   Santo Niño, a home altar, a crucifix, a Simbang Gabi church scene, a
   procession, or a mosque. On **Good Friday** much of the country keeps
   a meatless, sober day; **don't stage a lechon or festive feast on
   Holy Thursday or Good Friday** — stage fish or vegetables if a Holy
   Week scene is briefed at all. [EDITORIAL, same stance as `spain.md`'s
   Semana Santa]
6. **Other drinks are the intruders**: a pitcher of iced water or
   sago't gulaman, a glass of calamansi juice, a buko (young coconut)
   with a straw, a mug of 3-in-1 coffee, a bottle of beer. Exclude them
   by name.
7. **Competitor and fast-food branding**: Filipino-style spaghetti and
   fried chicken are national dishes partly shaped by fast-food chains
   (see catalog). **Never show any chain's branding, mascot, bucket,
   clamshell box, paper cup, tray liner or uniform colour scheme** —
   stage the dishes on plain home or carinderia plates.
8. **General project rules**: no legible text anywhere (shop signs,
   jeepney paintwork and route boards, tarpaulin banners, sari-sari
   store price lists and hanging sachet strips — all heavy priors);
   nothing held in a hand; no drinks other than the hero; never a TCCC
   product as a mixer.

### Structural decision: one file, seven zones (recommendation — reviewer has final say)

The `country-file-schema.md` §1.1 swap test, applied with this pass's
evidence:

- **A strong national core travels.** Adobo, sinigang, pancit, lumpia,
  fried chicken, Filipino spaghetti, silog plates, lechon (as a
  category), leche flan and halo-halo read correctly from Luzon to
  Mindanao's Christian cities; a single national carinderia repertoire
  exists. This argues for one file, unlike India.
- **Some dishes are form-changing** (§4.2): **lechon** — Cebu's
  herb-stuffed, sauce-free pig vs. Luzon/Manila's plainer pig served
  with a brown liver sauce [HIGH — Suroy.ph, Primer, Business Diary
  PH agree]; **empanada** (Vigan's orange rice-flour shell vs. the
  baked Spanish-style pastry); **batchoy** (La Paz, Iloilo); **inasal**
  (Bacolod's annatto-basted chicken vs. a generic grilled chicken).
- **Religion of the table changes in Muslim Mindanao** (hard rule 3):
  halal, no pork, no alcohol, Tausug and Maguindanaon dishes with
  burnt-coconut and turmeric bases, and a different visual register
  (okir carvings, malong textiles, mosques).
- **Regional signatures differ visibly**: coconut-milk-and-chilli Bicol
  (laing, Bicol express — green and cream-coloured), Ilocos' bagnet,
  pinakbet and Vigan longganisa (garlicky, small, sour), Pampanga's
  sisig and kare-kare, Iloilo's batchoy and Bacolod's inasal.
- **Environment varies but less than in India**: a Metro Manila condo or
  townhouse, a provincial bahay na bato (stone-and-wood colonial house)
  in Vigan, a concrete single house with a galvanised-iron roof (the
  national baseline), a nipa-and-bamboo bahay kubo by a rice field, a
  Cebu or Davao suburb.

**Recommendation: one national file with seven internal zones** (the
Mexico/Spain/Türkiye pattern, not India's split). **Zone 7 (Bangsamoro /
Muslim Mindanao) is a spinout candidate** if a brief targets it — a
different food system with different hard rules. **A recommendation for
the reviewer, not a decision** (§7).

Zamboanga City is majority Christian and Chavacano-speaking but eats
satti like its Tausug neighbours, and many Muslim Filipinos live in
Manila (Quiapo). [EDITORIAL]

### Default when no zone is named

Fall back to **zone 1 (Metro Manila) at the everyday register**: a
townhouse or condo dining table with rice, a pork-or-chicken adobo or
sinigang, a vegetable side, spoon and fork; or a neighbourhood
carinderia/turo-turo. Manila is the capital and its Tagalog home
repertoire is the one served across the country's cities. [EDITORIAL

## METHOD NOTE (read first)

**Build method, this pass.** Drafted directly from model knowledge and
checked with ~40 WebSearch queries (the shared budget set for this file),
prioritising the claims a scene visibly depends on: pack formats and can
size, bottler, religion statistics, housing, meal times, lechon forms,
dish sizes, and 2026–2027 festival dates. Tags mean:

- **[HIGH] / [MEDIUM] / [LOW]** — schema §6 tags earned this pass (HIGH =
  2+ independent corroborating sources; MEDIUM = 1 credible source).
- **[… — not independently re-checked this pass]** — model knowledge that
  is uncontested general culinary or cultural knowledge but was not
  individually searched. Plausible, not verified.
- **[EDITORIAL]** — a judgment call (defaults, portion counts, surface
  shares, caricature-avoidance guidance), never a factual claim.
- **[HIGH — first-party test]** — this project's own image-generation
  findings (`coca-cola-guidelines.md` §1, `country-file-schema.md` §7.5).
  **No Philippines-specific image tests have been run.**
- **"(via search)"** — the source page itself was blocked by the egress
  proxy; the claim rests on the search-result snippet.

**Writing principle.** Describe what the camera sees: the lacquered,
amber crackle of lechon skin, the dark soy gloss of adobo, the cloudy
tamarind broth of sinigang with a green kangkong tangle, the orange
peanut sauce of kare-kare, the sizzle and char of sisig, and real-world
size.

**File-wide rules for every scene built from this file:**

1. **Text as atmosphere.** Shop signs, carinderia menu boards,
   sari-sari price lists, hanging sachet strips, tarpaulins and jeepney
   paintwork appear only as heavily blurred, unreadable colour. Any
   readable word, number or brand mark means reject or retouch. Blur
   instructions are known to fail [HIGH — first-party test;
   `country-file-schema.md` §7.5]. Filipino street scenes carry a heavy
   signage prior — prefer tight framing.
2. **The hero product is a TCCC beverage named by the brief** (HERO
   PRODUCT SLOT). Branding is composited in post
   (`coca-cola-guidelines.md` §1–2).
3. **No alcohol in any scene**, never a TCCC product as a mixer with alcohol (non-alcoholic TCCC-brand mixes are allowed, schema §5.5).
4. **No drinks in frame other than the hero product** unless the brief
   explicitly allows a named non-alcoholic companion. Name the likely
   intruders in the negative: water pitchers and glasses, sago't
   gulaman, calamansi juice, buko with straw, coffee mugs, beer bottles.
5. **Nothing held in a hand.** Fishballs on a stick, banana cue, isaw,
   lumpia and siopao rest on a plate, a paper or banana-leaf liner, or a
   counter (`country-file-schema.md` §7.5).
6. **Rice, spoon-and-fork and the halal rule** (hard rules 1–3) apply to
   every scene.

## QUICK-REFERENCE: GENERIC SCENE REGISTERS

| Generic scene type | What to draw on |
|---|---|
| **Home lunch/dinner (urban)** | A rectangular or round dining table (wood or glass top, often under a clear plastic cover or a lace/plastic tablecloth), a rice pot or bowl of white rice at the centre, 2–3 ulam in ceramic or melamine serving bowls, each place a flat ~25 cm plate with spoon right and fork left. |
| **Carinderia / turo-turo** | Small eatery with a glass-fronted counter of 8–15 pre-cooked ulam in stainless-steel trays or aluminium pots ("turo-turo" = point-point), diners at monobloc plastic chairs and long tables with oilcloth, plain plates of rice plus one or two ulam. |
| **Sari-sari store** | A tiny neighbourhood shop run from the front of a house: a wooden or iron-grille window counter, shelves of small "sakto/tingi" packs, a glass jar row of candies and biscuits, a chest cooler of returnable glass bottles; very heavy text prior — frame tight. |
| **Boodle fight / kamayan** | A long table covered end to end in banana leaves, white rice laid in a continuous ridge down the centre, grilled and fried ulam (liempo, fish, shrimp, adobo, salted egg and tomato) along both sides, no plates or cutlery. |
| **Fiesta / handaan** | A buffet or long family table: a whole lechon on a banana-leaf-lined tray, pancit in a big bilao (woven round tray), lumpia shanghai, spaghetti, kare-kare, leche flan and fruit salad; paper or plastic bunting softly in the background. |
| **Mall food court / fast-casual** | Bright, clean, plastic trays and paper liners — **always unbranded**; rice plates, fried chicken, spaghetti, burger steak; avoid any chain's colours. |
| **Seaside grill / ihaw-ihaw** | Charcoal grill (ihawan) with skewers and whole fish, a nipa-shaded table, plates on banana leaf, a beach or fishing boats behind; daylight only (night grills read as inuman). |

## VENUE PROFILES

Schema §5.9 applies. The default camera keeps the table sharp and the
room soft, so each profile leads with what the blurred background must
show. First wave (2026-10-01): the five most-used staging venues. The
QUICK-REFERENCE table above stays the short index; the sari-sari store,
boodle fight, mall food court and seaside grill keep their register rows
until a later wave. Hard rules 1–8 apply to every profile; in Filipino
backgrounds the two traps that matter most are **religious objects**
(the Last Supper picture, a Santo Niño, a home altar: hard rule 5) and
**drinking cues** (beer and gin bottles, an inuman table: hard rule 4),
because both are common in exactly these rooms.

#### Venue: Concrete-house dining area (kainan / silid-kainan)
- Use for: home indoor; casual lunch at home (1–3), dinner at home,
  Noche Buena and Media Noche, the sala-side PBA and boxing watch
  parties; 1, 2 or a small group. The national default: 87.6% of
  occupied housing units are single houses, 60.4% with concrete, brick
  or stone outer walls and 85.9% with metal roofs [HIGH — PSA 2020 CPH,
  see ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES]. The dining area is usually a corner
  of one open living-dining room, not a separate room [EDITORIAL].
- Soft background (the core): **back wall**: smooth painted plaster in
  a light colour (cream, off-white, pale yellow, mint or peach), with a
  **framed picture or wooden plaque of the Last Supper** above the table
  as the classic fixture, framed graduation portraits and a wall clock
  [MEDIUM — homify.ph "15 most common things inside a Filipino house";
  Subli blog; file interior markers]. **The Last Supper and any Santo
  Niño or altar shelf must be out of frame or melted into an
  unreadable warm rectangle** (hard rule 5); framed photos stay as pale
  blurred rectangles with no faces readable. **Middle distance**: the
  kitchen counter or a doorway to the kitchen, with a **rice cooker**, a
  dish rack of plates and a gas range with a small LPG tank; a
  refrigerator as a tall pale block, often with magnets; a wooden or
  glass display cabinet (platera) of "for guests" plates and glasses;
  the sala's sofa set and TV cabinet at one side; plastic or rattan
  storage at the edges [MEDIUM — homify.ph; file interior markers;
  platera LOW — not verified]. **Light**: daylight through **jalousie
  windows** (horizontal glass louvres) or sliding aluminium windows with
  **decorative iron grilles**, which throw thin striped light; at night
  a white fluorescent tube or LED ceiling light (cool white), sometimes
  a small warm pendant over the table; a **stand fan** or ceiling fan as
  a soft disc [MEDIUM — Real Living "parts of a Filipino house"
  (jalousies); file markers]. **Palette**: light plaster, white or beige
  glossy floor tiles, dark varnished wood or glass-top table, the
  colour of the plastic or lace tablecloth, silver grilles. **Signature
  shapes (pick 3–4)**: the horizontal slats of jalousie glass; the
  scroll or geometric pattern of window grilles; the stand-fan disc; the
  rice cooker; the long wall rectangle of the (blurred) Last Supper.
  **Density and wear**: lived-in, family-sized, a little full (a
  calendar, a plastic flower arrangement, folded laundry on a chair);
  clean, never a slum or a showroom. **People cues**: one blurred
  relative at the kitchen or on the sofa, within the limit.
- Shell: a one- or two-storey concrete house, often in a subdivision or
  a dense barangay street; ceramic tile floor; plywood or gypsum ceiling
  about 2.6–2.8 m with a ceiling light; a front door with a steel
  screen or grille; a metal roof that is not seen indoors [HIGH for
  materials — PSA; EDITORIAL for heights].
- The table as set here: a rectangular wooden table (narra-look
  varnish) or a glass-topped table, often with a **clear plastic cover
  over a lace or printed cloth**, or a printed vinyl cloth; monobloc or
  wooden chairs; always on it: **rice** in a pot, bowl or on each plate
  (hard rule 1), 2–3 ulam in ceramic or melamine bowls with serving
  spoons, saucers of **sawsawan** (soy-calamansi, vinegar with chilli,
  patis), spoon right and fork left on a flat plate, a paper-napkin
  holder; a plastic food-cover dome (taklob) at the edge when food waits
  [HIGH for spoon-fork and rice — hard rules; MEDIUM for plastic table
  cover — file register; napkin holder and food cover LOW — not
  verified]. House tableware: white Corelle-type or floral melamine
  plates, steel spoons and forks.
- Subregional variants and the national default: **Metro Manila
  condo** (young professionals, couples; only 0.7% nationally): a
  studio or one-bedroom, a two-seat table against a wall, a window
  onto towers, laminate or tile floor. **Metro Manila townhouse**: a
  narrow row house in a subdivision, the same interior on a smaller
  footprint, a carport in front. **Provinces (Visayas, Mindanao, rural
  Luzon)**: a wider house, half-concrete half-wood or wooden upper
  floor, capiz or sliding wooden windows in older houses, banana and
  coconut trees through the window. **Zone 7 (Bangsamoro)**: no
  Christian imagery; framed Arabic calligraphy possible (unreadable or
  out of frame); okir-pattern textile or carving as a colour accent
  [LOW — not verified]. National default when nothing is named: the
  Metro Manila concrete house above.
- Hallucination traps: a nipa hut or bamboo stilt house as the default
  home; a Spanish-colonial bahay na bato with capiz windows and narra
  floors (heritage only: Vigan, Taal, Iloilo); a Japanese or Korean
  apartment; a Thai or Balinese resort interior; chopsticks; a Western
  suburban kitchen island; a poverty-coded informal-settlement room.
- Never stage: the Last Supper, a crucifix, a Santo Niño or an altar
  in readable form; a beer bottle, gin bottle or tagay glass anywhere;
  a fast-food chain's bucket, box or paper bag (hard rule 7); legible
  calendars, labels or TV screens.
- Prompt-ready line: "A Filipino family table in a concrete house:
  a glass-topped table with a clear plastic cover over a lace cloth,
  cream plaster walls, a softly blurred kitchen counter with a rice
  cooker behind, and daylight through jalousie glass louvres and a
  patterned iron window grille."
- Confidence and sources: MEDIUM-HIGH. 1 search (Filipino dining-room
  interiors). PSA 2020 CPH; homify.ph; Subli blog; Real Living "46

### HERO PRODUCT SLOT

Every scene carries one TCCC hero product, **named by the brief**. This
file uses `south-africa.md`'s generalised slot; since 2026-09-27 that is
the project-wide rule (the brief dictates the SKU, never the region — see
`DECISIONS.md` and `country-file-schema.md` §5.4). **If a brief names no
product, ask for one.** Nothing below is a default.

**Template:**
> [HERO PRODUCT]: {brand and variant exactly as named on pack}, in
> {format and size}, {dominant pack colour and material cue},
> {negated lookalikes}. {Position: standing upright on the surface,
> label facing camera or turned slightly}. Pack text will be composited
> in post.

**Rules:**
- **Name the variant; negate the closest lookalike** (Coca-Cola Original
  vs. Zero Sugar vs. Coke Light — the Philippines sells **Coke Light**,
  the local Diet Coke equivalent, in 320 mL cans [MEDIUM — foodpanda.ph
  listing]; Royal Tru-Orange vs. a generic orange soda; Sprite vs. 7UP).
  An unspecified "Coca-Cola can" rendered as the wrong variant in 2 of 3
  generations [HIGH — first-party test, `coca-cola-guidelines.md` §1].
- **Also negate the local competitors.** **Pepsi** and **Mountain Dew**
  (Pepsi-Cola Products Philippines), **RC Cola** (ARC Refreshments) and
  **7UP**; note that **Sarsi and Royal are TCCC brands, not competitors**
  (see ICONIC BEVERAGES). One market brief put Coca-Cola's bottler at ~40% share,
  Pepsi ~19%, Asia Brewery ~10%, ARC Refreshments and Universal Robina
  ~4% each [MEDIUM — USDA FAS GAIN "Non-Alcoholic Beverages Market
  Brief" 2022 (via search); share figures are dated]. Always state "not
  any other cola brand".
- **The Philippines is a 320 mL-can market — not 330 mL, not 355 mL.**
  Philippine retail lists **Coca-Cola Original, Coke Zero, Coke Light,
  Sprite and Sprite Zero in 320 mL cans** (foodpanda.ph ₱39, Lazada
  24 × 320 mL cases, ShopSuki, Ever, AAA Shopping Center, Boozeone)
  [HIGH — many independent retail listings agree]. A **330 mL** Coca-Cola
  can also appears on one B2B listing (Shoppable) and Coke Zero Sugar
  330 mL in a search summary [LOW — likely older or imported stock]. A
  collector listing describes the 2023 Coke Zero can as **"Tall
  320ml"** [LOW]. **This contradicts `coca-cola-guidelines.md` §4.3's
  "default to 330 mL for any non-US market"** — the fourth market (after
  Mexico 355, Brazil 350 and India 300) to break it. Logged in the GAP
  LOG; not edited here.
- **The 320 mL can's silhouette is NOT confirmed.** Neighbouring Vietnam
  sells Coca-Cola as a **"Sleek Can 320ml"** (3sach.vn, Annam Gourmet
  listings) [MEDIUM for Vietnam], and the "tall" collector description
  points the same way, but **no source confirmed whether the Philippine
  320 mL can is a sleek can (~57 mm body) or a standard-diameter (~66 mm)
  can with a shorter body**. Arithmetic, not a source: a 57 mm sleek can
  holding 320 mL stands roughly **14–14.5 cm** tall; a 66 mm can holding
  320 mL stands roughly **11–11.5 cm**. **Working anchor for this file's
  prompt lines: "the can", treated as a tall slim can ~14 cm tall and

  - **Cans**: **320 mL** (standard); 330 mL (LOW, single listings).

  - **Coke Mismo** — the small single-serve **PET** bottle, launched

### ICONIC BEVERAGES (documented context — staging rules follow)

This section records the real Filipino drinks landscape, including
alcohol, and restricts only what is staged — the same design
`south-africa.md` uses.

**TCCC Philippines' local brands — the brands a brief may name.**

- **Coca-Cola** has been bottled in the Philippines since the early
  20th century; **San Miguel Corporation** ran Coca-Cola bottling for
  decades and in 1981 spun it off into **Coca-Cola Bottlers
  Philippines, Inc.** (a San Miguel 70% / TCCC 30% joint venture);
  Coca-Cola FEMSA bought 51% in 2012; TCCC later took it back, and in
  2024 sold it to CCEP and Aboitiz (see front matter) [HIGH — Wikipedia
  "Coca-Cola Beverages Philippines", Rappler, GMA News].
- **Royal Tru-Orange** (and the Royal flavour range) — a carbonated
  fruit soda **owned by TCCC and exclusive to the Philippines**,
  acquired from San Miguel in 2007; it works as the Philippine
  counterpart of Fanta [MEDIUM-HIGH — Wikipedia "Royal Tru"]. Bright
  orange; a fiesta and children's-party register.
- **Sarsi** — sarsaparilla soda, originally Cosmos Bottling's, now sold
  by Coca-Cola Philippines [MEDIUM — Wikipedia "Cosmos Bottling
  Corporation" via search]. Dark brown, a nostalgic provincial
  register.
- **Also TCCC Philippines**: Coca-Cola Original, Coke Zero, Coke Light,
  Sprite, Wilkins (water), Minute Maid, Powerade [MEDIUM — Wikipedia via
  search; full current list not checked].

**Competitors to negate**: Pepsi, Mountain Dew, 7UP, Mirinda (Pepsi-Cola
Products Philippines); **RC Cola** (ARC Refreshments); Asia Brewery and
Universal Robina (C2 tea) drinks [MEDIUM — USDA FAS GAIN 2022 (via
search)].

**Non-alcoholic context (never beside the hero; staged only if a brief
allows a named companion):**

- **Water** — a plastic pitcher and glasses at most home tables, often
  iced. **The single most likely intruder. Negate it.** [EDITORIAL]
- **Sago't gulaman** — brown-sugar syrup drink with tapioca pearls and
  agar cubes, sold from big plastic jugs at street stalls [MEDIUM — not
  independently re-checked this pass].
- **Calamansi juice** (calamansi = small green citrus), **buko juice**
  (young coconut water, often in the shell with a straw), **mango and
  pineapple juice**, **iced tea** (powdered, sweet — a carinderia
  "bottomless" drink).
- **Coffee**: **3-in-1 instant coffee sachets** dominate home and
  office; **kapeng barako** (strong Liberica coffee, Batangas) is the
  heritage cup. **Tsokolate** (thick hot chocolate from tablea cacao
  tablets, whisked with a wooden batirol) and **salabat** (ginger tea)
  appear at Christmas and Simbang Gabi. [MEDIUM — not independently
  re-checked this pass]
- **Halo-halo** is a dessert, not a drink (see catalog), but it is a
  tall glass of colour and ice — **keep it out of the hero's frame** or
  stage it as the food item with the hero beside it, never as a second
  "drink".

**Alcohol context (never staged):**

- **Beer**: San Miguel Pale Pilsen, San Mig Light and **Red Horse**
  (strong beer, 1 L "mucho" bottles at inuman). **Gin**: Ginebra San
  Miguel ("gin pomelo" mixed with powdered juice). **Rum**: Tanduay;
  **rum-and-Coke is a real habit**. **Brandy**: Emperador. **Native
  drinks**: **tuba** (coconut toddy, Visayas), **lambanog** (coconut
  spirit, Quezon), **basi** (sugar-cane wine, Ilocos). [MEDIUM — not
  independently re-checked this pass; uncontested]
- **TCCC-branded RTDs sold in the Philippines**: Jack Daniel's & Coca-Cola
  (2023) and Absolut & Sprite (2024) cans [MEDIUM — Manila Millennial,
  Inquirer, Ilonggo Tech Blog via search]. **Never stage them, and never
  let a 320 mL can scene read as one** — the RTD cans share the can
  format.
- **Inuman / tagay culture**: friends drinking around a table or on a
  sari-sari store bench in the evening, with **pulutan** (sisig,
  chicharon, kropek, adobong mani, isaw). **Never stage a TCCC product
  in a pulutan-at-night composition** (hard rule 4).

**Table norms:**
- **Rice in the centre**: a rice cooker pot, a big bowl, or each plate
  pre-served with a mound; second helpings expected ("unli-rice" —
  unlimited rice — is a selling point at chicken-inasal restaurants).
  A carinderia "cup" of rice is about **200 g cooked**, served as a
  domed mound [MEDIUM — Pinoy Negosyo / carinderia sources via search].
  [HIGH for the rice-centred meal]
- **Ulam family-style**: two or three shared dishes in serving bowls;
  each diner takes a little at a time onto their rice. [MEDIUM — not
  independently re-checked]
- **Spoon (right) and fork (left), no knife** (hard rule 2). Chopsticks
  are not Filipino table cutlery except at Chinese-Filipino restaurants.
- **Sawsawan (dipping sauces) on the table**: small saucers of **soy
  sauce with calamansi** (toyomansi), **cane or coconut vinegar with
  garlic, onion and chilli** (sinamak, spiced vinegar), **fish sauce
  (patis)**, **bagoong** (fermented shrimp paste), **banana ketchup**
  for fried food. Each diner mixes their own in a small dish. [HIGH —
  Wikipedia "Philippine condiments" via search, uncontested]

  tablecloth, sometimes under a clear glass or plastic sheet; a
  **food cover (a domed mesh or woven "taklob")** over dishes between
  servings. [LOW-MEDIUM — not re-checked; widely observed]
- **Eating with the hands (kamayan)** survives at boodle fights, beach
  grills and in provincial homes, especially with fried fish and rice.
  [HIGH — Wikipedia "Kamayan", Panlasang Pinoy]

### VISUAL & PLATING NORMS

- **Palette**: the white of rice; the deep soy-brown of adobo and
  humba; lechon amber; annatto orange (kare-kare, inasal, Vigan
  empanada); the cloudy pale broth and bright green kangkong of
  sinigang; coconut cream and chilli red (Bicol); banana-leaf green;
  ube purple and leche-flan yellow in desserts. [EDITORIAL]
- **Home food is generous and unfussy**: ulam in bowls, rice heaped, no
  garnish beyond a scatter of spring onion or fried garlic.
  Restaurant "modern Filipino" plating exists but is not the default.
  [EDITORIAL]
- **Shared, not plated singly**: a home or fiesta table shows several
  dishes at once around the rice; a single composed plate fits a
  carinderia rice-plus-ulam, a silog plate or a street item. [MEDIUM]
- **Sawsawan saucers** are a quiet but authentic marker — one or two
  small dishes of dark soy-calamansi or vinegar with chilli near the
  plates. [EDITORIAL]
- **Light**: strong tropical daylight, deep shade under eaves, humid
  haze; afternoon merienda light is warm and low; rainy-season
  (June–October) grey-green light is just as real. Grade neutrally —
  no oversaturated "tropical paradise" filter. [EDITORIAL]

### ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES

#### General environmental norms

- **"Home" is overwhelmingly a single house.** The 2020 census counted
  **25.2 million occupied housing units: 87.6% single houses**,
  6.5% apartment/accessoria/row house, 3.0% duplex, 2.1% other
  multi-unit residential and **only 0.7% condominium/condotel** [HIGH —
  PSA 2020 Census of Population and Housing]. **85.9% have galvanised
  iron or aluminium roofs**; 4.7% cogon/nipa/anahaw thatch; 4.5% concrete
  or clay tile. **60.4% have concrete/brick/stone outer walls**, 14.5%
  wood, 11.5% half concrete and half wood, 11.1% bamboo/sawali/nipa
  [HIGH — PSA 2020]. **So the national baseline is a one- or two-storey
  concrete house with a corrugated-metal roof**, painted plaster walls,
  iron window grilles and a small front yard or carport — not a condo
  tower and not a nipa hut. [EDITORIAL inference from the figures]
  [SOURCE: [PSA — Housing Characteristics in the Philippines (2020 CPH)](https://psa.gov.ph/content/housing-characteristics-philippines-2020-census-population-and-housing)]
- **Metro Manila exception**: condominiums, townhouses (row houses in
  gated "subdivisions"), and dense informal neighbourhoods are the
  capital's visible housing; a young professional or a small couple in
  Makati, BGC, Ortigas or Quezon City plausibly lives in a **small condo
  unit** (studio or one-bedroom, a two-seat dining table against a wall,
  a window onto towers). [EDITORIAL; condo share nationally only 0.7%]
- **§5.2 contrast**: even more house-dominant than Mexico or India; far
  from Spain's or Türkiye's flat majority.
- **Interior markers (pick one or two per scene)**: tiled floor (white
  or beige ceramic, or polished cement); a wooden or glass-topped dining
  table with monobloc or wooden chairs; a lace or printed plastic
  tablecloth; an electric stand fan or ceiling fan; jalousie (glass
  louvre) or sliding windows with grilles; a rice cooker on the kitchen
  counter; a "Last Supper" wall plaque or a small Santo Niño statue —
  **keep religious objects out of frame or deeply out of focus** (hard
  rule 5); framed graduation photos on the wall. [MEDIUM — uncontested
  general knowledge, not individually re-checked]
- **Exterior markers**: a gate and a small concrete yard with potted
  plants; a sari-sari store window in a neighbour's façade; tricycles
  and jeepneys on the street (**no legible paintwork**); power lines
  thick overhead; banana and coconut trees in provincial lots; Spanish-
  era stone-and-wood houses in Vigan, Taal or Iloilo. [MEDIUM — not
  individually re-checked]
- **Outdoor eating spaces**: a **garage or carport** turned into a party
  space for birthdays and fiestas (monobloc chairs, rented tables, a
  tarpaulin); a **front porch**; a **nipa-roofed kubo hut** (open bamboo
  pavilion) in a provincial yard or resort. [LOW-MEDIUM — not re-checked;
  widely observed]
- **Gen Z lens (§5.3)**: many young adults live with parents (extended
  families are common); students and young workers in Manila, Cebu and
  Baguio rent **bedspaces, dorms or boarding houses** or share small
  condos, and order through delivery apps (never show legible branded
  bags) [LOW — not independently re-checked]. Stage a young adult at the
  family table, at a condo counter with delivered food decanted onto
  plates, or at a milk-tea-and-rice-meal café — neither a crowded
  boarding house nor a showroom.
- **Caricature avoidance [EDITORIAL]**:
  - **Postcard Philippines**: every scene on a white-sand beach with a
    hammock and a coconut drink; the nipa hut as the default home.
  - **Poverty Philippines**: slums, flooded streets and scavengers as the
    "authentic" default. Street food is shown clean and bright.
  - **Generic Asia**: chopsticks, Thai curries, pagodas, kimono-style
    clothing, sticky rice.
  - **Luau Philippines**: Hawaiian leis, tiki torches and pineapple
    centrepieces around a lechon.
  - The ordinary baseline is a tidy concrete house with a tiled dining
    room, a busy clean carinderia, a mall food court with no branding,
    and a family fiesta under a carport tarp.

## CELEBRATIONS & LARGE GATHERINGS

Schema §5.7 applies: the frame shows only the operator's party (1, 2
or a small group of place settings) at one stretch of a bigger table;
the gathering is implied, never counted out. Hard rules 1–8 above all
still apply, above all **hard rule 4**: no inuman, no pulutan-at-night
composition (sisig, chicharon, kropek on a sizzling plate after dark),
no beer, gin or rum anywhere, even at evening receptions where drinking
is real; **hard rule 3**: no pork in zone 7 or beside Muslim-coded
cues; **hard rule 5**: no product near a Santo Niño, altar, belen, church
or procession.

### How large gatherings work here

- **Who gathers**: the extended clan (titos, titas, lolos, cousins),
  godparents (ninongs and ninangs), neighbours, and at fiestas anyone who
  walks in. Filipino social life runs on **handaan** (a feast prepared
  for guests) or **salu-salo**: birthdays, fiestas, graduations,
  christenings and holidays [MEDIUM — Barong World and Jojie's fiesta
  guides (tier 4), consistent with factsanddetails and SunStar].
- **Typical size**: a home birthday or christening is roughly 20–60
  guests [LOW — not verified this pass]; a catered debut or wedding is
  **100 to 200+**, with caterers splitting buffets above 200 [MEDIUM —
  Event Nest debut and wedding guides (tier 3, vendor)]; a fiesta open
  house has no count, people come and go all day [MEDIUM — SunStar,
  factsanddetails].
- **Where (intake venue)**: **home outdoor** is the default (a carport
  or front yard turned into a party space with rented tables, monobloc
  chairs and a tarpaulin or tent overhead; see ENVIRONMENT, Meal outdoors
  at home); **home indoor** for Noche Buena and Media Noche; **restaurant**
  (a function room or a Filipino restaurant) for christening and
  birthday lunches in cities; **other** for weddings and debuts (a
  hotel ballroom, events place, garden venue or barangay hall).
- **Table form and serving style**: the **buffet** is the default for
  any big handaan: a long table along a wall with foil trays or
  chafing dishes, a lechon at the head, a rice tray, and guests sitting
  at round or rectangular rented tables with their filled plates. A
  standard catered buffet runs **rice (plain and garlic), two or three
  meat dishes, one seafood, one vegetable, a pasta, a dessert spread**
  [MEDIUM — Executive Gourmet and Event Nest (tier 3, vendors)]. Family
  style at round tables is used for 80–150 guests [MEDIUM — Event Nest].
  Noche Buena and Media Noche are one family table, everything at once.
- **Who serves**: relatives and helpers at home (the cooks are often
  aunts or a hired "kusinera"); caterer staff in uniform behind chafing
  dishes at catered events; guests serve themselves.
- **Plate and cutlery norms that differ**: still spoon and fork, no knife;
  big events use caterers' white china or heavy paper and plastic plates
  at home parties; rice is served by the scoop from a tray; take-home
  "balot" (food wrapped for guests) is common [LOW — not verified].
  [EDITORIAL]
- **Snapshot staging default for the Philippines [EDITORIAL]**: the three
  most authentic crowd cues here are (1) **a lechon on a banana-leaf tray
  at the head of a buffet**, soft in the background or cropped at the
  edge (outside zone 7); (2) **a row of foil trays or chafing dishes**
  (pancit, spaghetti, lumpia, caldereta) running out of frame; (3)
  **monobloc chairs, rented tables under a tarpaulin or tent, and
  banderitas** (small triangular bunting) overhead, with no legible text
  on any tarp or banner.

## ZONE CALLOUTS (environment + dish pointers)

1. **Metro Manila & Central/Southern Luzon** — Condos, townhouses,
   gated subdivisions and dense barangays; malls and food courts;
   jeepneys and tricycles; carinderias everywhere. Tagalog home cooking
   (adobo, sinigang, tinola, nilaga), Kapampangan cooking in Pampanga
   (sisig, kare-kare, tocino — Pampanga styles itself the culinary
   capital [MEDIUM — not re-checked]), Batangas lomi and bulalo, Luzon-
   style lechon with liver sauce (La Loma, Quezon City).
   → catalog: Adobo; Sinigang; Kare-kare; Sisig; Lechon (Luzon);
   Pancit; Lumpia; Spaghetti; Fried chicken; Silog; Bulalo; Tinola;

2. **North Luzon — Ilocos & Cordillera** — Vigan's cobblestone Calle
   Crisologo and bahay na bato; pine-forest Baguio; Ifugao terraces.
   **Bagnet** (deep-fried pork belly slabs), **pinakbet** (vegetables
   with bagoong), **Vigan longganisa** (small, garlicky, sour),
   **Vigan/Ilocos empanada** (orange rice-flour shell with egg,
   longganisa and green papaya). Basi wine (documented only).
   → catalog: Bagnet & pinakbet; Ilocos empanada.
3. **Bicol** — Mayon volcano, coconut groves, chilli. **Laing** (taro
   leaves in coconut milk), **Bicol express** (pork in coconut milk with
   lots of chilli), **pinangat**, **kinunot** (shark or stingray in
   coconut milk).
   → catalog: Bicol express & laing.
4. **Western Visayas** — Iloilo's old mansions and Jaro cathedral;
   Bacolod's sugar country and MassKara. **La Paz batchoy** (noodle soup
   with pork, liver, crackling), **chicken inasal** (Bacolod), **KBL**

   → catalog: Batchoy; Chicken inasal.
5. **Central Visayas — Cebu & Bohol** — Cebu's coast and city, Bohol's

   sauce), **puso** (rice in woven coconut-leaf pouch), **sutukil**

   no alcohol.** **Satti** (Tausug/Zamboanga breakfast skewers in a

   soup with burnt coconut), **piaparan**, **beef rendang**, **curacha**
   (Zamboanga spanner crab), **knickerbocker** (fruit dessert), **dodol**

### A. National core (ulam with rice)

#### Adobo (chicken, pork, or both)

- **Category**: Everyday — the unofficial national dish; home, baon,
  carinderia. **Halal**: chicken adobo can be halal; pork adobo is not.
- **Lineage**: Native Filipino braise in vinegar (a pre-Hispanic
  preservation method) that took a Spanish name; soy sauce is a Chinese-
  Filipino addition. Not Mexican or Spanish adobo, despite the name
  [MEDIUM — not independently re-checked this pass; widely stated].
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **dark soy-vinegar adobo** (the Manila/national
  form); **adobong puti** (white adobo, vinegar and salt, no soy — pale);
  **adobo sa gata** (with coconut milk, Bicol/South); **adobong dilaw**
  (turmeric, Batangas/Cavite); **adobong pusit** (squid in black ink).
  **Default when unspecified**: dark chicken-and-pork adobo, or chicken
  only for a national brief (hard rule 3). [EDITORIAL]
- **Format**: plated over or beside rice; served from a bowl at home.
- **Vessel & scale**: a ceramic serving bowl ~18–20 cm, then onto a
  ~25 cm plate; chicken pieces 6–9 cm bone-in; pork belly cubes 3–4 cm.
- **Texture & finish**: meat glossy, deep chestnut-to-near-black brown,
  edges caramelised and slightly crisp where pan-fried after braising;
  pork fat translucent amber; sauce thin, dark, oily, pooling, not a
  gravy; whole soft garlic cloves, black peppercorns, one or two dried
  bay leaves.
- **Staging**: a weekday lunch, a baon box decanted, the Sunday table.
- **Model failure / confusion**: Japanese teriyaki (sticky sweet glaze,
  sesame); Chinese red-braised pork (star anise, glossy lacquer);
  Mexican adobo (red chilli paste); a thick brown "stew gravy".
- **Confidence**: MEDIUM overall (uncontested; variants not re-checked).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one plate served from the pot.
  - **What dominates**: rice ~50% of the plate; adobo meat ~35%; sauce
    pooling ~10%; garlic and bay accents ~5%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Rice | dome ~10–11 cm across, ~5 cm high (one "cup", ~200 g) | 1 | White, steaming | Left half of the plate |
    | Chicken pieces | 6–9 cm bone-in thigh or drumstick | 2 per portion; 8–10 in the serving bowl | Dark glossy brown, skin taut, edges crisped | Right half, against the rice |
    | Pork belly cubes (if mixed) | 3–4 cm, layered fat and lean | 2–3 per portion | Brown lean, amber translucent fat | Between the chicken |
    | Sauce | 3–5 mm pool | — | Thin, near-black, oil beads on top | Under the meat, seeping into the rice edge |
    | Garlic cloves | whole, 1.5–2 cm | 3–4 | Soft, tan-brown | Scattered on the meat |
    | Bay leaf | 6–8 cm | 1 | Dull olive-brown, dried | Tucked against the meat |
    | Peppercorns | 4–5 mm | a few | Black | In the sauce |

  - **Arrangement**: rice and adobo side by side, sauce running toward
    the rice; no garnish.
  - **Vessel fill**: the plate's centre ~70% covered, rim visible all
    round.
  - **Served portion vs. whole**: the serving bowl holds 8–10 pieces in
    sauce; one portion is 2 pieces with a spoonful of sauce.
  - **State cues**: light steam from the rice; sauce glistening with oil;
    meat edges darker and crisp.
  - **Absent on purpose**: sesame seeds, spring onion pile, chilli
    flakes, potatoes and carrots (that is a different stew), thick gravy,
    chopsticks, a knife.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A white plate a little under twice the can's
    height across: a domed mound of steaming white rice on the left and,
    beside it, two bone-in chicken pieces braised deep glossy brown in
    soy and vinegar, edges crisped, a few soft whole garlic cloves, black
    peppercorns and one dried bay leaf, thin dark oily sauce seeping into
    the rice. Spoon and fork, no knife, no garnish, no sesame."

#### Sinigang (sour tamarind soup — pork, shrimp or fish)

- **Category**: Everyday — the comfort soup-ulam, especially in the rainy
  season. **Halal**: shrimp or fish sinigang is pork-free.
- **Lineage**: Native Filipino (Tagalog heartland).
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **sinigang na baboy** (pork ribs/belly), **na
  hipon** (shrimp), **na bangus** (milkfish), **sa miso** (fish with
  miso, cloudier); sour base tamarind (default), guava (bayabas, pinker),
  kamias or green mango. **Default when unspecified**: pork sinigang
  with tamarind; shrimp for a pork-free brief. [EDITORIAL]
- **Format**: soup served in a bowl beside the rice; spooned over rice.
- **Vessel & scale**: a family tureen or a pot ~22–25 cm; individual
  bowl ~15–16 cm; okra pods 6–8 cm; kangkong stems 8–12 cm.
- **Texture & finish**: broth pale, slightly cloudy, greyish-gold with
  fine fat droplets; vegetables bright — green kangkong leaves wilting,
  okra whole and glossy, long beans in 6–8 cm pieces, white daikon
  slices, soft tomato wedges, a whole long green chilli (siling haba);
  pork ribs pale grey-brown, tender.
- **Model failure / confusion**: Thai tom yum (red chilli oil,
  lemongrass, galangal, mushrooms); Vietnamese canh chua (pineapple,
  bean sprouts); miso soup.
- **Confidence**: MEDIUM (uncontested; not re-checked this pass).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one serving bowl beside rice.
  - **What dominates**: broth ~45% of the bowl surface; leafy kangkong
    ~20%; meat ~15%; other vegetables ~20%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count (per bowl) | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Broth | fills to ~1.5 cm below rim | — | Pale cloudy gold, fat droplets | Base |
    | Pork ribs / belly | 4–5 cm chunks | 3–4 | Pale grey-brown, bone showing | Half-submerged |
    | Kangkong (water spinach) | leaves 5–8 cm, stems 8–12 cm | a tangle | Dark green, wilted, glossy | Floating on top, one side |
    | Okra | 6–8 cm whole | 2 | Bright green, ridged | On top |
    | Sitaw (long beans) | 6–8 cm lengths | 3–4 | Green | Half-submerged |
    | Labanos (daikon) | 4–5 cm rounds, 5 mm thick | 3 | White, translucent | In broth |
    | Tomato | wedges ~3 cm | 2–3 | Soft red, skins loosening | In broth |
    | Siling haba | 8–10 cm long green chilli | 1 | Glossy pale green | Floating diagonally |
    | Rice (separate plate) | ~10 cm dome | 1 | White | Beside the bowl |

  - **Arrangement**: kangkong and chilli float on top; meat and roots
    below the surface, edges breaking through.
  - **Vessel fill**: broth ~85% of the bowl's depth; vegetables mounded
    slightly above the broth line.
  - **Served portion vs. whole**: the pot or tureen sits mid-table; each
    diner's small bowl or a ladle of broth goes over their rice.
  - **State cues**: steam rising strongly; a few fat droplets on the
    surface.
  - **Absent on purpose**: red chilli oil, lemongrass, mushrooms, lime
    wedges, coriander, noodles.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A deep white bowl about the can's height
    across, filled with pale, slightly cloudy golden sour broth, steam
    rising: a tangle of wilted dark-green water-spinach and a long green
    chilli floating on top, two whole okra pods, short green bean
    lengths, white radish rounds, soft tomato wedges and three tender
    pork rib chunks half-submerged. Beside it a plate with a mound of
    white rice. No red oil, no lime, no herbs."

#### Kare-kare (oxtail and vegetables in peanut sauce)

- **Category**: Special-occasion and Sunday — fiestas, family lunches,
  Pampanga's pride. **Halal**: the beef version is pork-free (some add
  pork hock); bagoong is shrimp.
- **Lineage**: Kapampangan/Tagalog; origin disputed (Moro, Indian-sepoy
  "curry" theories) [MEDIUM — studiotributes blog via search; origin
  contested]. Not a curry.
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **oxtail** (default), oxtail with tripe (tuwalya),
  pork hock, seafood kare-kare; **crispy kare-kare** (lechon kawali in
  the sauce, modern). **Default when unspecified**: oxtail. [EDITORIAL]
- **Format**: served in a clay palayok or bowl; spooned over rice; bagoong
  alamang on the side.
- **Vessel & scale**: a palayok or ceramic bowl ~22–25 cm; oxtail rounds
  5–7 cm across; eggplant slices 4–6 cm; pechay 12–15 cm; banana heart
  wedges 5–7 cm.
- **Texture & finish**: sauce thick, smooth, matte orange-tan from
  annatto and ground peanuts, thickened with toasted rice; oxtail with
  gelatinous glistening fat rings around the bone; vegetables blanched
  bright — dark-green pechay (bok choy), green string beans, purple-skinned
  eggplant, pale banana heart [HIGH — Panlasang Pinoy, Just One Cookbook,
  Rasa Malaysia agree on oxtail, peanut, annatto, toasted rice,
  vegetables, bagoong on the side].
- **Model failure / confusion**: Thai red or massaman curry (oily red
  surface, potatoes); Indonesian satay sauce; a West African peanut
  stew (with tomato, darker). **Bagoong is served on the side, never
  stirred in** [HIGH — same sources].
- **Confidence**: HIGH for components; MEDIUM for sizes.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one clay pot at table.
  - **What dominates**: orange peanut sauce ~45% of the pot surface;
    vegetables ~30%; oxtail ~25%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count (per pot) | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Peanut sauce | ~3–4 cm deep | — | Thick, smooth, matte orange-tan | Base filling the pot |
    | Oxtail rounds | 5–7 cm across, bone centre | 5–6 (1–2 per portion) | Brown meat, glistening gelatinous fat ring | Half-submerged, centre |
    | Pechay (bok choy) | 12–15 cm half-heads | 2–3 | Dark green leaves, white stems | Laid along one side on top |
    | String beans | bundled 8–10 cm lengths | a small bundle | Bright green | On top, one side |
    | Eggplant | 4–6 cm diagonal slices | 4–5 | Purple skin, soft cream flesh | On top, beside beans |
    | Banana heart (puso ng saging) | 5–7 cm wedges | 2–3 | Pale cream-violet layers | Edge |
    | Bagoong alamang | a teaspoon to a tablespoon | 1 saucer | Pinkish-purple to brown, coarse paste | Separate small saucer beside the pot |
    | Rice (each plate) | ~10 cm dome | 1 per diner | White | Plates |

  - **Arrangement**: vegetables arranged in neat sections on top of the
    sauce (a restaurant habit); at home they are mixed in.
  - **Vessel fill**: pot ~80% full; meat and vegetables rise ~2 cm above
    the sauce.
  - **Served portion vs. whole**: one portion is an oxtail round, a
    spoon of sauce and a few vegetables over rice, with a dab of bagoong
    at the plate edge.
  - **State cues**: sauce warm and thick, not steaming much; a slight
    oil sheen at the edges; vegetables bright and just-cooked.
  - **Absent on purpose**: potatoes, chilli oil, coriander, lime, naan,
    peanuts scattered on top (crushed peanuts are a rare garnish — omit).
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A round unglazed brown clay pot about one and
    a half can-heights across, filled with thick, smooth, matte orange
    peanut sauce; five or six oxtail rounds with glistening gelatinous fat
    rings half-submerged in the centre, neat bundles of bright green beans,
    dark-green bok choy and purple eggplant slices on top. Beside it a
    small saucer of pinkish-brown shrimp paste and plates of white rice.
    No potatoes, no chilli oil."

#### Tinola and nilaga (clear ginger or boiled-meat soups)

- **Category**: Everyday — home soups, sick-day food, rainy-season
  dinner. **Halal**: chicken tinola and beef nilaga are pork-free.
- **Lineage**: Native Filipino (tinola is in Rizal's *Noli Me Tangere*
  — [LOW — not re-checked]).
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **tinolang manok** — chicken in ginger broth with
  green papaya or sayote and malunggay or chilli leaves; **nilagang
  baka/baboy** — beef or pork boiled with potato, corn on the cob,
  cabbage, pechay, saba banana. **Default when unspecified**: chicken
  tinola. [EDITORIAL]
- **Format**: soup bowl beside rice; patis (fish sauce) with chilli for
  dipping.
- **Vessel & scale**: individual bowl ~15–16 cm; papaya wedges 4–6 cm;
  chicken pieces 6–8 cm.
- **Texture & finish**: broth **clear**, pale gold, ginger slivers
  visible; chicken pale (not browned); green papaya translucent pale
  green; small dark-green malunggay (moringa) leaflets floating like
  confetti.
- **Model failure / confusion**: Chinese chicken soup; Vietnamese pho;
  Korean samgyetang (whole small chicken).
- **Confidence**: MEDIUM (not re-checked this pass).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one bowl.
  - **What dominates**: clear broth ~55%; chicken ~25%; papaya ~15%;
    leaves ~5%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |

#### Bulalo (Batangas beef-shank marrow soup)

#### Pancit (bihon, canton, palabok) — the long-life noodles

- **Category**: Merienda and celebration — every birthday and fiesta
  (long noodles = long life, never cut) [HIGH — Executive Gourmet,
  Manila Sun, studiotributes and others agree]; also everyday merienda.
  **Halal**: usually pork and chicken; a chicken-only version exists.
- **Lineage**: Chinese-Filipino (Hokkien "pian i sit") adapted [MEDIUM —
  not independently re-checked].
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **pancit bihon** (thin rice vermicelli) and
  **pancit canton** (thick yellow egg noodles) — often mixed
  (bihon-canton, "bam-i" in Cebu); **pancit palabok/luglug** (thick rice
  noodles under an orange shrimp-annatto sauce topped with crushed
  chicharon, shrimp, hard-boiled egg slices, tinapa flakes, spring
  onion); **pancit habhab** (Lucban, eaten off banana leaf), **pancit
  Malabon**, **lomi** (Batangas, thick soup). **Default when
  unspecified**: bihon-canton in a bilao. [EDITORIAL]
- **Format**: party bilao (woven tray) for sharing; small plate at
  merienda; calamansi halves on the side.
- **Vessel & scale**: a **bilao** 30–45 cm across lined with banana leaf

- **Texture & finish (bihon)**: thin, glossy, pale-tan noodles tangled

#### Filipino-style spaghetti (sweet spaghetti)

- **Category**: Party and everyday — birthdays, Christmas, children's
  favourite; also fast-food register. **Halal**: hot dog and ground pork
  are standard; a beef version exists.
- **Lineage**: Filipino adaptation of Italian-American spaghetti with
  meat sauce — **not Italian**: sweetened with banana ketchup or sugar
  or condensed milk, with sliced hot dogs and grated processed cheese
  [HIGH — Wikipedia "Filipino spaghetti", SBS Food, Kawaling Pinoy
  agree]. Banana ketchup itself is a WWII-era Filipino invention [HIGH —
  Wikipedia "Banana ketchup"].
- **Variants (§4.6)**: home party version in a big tray (default for
  scenes); fast-food style single plate (**stage unbranded, on a plain
  plate**, hard rule 7).
- **Format**: a foil or ceramic tray at parties; a single plate beside
  fried chicken.
- **Vessel & scale**: a ~25 cm plate; hot dog coins ~2 cm across,
  5–8 mm thick.
- **Texture & finish**: soft, well-cooked spaghetti; **sauce bright
  orange-red, glossy, sweet-looking, thick and clingy**, with small
  browned ground-meat bits; **bright red-orange hot dog coins**; a heavy
  mound of **finely grated pale-yellow processed cheese** on top, not
  melted, fluffy.
- **Model failure / confusion**: Italian spaghetti bolognese (dark red,
  basil, parmesan shavings); American spaghetti and meatballs (no
  meatballs here).
- **Confidence**: HIGH for components.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one plate.
  - **What dominates**: sauced spaghetti ~65% of the mound; grated cheese
    cap ~20%; hot dog coins ~15%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |

    | Hot dog coins | ~2 cm across, 5–8 mm thick | 8–12 | Bright red-orange skin, pink inside | Scattered on top and through |

  - **Arrangement**: mounded, cheese capping the top, hot dog coins

  - **Vessel fill**: ~60% of the plate, wide rim showing.
  - **Served portion vs. whole**: from a party tray one portion is a

  - **State cues**: sauce glossy; cheese unmelted and fluffy.
  - **Absent on purpose**: basil, parmesan shavings, meatballs, a dark

  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A white plate a little under twice the can's

#### Filipino fried chicken with rice and gravy

- **Category**: Everyday — the national fast-food and party plate; home
  "pritong manok" too. **Halal**: halal if the chicken is.
- **Lineage**: American fried chicken adapted — always eaten **with rice
  and a smooth brown gravy**, with banana ketchup as an alternative dip
  [HIGH — Panlasang Pinoy, Kusina Secrets, Ang Sarap].

  coating, brown gravy cup — **stage unbranded**); **home pritong manok**

  golden-brown, with banana ketchup or spiced vinegar). **Default**: the
  home/carinderia plate with rice and gravy.
- **Format**: a plate with rice; gravy in a small cup or ladled over
  the rice.
- **Vessel & scale**: a ~25 cm plate (fast-food register: an oval
  melamine plate); a leg-quarter or thigh-drumstick 10–14 cm.
- **Texture & finish**: skin crisp, golden to deep golden-brown, thin

- **Model failure / confusion**: thick US Southern breading, Korean

- **Confidence**: MEDIUM-HIGH.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one plate.
  - **What dominates**: rice ~45%; chicken ~40%; gravy ~15%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

#### Lechon — style map (form-changing: §4.3 / §4.6)

The whole spit-roasted pig is the fiesta centrepiece everywhere outside
the Muslim south, but it comes in **two visibly different regional
forms**: [HIGH — Suroy.ph, Primer, Business Diary PH, Studio Tributes
agree]

| Variant | Where | What you see | Sauce |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cebu lechon** | Zone 5 (Cebu; Visayas) | Belly **stuffed and sewn** with lemongrass bundles, garlic, onions, leeks, star anise/pepper; very glassy, often reddish-amber skin; herb stalks visible where cut | **None** — "needs no sauce"; at most spiced vinegar |
| **Luzon / Manila (La Loma) lechon** | Zone 1 (Manila, Luzon) | **Little or no stuffing** (salt, pepper, maybe lemongrass); even golden-brown crackling | **Brown liver sauce** ("lechon sauce": mashed liver, vinegar, sugar, breadcrumbs) in a bowl beside it |

**Default when unspecified**: the **Luzon form with liver sauce** for a
zone-1 or national scene; **Cebu form** whenever Cebu or the Visayas is
named. Offer both (§4.6). [EDITORIAL] Related: **lechon kawali**
(home-fried pork belly, see below) and **lechon manok** (rotisserie
chicken) are everyday cousins.

#### Lechon (whole pig, fiesta table) — Luzon and Cebu forms

- **Category**: Special-occasion — fiestas, Christmas, weddings,
  birthdays (the status centrepiece). **Halal**: **pork — never in zone 7
  or any Muslim-coded scene** (hard rule 3).
- **Lineage**: Spanish-era name (lechón), Filipino practice; Cebu claims
  the best [HIGH — sources above].
- **Format**: the whole pig on a tray or banana-leaf-lined board at the
  head of a buffet; chopped pieces (skin + meat) on a platter for the
  table.
- **Vessel & scale**: whole pig **~80–100 cm long** on a ~1 m tray
  [LOW — not re-checked]; chopped pieces 5–8 cm with skin attached.
- **Texture & finish**: **skin lacquered, glassy, amber to deep
  mahogany, thin and shattering, covered in tiny bubbles**, a few darker
  spots at the ears and snout; under it a 1 cm band of white fat, then
  pale, moist meat; Cebu's cut belly shows green lemongrass stalks and
  soft garlic.
- **Staging**: the whole pig at the far end of a fiesta table, softly
  out of focus; the **hero sits near the chopped-lechon platter and the
  rice**, never touching the pig. **No apple in the mouth** (a
  Western/Hawaiian cliché — avoid). [EDITORIAL]
- **Model failure / confusion**: Hawaiian kalua pig (pulled, in leaves);
  Spanish cochinillo (small suckling pig, pale, in a clay dish); a glazed
  ham; Chinese roast pig (siu yuk, cut in rectangles); an apple in the
  mouth.
- **Confidence**: HIGH for the regional forms; LOW for sizes.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — chopped-lechon platter at a
  fiesta table (the whole pig behind).
  - **What dominates**: amber skin ~55% of the platter surface (pieces
    are cut and laid skin up); pale meat at cut faces ~30%; sauce bowl or
    herb stalks ~15%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Chopped pieces | 5–8 cm, skin-on, ~3–4 cm thick | 15–20 per platter; 2–3 per portion | Glassy amber crackling, white fat band, pale meat | Laid skin up, overlapping |
    | Liver sauce (Luzon) | bowl ~10 cm | 1 | Smooth, thick, opaque medium brown | Beside the platter |
    | Lemongrass and garlic (Cebu) | stalks 6–10 cm | 3–5 strands | Green-yellow stalks, soft garlic | Tucked between pieces |
    | Whole pig (background) | 80–100 cm long | 1 | Amber-mahogany glossy body on banana leaves | Far end of the table, out of focus |
    | Banana leaf | lining | — | Glossy green | Under platter and pig |
    | Rice (diners' plates) | domes ~10 cm | per diner | White | Plates |

  - **Arrangement**: pieces overlapping like tiles, crackling facing up
    and toward the light.
  - **Vessel fill**: ~85% of a ~35–40 cm platter.
  - **Served portion vs. whole**: a portion is 2–3 pieces with rice and a
    spoon of liver sauce (Luzon) or spiced vinegar (Cebu).
  - **State cues**: skin still glassy (not soggy); juices glistening at
    the cut faces.
  - **Absent on purpose**: apple in the mouth, pineapple rings, leis, tiki
    décor, a carving knife (stage pre-chopped), any alcohol bottle.
  - **Prompt-ready line (Luzon)**: "On a banana-leaf-lined platter about
    three can-heights across, overlapping chopped pieces of whole roast
    pig laid skin up, each roughly half the can's height, the skin glassy,
    amber and bubbled over a band of white fat and moist pale meat; a bowl
    of smooth, thick brown liver sauce beside it, the whole roast pig
    blurred at the far end of a fiesta table. No apple, no pineapple, no

  - **Prompt-ready line (Cebu)**: replace the liver sauce with "green
    lemongrass stalks and soft garlic cloves visible between the
    reddish-amber, very glassy pieces, no sauce, a saucer of spiced

#### Lechon kawali and crispy pata (fried pork belly / pork knuckle)

- **Category**: Everyday treat and restaurant sharing dish. **Halal**:
  pork — never in zone 7.
- **Lineage**: Filipino home-style; crispy pata is a Manila restaurant
  classic [MEDIUM — not re-checked].
- **Variants**: **lechon kawali** — boiled then deep-fried pork belly cut
  in 3–4 cm chunks (default); **crispy pata** — a whole deep-fried pork
  hock ~15–20 cm long served on a board; **bagnet** (zone 2, see B).
- **Format**: platter with a saucer of liver sauce or soy-vinegar-chilli.

#### Sisig (Kapampangan sizzling chopped pork)

- **Category**: Everyday — restaurant and carinderia ulam with rice;
  **also the classic pulutan** (bar snack) — so stage it only as a
  daytime rice meal (hard rule 4). **Halal**: pork; chicken, tuna and
  tofu sisig exist (a tuna or chicken sisig is pork-free).
- **Lineage**: **Kapampangan (Angeles City, Pampanga)**; the sizzling
  form was created in **1974 by Lucia Cunanan ("Aling Lucing")** from
  pig's head parts left over from Clark Air Base: the face and ears
  boiled, marinated in vinegar and calamansi, grilled over charcoal,
  chopped fine, mixed with raw onion and mashed chicken liver, and
  served sizzling on a cast-iron plate [HIGH — Wikipedia "Sisig" and
  "Lucia Cunanan", Positively Filipino, kapampangan.org]. The **raw egg
  cracked on top and mayonnaise are later Manila additions**, rejected
  by purists [MEDIUM — widely stated, not re-checked this pass].
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **Kapampangan** (no egg, no mayo, crisp, tangy —
  zone 1 Pampanga); **Manila restaurant sisig** (raw egg on top stirred
  in at table — default for a generic brief); chicken/tuna/tofu sisig.
- **Format**: on a sizzling plate, with rice and calamansi.
- **Vessel & scale**: black cast-iron oval sizzling plate ~22–26 ×
  16–18 cm on a wooden board [LOW — not re-checked]; pork bits 0.5–1.5 cm.
- **Texture & finish**: finely chopped bits of crisp ear cartilage,
  browned skin and soft meat — **mottled brown, golden and charred

  bright orange disc in the centre; two calamansi halves on the board.
- **Model failure / confusion**: Mexican carnitas or pulled pork (long
  shreds), ground-beef hash, Korean bulgogi on a hot plate.
- **Confidence**: HIGH for origin and components; LOW for plate size.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one sizzling plate.
  - **What dominates**: chopped pork ~75% of the plate surface; onion and
    chilli ~10%; egg ~8%; calamansi ~7%. [EDITORIAL]
