# Japan

## FILE ROLE & METHOD

This is Japan's country file: one national staging brief with eight
labeled internal zones. Each scene gets one TCCC hero beverage, named by
the brief (see HERO PRODUCT SLOT). It is staged against real Japanese
dishes, vessels and settings. The wider drinks landscape is documented
as context: free water and tea at every restaurant table, green and
barley tea at home, and alcohol, including TCCC's own lemon sour. Some of
these drinks are never staged.

**Scope.** This file uses the project default, the same as the Mexico,
Spain, Türkiye and Brazil files, so it is not a new decision. **Hirugohan**
(lunch), **bangohan / yūshoku** (dinner, the main family meal) and
snacks, street and festival food are in scope. **Asagohan** (breakfast)
is out of scope except through the opt-in MORNING MODULE, as in
`brazil.md`. Breakfast entries are written in full but flagged as
morning-only. The only beverage staged is the hero TCCC product.

### Hard staging rules specific to Japan (read before any scene)

1. **No alcohol staged, ever. That includes TCCC's own alcohol.** Coca-Cola
   Japan sells a canned lemon sour (chūhai), **檸檬堂 (Lemon-Dou)**. It was
   the company's first own-brand alcoholic drink and was fully relaunched
   in March 2025 in 5% and 7% ABV versions [HIGH: Coca-Cola Japan media
   centre releases of 2025-02-27 and 2025-08-25, Nikkei, ja.wikipedia (via
   search)]. **Never stage a Lemon-Dou can or any can that reads as a
   chūhai, highball or beer.** Most of these are tall 350 mL or 500 mL cans
   in the same format as the hero, so a model can easily swap one in.
   Beer, sake, shōchū and highballs are documented in ICONIC BEVERAGES
   only.
2. **The izakaya is a risky register. Default away from it.** An
   izakaya (a pub-restaurant with small plates) is where yakitori,
   karaage and edamame are most often eaten with beer. Even with the
   alcohol removed, its cues read as a drinking scene: a beer tap, a
   tower of mugs, sake flasks, a red lantern outside, a salaryman crowd.
   Stage yakitori and karaage in **non-drinking registers**: a yakitori
   takeaway counter or supermarket deli, a festival stall, a home table,
   a bento, a teishoku. Stage them at a counter only if it reads as a
   **lunch** or family counter.
   [EDITORIAL; the izakaya-beer pairing is uncontested general knowledge,
   not independently re-checked this pass]
3. **Free water and tea are the most likely intruders.** Almost every
   Japanese restaurant brings a glass of cold water (**お冷, ohiya**) or
   tea unasked. Ramen and teishoku shops often have a self-serve water
   jug. Many Japanese restaurants and sushi bars serve hot tea after the
   meal [HIGH: Otonanswer, MATCHA, Visipri food-service dictionary, all
   via search]. A model will add a water tumbler and a **yunomi** (a tall
   handleless tea cup) to every restaurant table. **Exclude both by
   name.** At home, name and exclude the **barley-tea jug** (mugicha, a
   summer staple) and the teapot too.
4. **Chopstick and altar taboos.** Never show **chopsticks standing
   upright in a bowl of rice** (tsukitate-bashi). It evokes the rice
   offered to the dead at funerals. Rest chopsticks on a **hashioki**
   (chopstick rest) or across the front of the tray, tips to the left.
   Never place a hero product on or beside a **butsudan** (household
   Buddhist altar), an Obon offering shelf, a **kamidana** (Shinto
   shelf), or inside a shrine or temple precinct. [EDITORIAL rule; the
   funeral association is uncontested general knowledge, not
   independently re-checked this pass]
5. **General project rules**: no legible text anywhere (Japanese scenes
   are dense with signage, noren curtains with characters, menu strips on
   walls, and packaging, so expect to fight this); nothing held in a
   hand; no drink other than the hero; never a TCCC product as a mixer with alcohol (non-alcoholic TCCC-brand mixes are allowed, schema §5.5).
6. **No dietary hard rule of the halal or beef-avoidance kind applies.**
   Pork, beef, chicken and seafood are all everyday. Pork is the default
   curry meat in the east, and katsu and ramen chāshū are pork. Two
   narrower sensitivities apply: Obon altar offerings are **shōjin**
   (vegetarian Buddhist food), and whale meat is a real but contested
   food that should never be staged. [EDITORIAL]

### Structural decision: one file, eight zones (recommendation — reviewer has final say)

The `country-file-schema.md` §1.1 swap test, applied with the evidence
gathered this pass:

- **A strong national core travels everywhere.** This covers the
  ichijū-sansai home tray (rice, miso soup, a main and sides), the
  teishoku set meal, curry rice, karaage, tonkatsu, gyūdon, gyōza, ramen,
  udon and soba as categories, onigiri and bento, the konbini, and
  osechi at New Year. [HIGH for the tray layout (MAFF, nippon.com); the
  national reach of each dish is MEDIUM, from its own entry's sources]
- **Several dishes are genuinely form-changing by region** (§4.2) and
  look wrong transplanted:
  - **Okonomiyaki.** Osaka's is *mixed*: batter, cabbage and fillings
    stirred together and cooked as one thick cake. Hiroshima's is
    *layered*: a thin crêpe, a tall heap of cabbage, pork, a layer of
    fried noodles and an egg. [HIGH: Tenpos Food Media, Rekishi Kaidō,
    Rakuten Recipe and MAFF's Osaka entry agree (via search)] Showing one
    for the other is a visible error to anyone from either city.
  - **Ramen.** Sapporo miso, Hakata's milky tonkotsu with very thin
    straight noodles, and Tokyo's clear shōyu look different in the bowl.
    [HIGH: MAFF Hokkaido ramen entry; ja.wikipedia 博多ラーメン and
    Cookpit (via search)]
  - **The meat in curry and nikujaga.** Pork in the east and beef in
    Kansai. House Foods' 2010 survey drew the line in the Chūbu region.
    [HIGH: SUUMO Journal reporting House Foods, Tabizine, TownWork]
  - **Ozōni**, the New Year soup. Square grilled mochi in clear broth in
    the east; round mochi, often in white-miso broth, in Kansai. The line
    runs near Sekigahara. [HIGH: MAFF "aff" magazine, Weathernews,
    Otonanswer]
  - **Okinawa's Ryūkyū kitchen** (Okinawa soba, gōyā champurū, pork
    luncheon meat) and **Hokkaido's jingisukan** do not exist as everyday
    food on Honshū.
- **Environment does not travel.** These look nothing alike: Tokyo's
  dense mid-rise apartments and narrow residential lanes; Kyoto's
  wooden machiya; Osaka's neon-bright shopping arcades; Hokkaido's
  snow-country houses with steep metal roofs and wide roads; Okinawa's
  flat-roofed concrete houses with water tanks and shīsā lions; and
  Tohoku's rice-field towns under heavy snow.

**Recommendation: one national file, eight zones**, handled as
dish-variant and environment deltas. This follows the pattern of
Mexico (seven zones), Brazil (seven) and Türkiye (seven), not a US-style
index-plus-regional-files split.

**Flagged spinout candidate (not triggered)**: **zone 6, Okinawa**. It
has a distinct Ryūkyū cuisine and a strong American post-war layer (pork
luncheon meat, taco rice). Its architecture and climate are subtropical,
Obon follows the lunar calendar, and even the cola history differs:
Pepsi was bottled in Okinawa from 1954, under US administration, before
it reached mainland Japan [MEDIUM: ja.wikipedia ペプシコーラ (via
search)]. If Okinawa briefs grow, split it out to `japan-okinawa.md`
(§2.2). **This is a recommendation for Fernando, not a decision** (§7).

### Default when no zone is named

Fall back to **zone 1 (Tokyo) at the everyday register**. That means a
compact apartment dining table, or a neighbourhood teishoku shop, ramen
shop or konbini. Tokyo is the largest metro area, the place every
regional chain and style coexists, and the place an image model is most
likely to draw correctly without caricature. [EDITORIAL fallback, not a
sourced "most typical Japan" claim]

## METHOD NOTE (read first)

**Build method, this pass.** The file was drafted directly from model
knowledge and checked with 40 WebSearch queries in the same session (a
shared search cap was in force). The searches went to the claims a
scene visibly depends on: pack formats and can dimensions, housing,
dinner time, the tray layout, vessel diameters (rice bowl, soup bowl,
ramen bowl, donburi), piece sizes (onigiri, karaage, yakitori, sushi,
gyōza, takoyaki, tonkatsu), regional form changes, and festival dates.
No page was fetched. Tags mean:

- **[HIGH] / [MEDIUM] / [LOW]**: schema §6 tags earned this pass (HIGH =
  2+ independent corroborating sources; MEDIUM = 1 credible source).
- **[… — not independently re-checked this pass]**: model knowledge that
  is uncontested general culinary or cultural knowledge but was not
  individually searched. Plausible, not verified.
- **[EDITORIAL]**: a judgment call (defaults, portion counts, surface
  shares, caricature-avoidance guidance), never a factual claim.
- **[HIGH — first-party test]**: this project's own image-generation
  findings (`coca-cola-guidelines.md` §1, `country-file-schema.md` §7.5).
  No Japan-specific image tests have been run.
- **"(via search)"**: the claim rests on a search-result snippet of the
  named source, not a full page read.
- **Tier-4 sources** (recipe sites, calorie sites, Q&A boards, retailer
  blogs) were used only for piece weights and vessel sizes where nothing
  institutional exists, and they are marked when used.

**Writing principle.** Describe what the camera sees. Japanese food is
photographed in **small, separate vessels**, so the scale error to fear
is **over-sizing**. An image model draws a rice bowl as a Western cereal
bowl, a miso-soup bowl as a soup tureen, and a karaage piece as a
chicken tender. Every entry states vessel diameters against the 350 mL
can (12.2 cm tall, 6.6 cm diameter).

**File-wide rules for every scene built from this file:**

1. **Text as atmosphere.** Noren curtains, lanterns, menu strips (tanzaku
   menus on the wall), ticket-machine buttons, konbini shelf labels,
   vending-machine fronts, bento lids and onigiri wrappers appear only as
   heavily blurred, unreadable colour. Any readable character, number or
   brand mark means reject or retouch. Blur instructions are known to fail
   [HIGH — first-party test; `country-file-schema.md` §7.5]. **Japanese
   script is a strong prior**, and a model will cheerfully invent fake
   kanji. Ask for "plain unmarked fabric" and "blank paper strips".
2. **The hero product is a TCCC beverage chosen per brief** (HERO PRODUCT
   SLOT). Branding is composited in post (`coca-cola-guidelines.md` §1–2).
3. **No alcohol in any scene**, and never a TCCC product as a mixer with alcohol (non-alcoholic TCCC-brand mixes are allowed, schema §5.5).
   No izakaya drinking cues (hard rule 2). No Lemon-Dou.
4. **No drinks in frame other than the hero product** unless the brief
   explicitly allows a named non-alcoholic companion. Name the likely
   intruders in the negative: a glass of water, a yunomi tea cup, a
   teapot, a barley-tea jug, a miso-soup bowl mistaken for a cup (keep
   the soup bowl lidded or clearly holding soup), canned coffee, and
   plastic bottles of green tea.
5. **Nothing held in a hand.** Onigiri, takoyaki, crêpes and taiyaki rest
   on a surface (`country-file-schema.md` §7.5).
6. **Chopsticks lie flat**: on a hashioki or across the tray front, tips
   left, never standing in rice (hard rule 4).

## TRUSTED CONTENT

### HERO PRODUCT SLOT

Every scene carries one TCCC hero product, chosen by the brief. This file
uses `south-africa.md`'s generalised slot. Since 2026-09-27 that has been
the project-wide rule: the brief dictates the SKU, never the region (see
`DECISIONS.md` and `country-file-schema.md` §5.4).

**Template:**
> [HERO PRODUCT]: {brand and variant exactly as named on pack}, in
> {format and size}, {dominant pack colour and material cue},
> {negated lookalikes}. {Position: standing upright on the surface,
> label facing camera or turned slightly}. Pack text will be composited
> in post.

**Rules:**
- **Name the variant; negate the closest lookalike.** Coca-Cola Original
  vs. Zero Sugar vs. **Zero Caffeine** (a Japan-market variant sold in
  PET), plus Fanta flavours. An unspecified "Coca-Cola can" rendered as
  the wrong variant in 2 of 3 generations [HIGH — first-party test,
  `coca-cola-guidelines.md` §1]. [HIGH that Zero Caffeine is current:
  Coca-Cola Japan's 2025-04-21 release names "コカ·コーラ ゼロカフェイン"
  in 350/500/700 mL PET (via search)]
- **Also negate the competitors and the alcohol lookalikes.** Pepsi is
  sold in Japan by **Suntory** (which has run Japan-only lines such as
  "Pepsi Japan Cola") [MEDIUM: Nikkei release summaries, ja.wikipedia
  (via search); current market share not found]. Add "not any other cola
  brand" and **"not a chūhai, highball, beer or lemon-sour can"**
  (hard rule 1).
- **Japan is a 350 mL-can market, not 330 mL.** The Japanese standard
  can is **350 mL, 122 mm tall × 66 mm diameter**, the same size across
  all makers under the JIS standard. Coca-Cola's own 350 mL can is
  listed at 122 × 66 × 66 mm. [HIGH: Monolog retailer spec sheet for
  Coca-Cola 350 mL (JAN 4902102000055), CTT Media's JIS-size explainer,
  Size-ya (via search)] **This contradicts the `coca-cola-guidelines.md`
  §4.3 non-US default (330 mL, 115.2 mm)**, which the brand file says to
  use "unless that market's own file states a different confirmed local
  standard". This file states one. The Japanese can is only 1 mm shorter
  than the US 355 mL can (123 mm), so **the US can dimensions are the
  near-correct anchor for Japan, and the 330 mL figure is not**. Logged
  in the GAP LOG. The brand file is not edited here.
- **Coca-Cola Japan also sells small cans**: **160 mL** (~92.8 mm tall)
  and **250 mL** (~101.7 mm tall). The caller's own search also found a
  **500 mL can**. [HIGH for 160 mL and 250 mL as current formats:
  Kakaku.com, Kakuyasu, Komeri, Amazon JP listings; MEDIUM for their
  heights: Size-ya, a tier-4 measurement site (via search); the 500 mL
  can was confirmed by the orchestrating session's search, not re-run
  here, and its dimensions were not found]
- **The brief always names the SKU, never the region or this file**
  (standing rule, 2026-09-27). If a brief names no product, ask for one.
  The register list below is reference for whoever writes the brief, not
  a default:
  - Teishoku shop, ramen counter, okonomiyaki teppan, sushi counter:
    the **190 mL returnable glass bottle** is the classic on-premise
    format, "for events and restaurants". Otherwise a 350 mL can.
    [HIGH that the 190 mL returnable bottle is current: Kakuyasu,
    Kakaku.com, Liquor Depot, Tajimaya listings (via search); "fits" is
    EDITORIAL]
  - On the go, konbini, office, vending machine: a **500 mL PET** (the
    everyday personal bottle), a **350 mL PET**, a **700 mL PET**, or a
    350 mL can [HIGH: Coca-Cola Japan media release 2025-04-21 lists
    350 mL, 500 mL and 700 mL PET for Coca-Cola, Zero and Zero Caffeine
    (via search)]
  - Home dinner for three or more, nabe night, hanami, Christmas: a
    **1.5 L PET** in the midground with one filled glass per place
    [HIGH: MonotaRO, Kakaku.com, Shimojima and Rakuten 1.5 L × 6 listings
    for Zero Sugar; Original 1.5 L not separately checked]. **Whether a
    2 L PET is current in Japan was not confirmed.**
  - Small-portion scenes (a single onigiri, a dango stick, a snack): the
    **160 mL or 250 mL can** keeps the product from towering over tiny
    Japanese vessels. [EDITORIAL]
- **Label-less bottles exist.** Coca-Cola and Coca-Cola Zero have been
  sold in **label-less PET** (recycled PET, logo embossed, no paper or
  film label), mainly in online multipacks, and a 500 mL label-less
  bottle was added on 1 May 2025 [HIGH: Coca-Cola Japan media centre
  2025-04-21 (via search)]. Stage it only if the brief names it. It
  reads as a clear contoured bottle of dark cola with no red band, so a
  model may draw it as an unbranded or competitor cola.
- **Vending machines.** Japan had **~3.91 million vending machines at
  end-2024, ~2.2 million of them for beverages**, down more than 20% in
  ten years [HIGH: JVMA figures reported by Nihon Shokuryo Shinbun,
  Anshin Zaidan and Memorva (via search)]. A drinks machine is a real
  hero-adjacent setting, but its front is a wall of product faces and
  prices. Blur it fully, and never let competitor products appear.
- **One hero product per scene** unless the brief asks for several.

### ICONIC BEVERAGES (documented context — staging rules follow)

This section records the real Japanese drinks landscape, including
alcohol, and restricts only what is staged. It follows the same design
as `south-africa.md`.

**TCCC Japan portfolio (partly verified).** Coca-Cola Japan says it runs
**about 40 brands and more than 600 products** in Japan. Besides the
global brands, it has Japan-developed brands: **Georgia** (canned and
bottled coffee), **Aquarius** (sports drink), **I LOHAS** (い･ろ･は･す,
natural water), **Ayataka** (綾鷹, green tea) and **Sokenbicha**
(爽健美茶, blended tea) [HIGH: Coca-Cola Japan customer FAQ 19715 and
brands page (via search)]. Fanta, Sprite, Minute Maid, Qoo, Real Gold
and Costa Coffee are also sold [MEDIUM — not independently re-checked
this pass].

**Non-alcoholic context (never beside the hero; staged only if a brief
allows a named companion):**

- **Green tea (ryokucha, sencha; bancha and hōjicha at everyday
  tables)**. Brewed in a small side-handled **kyūsu** teapot and served
  in a **yunomi** (a handleless cylindrical cup ~7–8 cm tall) or a small
  teacup; free at restaurants, especially sushi bars, after the meal
  [HIGH for free tea after meals: Otonanswer, MATCHA (via search); vessel
  sizes not re-checked]. **The most likely intruder in any home or
  Japanese-restaurant scene.**
- **Ohiya (cold water)**, brought unasked or self-served from a jug at
  ramen and teishoku shops, in a plain or faceted glass tumbler with ice
  [HIGH: Otonanswer, MATCHA, Visipri (via search)]. **The most likely
  intruder in any restaurant scene.**
- **Mugicha (roasted barley tea)**. Cold, amber, kept in a tall plastic
  or glass jug in the fridge all summer [MEDIUM — not independently
  re-checked this pass].
- **Canned coffee and PET green tea** from vending machines and konbini.
  They are the everyday "on the go" drinks competing with the hero
  [MEDIUM — not re-checked]. Several are TCCC brands (Georgia, Ayataka),
  but they still count as "other drinks" unless the brief names one.
- **Matcha**: whisked powdered tea in a wide tea bowl, part of the
  tea-ceremony register and trendy cafés. Never stage the hero within a
  tea-ceremony setting. [EDITORIAL]
- **Ramune**: a marble-stoppered glass soda, a festival icon (a
  competitor soft drink; never in frame). [MEDIUM — not re-checked]

### GENERAL NORMS

**Meal pattern — who eats at home, and when.**

- **Dinner is the main shared meal at home, and it starts around 19:00.**
  Asahi University's 2023 survey on evening meals found the start-time
  peak in the **19:00 hour**, with weekday times more spread out than
  weekend ones. **59.9% on weekdays and 79.2% on holidays** have started
  dinner by the end of the 19:00 hour. The Statistics Bureau's 2021
  Survey on Time Use publishes average dinner start times by work status.
  [HIGH: Asahi University Marketing Institute report and the 2021 Survey
  on Time Use (e-Stat table 00334), both via search]
- **Lunch is mostly away from home on weekdays**: a bento from home, a
  konbini meal, a teishoku or ramen shop, or a school lunch (kyūshoku).
  [MEDIUM — not independently re-checked this pass; no survey searched]
- **§5.2 contrast**: like Türkiye and the US, and unlike Spain, Mexico
  and Brazil, the main family meal is the evening one. **Japanese
  dinner (~19:00) is two to three hours earlier than Spain's.**

**Table norms:**

- **Ichijū-sansai (one soup, three dishes) is the template for a
  Japanese home meal**: rice, a soup, pickles, and three okazu (a main
  and two sides). **Placement, from the diner's side**: **rice
  front-left, soup front-right, main (e.g. grilled fish) back-right,
  side (e.g. simmered vegetables) back-left, a second small side in the
  middle; chopsticks horizontal across the front, tips pointing left,
  on a chopstick rest**. The rice-left rule is traced to the old
  "left is senior" (左上位) convention. [HIGH: MAFF Chūshikoku regional
  office leaflet "和の配膳", nippon.com guide, Educe Shokuiku, Pietro,
  Kobayashi Foods (via search)] Most weekday home dinners are looser
  than the full template: rice, soup, one main and one side is common,
  and shared main dishes on one large plate in the middle are also
  normal. [EDITORIAL — the looser practice is not surveyed]
- **Individual vessels, not one big plate.** Each person has their own
  rice bowl, soup bowl and chopsticks (at home often personally assigned
  ones), plus small plates and bowls for sides. A shared main may sit in
  the middle on a larger plate, with a **torizara** (small plate) at each
  place. [MEDIUM — not independently re-checked; uncontested]
- **Bowls are lifted to the mouth** for rice and soup (not staged in
  hand; but it means soup has **no spoon**). Miso soup is drunk from the
  bowl, with ingredients picked out by chopsticks. **Spoons appear only
  for curry rice, omurice, donburi in some shops, and a flat-bottomed
  renge (ceramic spoon) for ramen.** [MEDIUM — not re-checked,
  uncontested]
- **Mismatched is correct.** A Japanese home table deliberately mixes
  shapes, materials and glazes (lacquer, porcelain, stoneware, a square
  plate, a leaf-shaped dish). A matching Western dinner set looks wrong.
  [EDITORIAL; widely described]
- **Condiments on the table**: soy sauce in a small dispenser,
  shichimi (seven-spice) in a small tube or gourd, sometimes a tub of
  ground sesame; at a ramen shop, a caddy of pepper, chilli oil, vinegar
  and pickled ginger. [MEDIUM — not re-checked]
- **Oshibori** (a rolled wet hand towel) is provided at restaurants
  before the meal [HIGH: MATCHA, Otonanswer (via search)]. It is fine
  as a scene element, folded on its tray.
- **Low-table and floor seating** (a chabudai or kotatsu on tatami or
  a rug, sitting on cushions) is real in many homes and ryokan. In a
  modern apartment, a Western-height dining table with chairs is the
  common default; the kotatsu is a winter living-room scene.
  [MEDIUM — not re-checked; editorial on the default]

### VISUAL & PLATING NORMS

- **Space and restraint.** Japanese plating leaves a lot of vessel
  showing. A side dish sits as a small, neat mound in the centre of a
  small plate, often less than half its surface. Food is not pushed to
  the rim. Ramen and donburi are the exceptions: they are full to near
  the rim. [MEDIUM — widely described plating principle, not
  independently re-checked this pass]
- **Colour balance ("goshiki", five colours).** A table or a bento aims
  at red, yellow, green, black and white: tomato or pickled plum,
  omelette, greens, nori or seaweed, rice. Osechi states this rule
  explicitly (五彩: red, green, yellow, black, white) [HIGH: NHK Kyō no
  Ryōri and osechi guides via search]. [Generalising it to everyday
  bentos is MEDIUM]
- **Seasonality is visible.** Cherry-blossom motifs in spring, cool
  glass and bamboo in summer, maple leaves and chestnuts in autumn,
  hot pots in winter. A dish or vessel out of season reads wrong to a
  Japanese viewer. [MEDIUM — not re-checked; uncontested]
- **Fish lies head left, belly toward the diner** on its rectangular
  plate. [MEDIUM — not independently re-checked this pass]
- **Shredded cabbage** is the standard bed beside fried food (tonkatsu,
  karaage, korokke, ginger pork). It is a fine, fluffy heap of very
  thin raw cabbage threads, with a wedge of tomato or lemon.
  [MEDIUM-HIGH: tonkatsu and karaage sources; the Daily Portal Z essay on
  cabbage shreds (via search)]
- **Steam and gloss** carry freshness: steam from rice and soup, a sheen
  of tare, bonito flakes moving on hot okonomiyaki. [EDITORIAL]
- **Grade neutrally.** Avoid a heavy teal-and-orange "Tokyo night"
  filter or a sepia "old Japan" filter. Home scenes are lit by cool
  white LED ceiling light or window daylight. Shops are warm and
  wood-toned. Konbini are harsh white. [EDITORIAL]

### ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES

#### General environmental norms

- **Japan is split between houses and apartments, and the big cities
  are apartment cities.** In the 2023 Housing and Land Survey, occupied
  dwellings were **52.7% detached houses (一戸建, 29.32 million) and
  44.9% multi-unit buildings (共同住宅, 24.97 million)**, as of
  1 October 2023. **Tokyo has the highest multi-unit share of any
  prefecture, at 71.6%.** [HIGH: Statistics Bureau results summary and
  basic tabulation PDF (via search)]
  [SOURCE: [総務省統計局 — 令和5年住宅・土地統計調査 調査の結果](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/jyutaku/2023/tyousake.html);
  [基本集計 結果の概要 (PDF)](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/jyutaku/2023/pdf/kihon_gaiyou.pdf)]
- So: **a Tokyo or Osaka scene defaults to an apartment** (a mansion,
  meaning a concrete condo, or an apāto, a smaller two-storey rental
  block). **A scene in a regional city, suburb or countryside
  defaults to a detached house.** [MEDIUM inference from the HIGH
  figures]
- **§5.2 contrast**: Japan's split sits between Mexico (~73% single
  houses) and Spain or Türkiye (mostly flats). Tokyo alone resembles
  the Spanish pattern.
- **Interior markers (pick one or two per scene)** [MEDIUM — not
  individually re-checked; uncontested]:
  - A compact **LDK** (living-dining-kitchen) with a small four-seat
    dining table, pale wood or white surfaces, a rice cooker on the
    counter, a narrow kitchen with a two-burner hob and a fish grill
    drawer.
  - Sliding doors; a **tatami room** (washitsu) beside the living room
    in many houses; a **kotatsu** (low heated table with a quilt) in
    winter.
  - The **genkan**: a sunken entrance step where shoes come off, and
    slippers.
  - Tidy, uncluttered surfaces, with many small useful objects.
  - Laundry drying on the balcony pole; a small balcony with an air-con
    unit.
- **Exterior markers**: dense two- and three-storey houses with small
  gaps between them, tiled or metal roofs, overhead power lines on
  concrete poles, vending machines at street corners, bicycles, narrow
  lanes without pavements, clean streets [MEDIUM — not re-checked].
  Shrines, temples, torii gates and Mount Fuji may appear as distant
  context in a wide shot, **never framed as a backdrop for the hero**
  [EDITORIAL].
- **Konbini density**: **56,659 convenience stores in 2025** (JFA
  statistics as reported), so a konbini is plausibly a few minutes'
  walk from any urban scene [HIGH: JFA statistics page and Nippon Soft /
  Yahoo! News expert analysis (via search)].
- **Gen Z lens (§5.3)**: young adults commonly live alone in small
  studio apartments (1R/1K, a single room with a kitchen strip in the
  corridor) or in share-houses in Tokyo and Osaka; many eat konbini or
  delivery meals at a low table in their room. [LOW-MEDIUM — not
  independently re-checked] Stage a small, bright one-room flat with a
  low table, a cushion, a laptop, plants, and a konbini bento or ramen
  on the table. It should be neither a chaotic otaku den nor a staged
  showroom.
- **Caricature avoidance [EDITORIAL]**:
  - **Postcard Japan**: Mount Fuji, torii gates, geisha, cherry blossoms
    in every season, pagodas and red lanterns behind every meal.
  - **Neon-cyberpunk Tokyo**: rain-soaked neon alleys, vending-machine

  - **Hyper-traditional Japan**: every meal on tatami in a ryokan with

500 mL PET beside each plate. No teapot, no barley-tea jug. [EDITORIAL]

#### Scenario: Casual lunch at home — 3 people

A weekend family lunch: **okonomiyaki or takoyaki cooked at the table**
(a home electric hot plate or takoyaki plate is a very common family
activity, especially in Kansai [MEDIUM — not re-checked]), or a platter
of **onigiri and karaage** with a salad, or **temaki-zushi** (hand-roll
sushi assembled at the table from nori, rice and fillings; see the Sushi
entry). Hero (from the brief; formats that fit): a 1.5 L PET in the
midground with a glass at each place. [EDITORIAL]

#### Scenario: Dinner at home, indoors

**The main family meal**, ~19:00 [HIGH for time]. Two registers:

- **The everyday ichijū-sansai table**: for each person, rice
  front-left, miso soup front-right, and a main back-right (ginger pork
  with cabbage, grilled salmon, hamburg steak, karaage); a shared plate
  of nikujaga or a salad in the middle, with small plates; chopsticks on
  rests. Ceiling LED light, a dark window. Hero (from the brief; formats
  that fit): a can or a 500 mL PET at each adult's place, or a 1.5 L PET
  in the midground with glasses. [EDITORIAL; layout HIGH]
- **Winter nabe night**: a clay pot on a portable gas burner in the
  centre, steam rising, a platter of raw vegetables and thinly sliced
  meat beside it, small bowls at each place. A strongly seasonal
  (November–February) and very Japanese register [MEDIUM — not
  re-checked]. Keep the gas canister's printed label out of view.

#### Scenario: Meal outdoors at home

Japan's housing makes the home garden barbecue less common than in the
US, Brazil or Argentina. The real outdoor-at-home registers are
[EDITORIAL; not surveyed]:

- **Engawa or balcony in summer**: a narrow wooden veranda of a house
  (or an apartment balcony) at dusk, with a plate of watermelon
  wedges, edamame (not in a drinking register), grilled corn; a small
  fan; wind chimes; mosquito coil smoke (optional). Hero: a can, which
  could be a 160 mL or 250 mL can for a snack scene.
- **Garden or riverside BBQ (baabekyuu)**: a real weekend activity at
  campsites, rivers and some houses: a small grill with yakiniku-style
  thin meat slices, vegetables, yakisoba cooked on a griddle plate at
  the end. Folding table, camping chairs. Hero: 1.5 L PET with plastic
  cups, or cans. **Avoid the beer cooler cue.**
- **Hanami** (see FESTIVALS) is the great outdoor meal, but it happens
  in parks, not at home.

#### Scenario: Meal on the go — 1 person

The **konbini** is the centre of Japanese on-the-go eating: an onigiri,
a sandwich, a bento heated at the till, fried chicken from the hot case.
It is eaten at the store's window-facing eat-in counter, on a park
bench, or at an office desk. Eating while walking is widely considered
bad manners, so food is eaten standing at or near the stall, or seated.
[MEDIUM for the walking-eating norm — not independently re-checked;
konbini count HIGH]. Other registers: **a takoyaki tray on a stall's
standing ledge** (zone 2), **an ekiben** (station bento) on a bullet-
train fold-down tray with the window blurred, **a standing soba counter**
(tachigui soba) at a station. Hero (from the brief; formats that fit): a
500 mL PET or a 350 mL can. **§5.2 contrast**: unlike Mexico or
Türkiye, the street itself is not where Japanese people eat day to day.
The konbini counter, the station counter and the bench are.

#### Scenario: Away from home — 1 person at a restaurant/café

- **A teishoku shop at 12:15**: one tray on a wooden counter (ginger
  pork, grilled mackerel or karaage set). Or a **ramen counter** with one
  bowl on the raised ledge, steam, the kitchen blurred behind. Or a
  **gyūdon counter** with a bowl and a small miso soup. Hero: a 190 mL
  glass bottle or a 350 mL can. **Exclude the water glass and the
  self-serve jug** (hard rule 3). [EDITORIAL; free-water HIGH]
- **Solo dining is fully normal** in Japan: counter seating, single-
  booth ramen shops. A person alone is not a sad scene. [MEDIUM — not
  re-checked; uncontested]

#### Scenario: Away from home — 2–3 people

An **okonomiyaki teppan table** with two cakes cooking on the built-in
griddle and small spatulas (zone 2 or 4); a **family restaurant**
(famiresu) booth with hamburg steak and curry plates; a **conveyor-belt
sushi** booth with a stack of small plates; a **yakiniku** table grill
at lunch; a **café** with omurice and a parfait. Hero: two small glass
bottles or cans, or 500 mL PET. **Never an izakaya table** (hard rule 2).
[EDITORIAL]

## CROSS-CUTTING REGISTER: STREET FOOD, KONBINI & COUNTERS

- **Konbini**: onigiri, sandwiches, bento, cup noodles, and the hot
  case by the till with fried chicken pieces, karaage skewers, oden
  (in winter), steamed buns (nikuman). A konbini fried-chicken piece is
  a boneless, flat-ish, crumbed piece in a paper sleeve. [MEDIUM — not
  re-checked] **Branding and shelf text are everywhere, so blur fully and
  never name a chain.**
- **Yatai (stalls)**: at festivals nationwide, and as a permanent
  night-time institution on Fukuoka's riverbanks (Nakasu). Fukuoka yatai
  serve ramen, oden and yakitori with a strong drinking register
  [MEDIUM — not re-checked]. **Stage Fukuoka yatai for ramen only, at
  early evening, with no glasses on the counter.**
- **Takoyaki and taiyaki stands**: standing ledges or a bench; food on
  its tray, never in hand.
- **Station eating**: tachigui soba and udon counters, ekiben shops.
- **Department-store food halls (depachika)**: beautifully packaged
  bento and sweets. Packaging text is dense.
- **Staging**: food on a ledge, bench, counter or tray, never in hand.

## CELEBRATIONS & LARGE GATHERINGS

Per `country-file-schema.md` §5.7: the frame shows the operator's party
(1, 2 or a small group of identical place settings) at one stretch of a
bigger event, and the crowd is implied. Every Japan hard rule applies:
no alcohol (Lemon-Dou included, and no kanpai toast), no izakaya
register, no water glass, yunomi or barley-tea jug, chopsticks on a
rest and never upright, and nothing on or beside a butsudan, kamidana or
Obon altar.

### How large gatherings work here

- **Who and how many.** Japanese celebrations are mostly **small and
  family-centred**; the large crowd is the exception (weddings, hanami,
  summer matsuri). New Year osechi is overwhelmingly eaten with the
  household: consumer surveys put osechi eaten "with family" at roughly
  nine in ten (88.2% in one 2025 survey), and one 2024 survey found only
  14.8% eat it with relatives [MEDIUM — commercepick and PR Times survey
  releases via search, commercial sources]. Obon is the main time relatives
  gather at the family home; one widely shared 2023 news story described
  a mother cooking for **16 relatives** [LOW as a norm — one Yahoo!
  News/Netorabo anecdote]. Weddings average **52.0 invited guests**
  (Zexy Marriage Trend Survey 2024, up 2.9 on the year) [MEDIUM —
  Recruit Bridal Research Institute report via search].
- **Where (intake venues).** *Home indoor*: New Year, Obon, Hinamatsuri,
  birthdays, Christmas Eve, at the dining table or a low table in the
  tatami room with zabuton cushions. *Restaurant*: a private room (個室)
  at a Japanese restaurant, hotel or family restaurant for Shichi-Go-San
  and other family milestones [MEDIUM — Studio Mario, HANKYU FOOD,
  Kosodate Hack via search]. *Other*: a wedding venue or hotel banquet
  room; a park under cherry trees for hanami (a blue tarp, see the
  festivals register for park rules).
- **Table form and serving style.** At home the everyday rule of
  individual bowls holds, with **large shared platters** added in the
  centre: a **sushi-oke** (round lacquered tub of delivered nigiri),
  an **ōdoburu** party platter, a tempura platter, a chirashi-zushi tub.
  Large Obon or New Year gatherings push two low tables together in the
  washitsu. Weddings use **round tables of 6–8 with plated courses**
  served by staff (usually French or Franco-Japanese) [EDITORIAL on the
  table size; course format MEDIUM, uncontested].
- **Plates and cutlery that differ from everyday.** Celebration tables
  bring out lacquerware (jūbako, red-and-black lacquer trays, nuri-bashi
  lacquered chopsticks), **iwai-bashi** (round, tapered at both ends,
  in a paper sleeve, for New Year) and small plates (torizara) for the
  shared platters. Weddings: Western cutlery laid outside-in, with
  chopsticks often added. Hanami: paper plates, disposable chopsticks.
- **Snapshot-staging default for Japan [EDITORIAL].** The most authentic
  crowd cues: (1) **a shared centrepiece too big for the visible diners**
  (a sushi-oke for five, an open three-tier jūbako, a tempura platter)
  partly cropped; (2) **the low table extending out of frame** in a
  tatami room with extra zabuton cushions at the edge; (3) **seasonal
  decor** in soft focus (a kagami-mochi on a shelf at New Year, hina
  dolls on their tiers in March, cherry branches overhead at hanami).
  Blurred relatives at the far end (no more than about 2.5 faces) are a
  fourth cue. Keep it calm and tidy: Japanese celebrations are not
  boisterous party scenes.

#### Celebration: New Year family meal (正月, osechi)

- Type: calendar holiday
- When: 1–3 January. Osechi is eaten across the holiday; stage the
  **midday** meal (1 or 2 January, when relatives visit), not the
  morning ozōni.
- Gathering: the household plus grandparents, 3–8, at the parents' home;
  *home indoor*, often at a low table in the tatami room [MEDIUM — about
  nine in ten eat osechi with family per consumer surveys; headcount
  EDITORIAL]. Most
  households buy osechi ready-made or partly so (83.8% "buy" or "buy and
  make" in a 2025 survey) [MEDIUM — commercepick survey via search,
  commercial source].
- The spread: see catalog: **Osechi (New Year) and ozōni** (tiered
  jūbako, five colours, iwai-zakana). Around it: a plate of sashimi or a
  sushi-oke, simmered nishime, red-and-white kamaboko, and a small bowl
  of ozōni per person. 1 jūbako (2–3 tiers opened side by side) plus 2–4
  other dishes.
- Snapshot staging: **1 setting**: a lacquer tray with iwai-bashi in
  their sleeve (text blurred) on a rest, a small plate with a few osechi
  pieces (datemaki slice, kuromame, kamaboko), an ozōni bowl; the open
  jūbako tiers cropped behind; hero beside the tray. **2 settings**: two
  identical settings at one long side of a low table, the open tiers
  between them, a sashimi plate at the edge. **Small group**: the low
  table running out of frame, three tiers opened side by side, a
  multi-serve bottle in the midground if the brief allows. Crowd cues:
  extra zabuton cushions; a kagami-mochi on a shelf soft in the
  background; winter light through shōji.
- Decor and cues: pine-and-bamboo kadomatsu (outside only), nanten
  berries, a small kagami-mochi on a shelf (not an altar). Clichés:
  kimono on everyone, Mount Fuji through the window.
- Never stage: toso or sake (the New Year spiced sake is a strong
  prior), the kamidana, shrine visits; legible text on chopstick sleeves.
- Confidence and sources: [HIGH] osechi form (catalog sources); [MEDIUM]
  family share and purchase share; [EDITORIAL] staging.

#### Celebration: Obon relatives' gathering (お盆)

- Type: calendar holiday (ancestral, but the meal is a family reunion)
- When: 13–16 August (July in parts of Tokyo; lunar in Okinawa); the
  shared meal is **midday** or **evening**.
- Gathering: relatives return to the family home, typically 6–16 [LOW —
  one news anecdote of 16; EDITORIAL range]; *home indoor*, two low
  tables pushed together in the tatami room, or the dining table plus a
  side table.
- The spread: big shared platters: a **sushi-oke** or hand-made
  **temari-zushi** and **chirashi-zushi** (see catalog: Sushi), a
  **vegetable tempura** platter (an Obon custom especially in Shinshū and
  Tōhoku), **sōmen** on ice, an **ōdoburu** of karaage and side dishes
  (see catalog: Karaage), watermelon after [MEDIUM — Delish Kitchen,
  Ninben, Jackery Obon food guides via search, tier 3–4, consistent].
  4–6 shared dishes.
- Snapshot staging: **1 setting**: a small plate with two nigiri and a
  piece of tempura, chopsticks on a rest, a dipping-sauce dish; the
  sushi-oke rim and the tempura platter cropped behind. **2 settings**:
  two settings, the sushi-oke between them, a glass bowl of sōmen on
  ice at the edge. **Small group**: the joined low tables running out of
  frame, platters crowding the centre. Crowd cues: extra torizara
  stacked; a summer fan and an open shōji to a garden; children blurred
  on the engawa.
- Decor and cues: summer light, a wind chime, an electric fan. Clichés:
  lantern-lit cemeteries, bon-odori in the room.
- Never stage: the bon-dana altar, the cucumber horse and aubergine ox,
  incense, butsudan; beer (the dominant prior at a relatives' table).
- Confidence and sources: [MEDIUM] food; [LOW] headcount.

#### Celebration: Hinamatsuri family dinner (ひな祭り)

- Type: calendar holiday (Girls' Day, 3 March)
- When: 3 March, **evening** family dinner.
- Gathering: the household and grandparents, 3–6, at home; *home
  indoor* [EDITORIAL].
- The spread: **chirashi-zushi** (vinegared rice in a wide tub or
  bowl, topped with pink shrimp, salmon roe, shredded egg ribbons, snow
  peas or rapeseed flowers), **hamaguri clear soup** (one pair of clam
  shells per bowl, for a good marriage), **hina-arare** (pink, white and
  green puffed rice), **hishimochi** (three-colour diamond mochi) and
  sakura-mochi; a strawberry cake is now common [HIGH — Benesse, Kyoto
  Culinary Art College, MATCHA, HANKYU FOOD via search, consistent].
  The chirashi tub plus 2–3 other dishes.
- Snapshot staging: **1 setting**: a small bowl of chirashi served from
  the tub, a lacquer bowl of clam soup with the open shells visible,
  chopsticks on a rest; the chirashi tub cropped behind. **2 settings**:
  two settings, the chirashi tub between them, a dish of hina-arare at
  the edge. **Small group**: the dining table with the tub, a plate of
  sakura-mochi and a cake running out of frame. Crowd cues: the hina
  doll tiers soft and far in the background (never the product beside
  them); peach blossom in a vase.
- Never stage: amazake or shirozake beside the hero (shirozake is
  alcoholic; amazake is a non-hero drink); the dolls as a backdrop for
  the product.
- Confidence and sources: [HIGH] food; [EDITORIAL] staging. Chirashi-zushi
  has no catalog entry (see CANDIDATE QUEUE).

#### Celebration: Hanami party (花見)

- Type: community or family gathering
- When: late March to early May depending on the region (see festivals
  register); **midday** or **golden-hour**.
- Gathering: friends, colleagues or families, 4–15 on blue tarps under
  cherry trees in a public park; many neighbouring groups around; *other*
  (park) [MEDIUM — park-rule sources in the festivals register; group
  size EDITORIAL].
- The spread: see catalog: **Hanami bento (pointer)** and **Bento**

  konbini snacks decanted, and sanshoku dango (see catalog: Dango).
- Snapshot staging: **1 setting**: one paper plate with an onigiri and two
  karaage on the tarp, the open jūbako tiers cropped beside it, the hero
  on the tarp; petals scattered. **2 settings**: two paper plates side by
  side, the jūbako tiers between them, a dango stick on a plate. **Small
  group**: a corner of the tarp with 3–4 plates and the tiers, the tarp
  running out of frame. Crowd cues: another group's tarp soft in the
  background; cherry branches overhead; shoes lined up at the tarp edge.
- Never stage: beer or chūhai cans (the strongest prior of all, and
  Lemon-Dou is a TCCC product); grills, folding tables and tents in a
  Tokyo park; drunken crowds; legible konbini packaging.
- Confidence and sources: [MEDIUM]; [EDITORIAL] staging.

#### Celebration: Shichi-Go-San family meal (七五三)

- Type: life event (children aged 3, 5 and 7, around 15 November)
- When: after the shrine visit and studio photos, a **midday** lunch.
- Gathering: parents, the child and grandparents, 4–8, in a restaurant
  private room, a hotel, a family restaurant or at home [MEDIUM — Studio
  Mario, HANKYU FOOD, Kosodate Hack via search: private rooms

- The spread: a celebration set (祝い膳) with **sekihan** (red-bean rice,
  pinkish-red with dark beans), a **whole grilled sea bream** (tai, pink
  skin, salt-grilled), sashimi, tempura, a children's plate, and
  **chitose-ame** (long red-and-white candy sticks in a printed bag)
  [MEDIUM — same sources; Studio Alice, Delish Kitchen]. At home, the
  same with a sushi-oke and a cake.
- Snapshot staging: **1 setting**: a lacquer tray set with a small bowl of
  sekihan, sashimi, a tempura piece, a soup bowl; a whole sea bream on a
  long plate cropped in the centre. **2 settings**: two trays, the sea
  bream between them. **Small group**: the private-room table with trays
  and a shared sea bream, extending out of frame. Crowd cues: the
  child's chitose-ame bag leaning at the frame edge (text blurred or
  bag turned away); a folded kimono sleeve on a blurred figure; a
  photo-studio folder set aside.
- Never stage: the shrine, the purification rite; the child as a
  costume tableau; legible bag text; sake for the adults.
- Confidence and sources: [MEDIUM] food and venue (photo-studio and

  [EDITORIAL] staging.

#### Celebration: Wedding reception (披露宴)

- Type: life event
- When: the reception follows the ceremony; **midday** or **evening**.
- Gathering: an average of **52 invited guests** (2024) at a wedding

### A. The home table and the set meal

#### Ichijū-sansai home meal (rice, miso soup, grilled fish, sides)

- **Category**: Everyday: home dinner (also breakfast in the Morning
  Module; see that entry for the lighter form).
- **Lineage**: Native Japanese; the structural template of washoku.
- **Variants (§4.6)**: the main changes by day: **grilled salted salmon
  (shiozake)** or **mackerel (saba shioyaki)**, ginger pork, hamburg
  steak, simmered fish (nizakana). The sides: **nimono** (simmered
  vegetables, e.g. hijiki, kabocha or chikuzenni), **ohitashi** (blanched
  spinach with bonito flakes), **hiyayakko** (a cold tofu block),
  **tamagoyaki**. **Default when unspecified**: grilled salmon, kabocha
  nimono, spinach ohitashi, pickles. [EDITORIAL]
- **Format (§4.4)**: one person's set of separate vessels on the table
  or a tray.
- **Vessel & scale**: rice bowl 11–12 cm front-left; lacquer soup bowl
  11.4–12 cm front-right; fish on a rectangular plate ~22 × 11 cm
  back-right; a small deep bowl ~10–12 cm back-left; pickles on a
  mamezara ~9 cm in the centre; chopsticks on a hashioki across the
  front. [HIGH for layout (MAFF, nippon.com) and bowl sizes; plate sizes
  MEDIUM — not re-checked]
- **Texture & finish**: see TEXTURE LEXICON (rice, miso soup, grilled
  fish, pickles). Salmon: orange-pink flesh with pale fat lines, salted
  skin crisped silver-brown at the edges, a small grated-daikon mound
  beside it.
- **Model failure**: one big plate with everything on it; a Chinese
  family-style spread; soup with a Western spoon; oversized bowls;
  chopsticks in the rice; a bowl of plain white rice with a fried egg
  on top as "Japanese".
- **Confidence**: HIGH for layout and vessel sizes; MEDIUM for dish
  choices.
- **Sources**: [MAFF 中四国農政局 — 和の配膳](https://www.maff.go.jp/chushi/syokuiku/katudou/attach/pdf/26_jireishuu-23.pdf);
  [nippon.com — 一汁三菜の配膳](https://www.nippon.com/ja/guide-to-japan/gu020011/);
  [Tōjian — サイズ表記](https://www.tojian.com/size.html);
  [Zenroku — 汁椀の選び方](https://zenroku.jp/column/choice-of-owan/).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: one person's setting.
  - **What dominates**: **the vessels, not the food.** Five small
    separate vessels occupy a ~40 × 30 cm area; visible food surface is
    roughly rice ~30%, soup ~25%, fish ~25%, sides and pickles ~20%.
    Between the vessels, ~40% of the table or tray shows. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count (per person) | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Rice | ~150 g in an 11–12 cm bowl, mound ~2 cm above the rim | 1 bowl | Glossy white, translucent grains, soft dome, faint steam | Front-left |
    | Miso soup | ~150–180 mL in an 11.5–12 cm lacquer bowl, filled to ~1.5 cm below rim | 1 bowl | Cloudy tan broth; 6–10 tofu cubes ~1 cm; a few wakame pieces; green onion rings ~3 mm | Front-right |
    | Grilled salmon | One fillet slice ~8–10 × 4–5 cm, ~2 cm thick | 1 | Orange-pink, crisp silver-brown skin edge | Back-right, on a rectangular plate, skin up, with a ~3 cm grated-daikon mound (a few drops of soy) at the front right |
    | Kabocha nimono | Wedges ~4 × 3 cm | 3–4 | Deep green skin, bright orange flesh, matte, glazed in thin amber broth | Back-left, stacked in the small bowl |
    | Spinach ohitashi | A pressed log ~5 cm long, cut in 2–3 | 1 small mound | Dark green, glossy, a pinch of bonito flakes on top | Centre-back or centre |
    | Pickles | Slices ~3 × 2 cm | 3–5 pieces | Yellow daikon, purple shibazuke, green cucumber | Centre front on a ~9 cm dish |
    | Chopsticks | ~21–23 cm | 1 pair | Lacquered wood | Across the front on a hashioki, tips left |

  - **Arrangement**: the triangle of rice (front-left), soup
    (front-right) and main (back-right) is the tell; everything is
    separate, with a finger's width or more between vessels.
  - **Vessel fill**: each small vessel about half covered by its food;
    rice and soup bowls nearly full.
  - **Served portion vs. whole**: this *is* the portion; nothing shared
    except perhaps a central plate.
  - **State cues**: steam from rice and soup; soup settled into slow
    clouds; the fish just off the grill, skin crackled.
  - **Absent on purpose**: a large dinner plate, a knife and fork, a
    soup spoon, chopsticks standing in rice, kimchi or banchan, a teapot
    and tea cups, a glass of water, sushi.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A Japanese home dinner setting seen from
    slightly above: at front-left a small patterned bowl of glossy
    short-grain rice, as wide as the can is tall and half its height;
    at front-right a matching-size red-brown lacquer bowl of cloudy miso
    soup with tiny tofu cubes; behind it a small rectangular plate with
    one grilled salmon slice and a little grated radish; a small bowl
    of orange pumpkin wedges; a few pickle slices; chopsticks lying
    across the front on a rest, tips left."

#### Teishoku (the set meal at a canteen) — shōgayaki default

- **Category**: Everyday: weekday lunch out; also dinner.
- **Lineage**: Native Japanese (the shop form of ichijū-sansai).
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **shōgayaki teishoku** (thin pork slices in
  soy-ginger sauce), **saba misoni** (mackerel in miso), **saba
  shioyaki**, **karaage teishoku**, **tonkatsu teishoku** (own entry),
  **hamburg teishoku**, **gyūtan teishoku** (zone 8, own entry).
  **Default when unspecified**: shōgayaki teishoku. [EDITORIAL; the
  variants are uncontested, not re-checked]
- **Form**: a rectangular tray holding a main plate (meat plus a
  fluffy heap of shredded cabbage, a tomato wedge, sometimes a dab of
  potato or macaroni salad), a rice bowl (often refillable), miso soup,
  a small kobachi side and pickles. [MEDIUM — not independently
  re-checked; uncontested]
- **Vessel & scale**: tray ~36–40 × 27–30 cm (LOW); main plate
  ~22–24 cm round or oval; rice bowl 12 cm; soup bowl 12 cm.
- **Texture & finish**: pork slices ~2–3 mm thin, curled and folded,
  glossy with a thick brown ginger-soy glaze, with caramelised edges
  and translucent onion slivers tangled through; cabbage pale green and
  airy.
- **Model failure**: a thick pork chop; Chinese stir-fry with
  vegetables mixed in; a bento box instead of a tray.
- **Confidence**: MEDIUM.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: one shōgayaki set on a tray.
  - **What dominates**: on the main plate, **cabbage shreds ~45%,
    pork ~45%, tomato/salad ~10%**; across the tray, the main plate
    takes about half the area and the rice and soup bowls a third.
    [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Pork slices | ~10 × 6 cm, 2–3 mm thick; ~120–150 g total | 5–7 slices, overlapping | Brown-glazed, curled edges, onion slivers between | Right half of the main plate, leaning against the cabbage |
    | Shredded cabbage | Threads ~1 mm; a heap ~8 cm tall | 1 heap (~50 g) | Pale green-white, airy, fluffy | Back-left of the main plate |
    | Tomato wedge | ~4 cm | 1–2 | Red, glossy | Front of the cabbage |
    | Mayo potato or macaroni salad (optional) | a ~4 cm scoop | 1 | Pale cream | Beside the tomato |
    | Rice | ~200 g in a 12 cm bowl, mounded | 1 | Glossy white | Tray front-left |
    | Miso soup | 12 cm lacquer bowl | 1 | Cloudy tan | Tray front-right |
    | Kobachi side | ~10 cm bowl, half-full | 1 | e.g. hijiki (black seaweed with carrot and soy pieces) or cold tofu | Tray back-left |
    | Pickles | 2–3 pieces | 1 small dish | Yellow and green | Between |

  - **Arrangement**: tray square to the counter edge; main plate at the
    back-right, bowls in front; chopsticks across the front in a paper
    sleeve or on a rest.
  - **Vessel fill**: main plate ~70% covered; rice mounded; soup near
    full.
  - **State cues**: glaze glistening; steam; cabbage crisp, not wilted.
  - **Absent on purpose**: a water glass, a teacup, a ticket or receipt,
    menu text, a knife.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "On a wooden tray on a canteen counter: a
    round plate with a fluffy heap of pale shredded cabbage and
    overlapping thin pork slices, curled and glossy with brown
    ginger-soy glaze and onion; a tomato wedge; in front, a rice bowl as
    wide as the can is tall and a lacquer bowl of cloudy miso soup; a
    tiny dish of black seaweed salad and yellow pickles; chopsticks
    across the front."

#### Karaage (Japanese fried chicken)

- **Category**: Everyday: home dinner, bento, teishoku, konbini hot
  case, festival stalls.
- **Lineage**: Native Japanese (marinated, starch-dusted, deep-fried
  thigh pieces). Not a Korean glazed fried chicken, not a US breaded
  tender.
- **Variants (§4.6)**: soy-garlic-ginger marinated (default); shio
  (salt) karaage; **Nakatsu karaage** (Ōita, zone 5; not re-checked);
  **zangi** (Hokkaidō, zone 7; not re-checked); **tebasaki** (Nagoya
  wings; see zone 3). **Default when unspecified**: soy-marinated thigh
  karaage with lemon and cabbage. [EDITORIAL]
- **Vessel & scale**: pieces **~25–40 g, ~4–5 cm**, irregular; 5
  pieces ≈ 200 g for a set-meal portion; at home, a pile on a shared
  plate; in a bento, 2–3 pieces. [HIGH for weight: Eiyō-keisan; Akizō
  bento-chain measurement at ~31 g average; chain standard ~40 g]
- **Texture & finish**: see TEXTURE LEXICON. Irregular lumps (the
  thigh piece folds on itself), a craggy deep golden-brown to
  mahogany crust with small crisp bubbles and patches of pale starch
  dust, juicy.
- **Model failure**: uniform nuggets; a thick breaded batter; red glaze
  and sesame (Korean); bones.
- **Confidence**: HIGH for size; MEDIUM for variants.
- **Sources**: [Eiyō-keisan — 唐揚げ](https://www.eiyoukeisan.com/calorie/gramphoto/zryouri/karaage.html);
  [Akizō — から揚の大きさ](https://www.akizou.com/hm-karaage).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: a home or set-meal plate of
  five pieces.
  - **What dominates**: **karaage ~55%** of the plate's covered area,
    cabbage ~35%, lemon/tomato/mayo ~10%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Karaage | 4–5 cm irregular lumps, 25–40 g | 5 (set) / 2–3 (bento) / 8–12 (shared home plate) | Craggy golden-brown crust, a few pale starch patches, shiny with oil | Heaped in a loose pyramid, front-right of the plate |
    | Shredded cabbage | 1 mm threads, heap ~7 cm tall | 1 heap | Pale green, airy | Behind the chicken |
    | Lemon | Wedge ~5 cm | 1 | Bright yellow | Leaning on the pile |
    | Japanese mayonnaise (optional) | A ~3 cm dollop | 1 | Pale yellow-cream, soft peak | Beside the cabbage |

  - **Arrangement**: pieces piled, not lined up; the crust facets catch
    the light.
  - **Vessel fill**: a 22–24 cm plate about two-thirds covered.
  - **State cues**: just fried, a faint oil sheen, a wisp of steam; the
    crust dry-crisp, not wet.
  - **Absent on purpose**: dipping sauce bowls, sesame seeds, red glaze,
    bones, a breaded smooth coat, a beer glass.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "Five irregular pieces of Japanese fried
    chicken, each a little smaller than the can's width, piled
    loosely on a white plate: craggy deep golden-brown crust with small
    crisp ridges and a dusting of pale starch, faint oil sheen, steam;
    behind them a fluffy heap of finely shredded pale cabbage; a lemon
    wedge leaning on the pile. No sauce, no sesame, no bones."

#### Tonkatsu (with the Nagoya miso-katsu variant)

- **Category**: Everyday to treat: tonkatsu specialist shop, teishoku,
  home, bento, katsu-sando, katsudon, katsu curry.
- **Lineage**: Yōshoku (Western-derived Japanese, Meiji era onward).
  Not a Wiener schnitzel: thick, not pounded thin, and panko-coated.
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **rōsu** (loin, with a fat rim; default) vs.
  **hire** (fillet, lean, cut in cylinders); **miso katsu** (zone 3,
  Nagoya: the cutlet under a dark, sweet red-miso sauce, often with
  sesame) [HIGH that miso katsu is a Nagoya-meshi icon: Nagoya
  Concierge, Jalan, Tripto (via search)]; katsudon (simmered with egg
  and onion on rice, in a lidded donburi; not detailed here); katsu
  curry. **Default when unspecified**: rōsu katsu teishoku with sauce.
- **Vessel & scale**: loin **~150 g, 1.5–2 cm thick** [MEDIUM-HIGH:
  Kikkoman recipe; chain menus list 150 g], a cutlet ~12–14 cm long and
  ~8 cm wide, **cut across into ~1.5 cm strips** (about 6–8 strips) and
  reassembled on a wire rack or plate with a big cabbage heap; a thick
  brown sauce in a pot for the diner to pour; yellow karashi (mustard)
  dab; ground sesame in a ridged mortar bowl at specialist shops
  [MEDIUM].
- **Texture & finish**: see TEXTURE LEXICON: coarse, spiky pale-gold
  panko, the cut faces showing juicy white-to-faint-pink meat and a
  thin white fat rim; crust lifting away very slightly from the meat.
- **Model failure**: pounded thin schnitzel; fine dark breadcrumb;
  sauce poured all over; served whole and uncut.
- **Confidence**: MEDIUM-HIGH.
- **Sources**: [Kikkoman — とんかつ](https://www.kikkoman.co.jp/homecook/search/recipe/00055169/);
  [Nagoya Concierge — グルメ](https://www.nagoya-info.jp/gourmet/?s_genre%5B%5D=35).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: one rōsu katsu on its plate
  (the rest of the set as in Teishoku).
  - **What dominates**: **the cut cutlet ~50%** of the plate,
    **cabbage ~40%**, garnishes ~10%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Cutlet strips | ~8 × 1.5 cm faces, 1.5–2 cm thick | 6–8 strips in a row, reassembled | Spiky pale-gold panko; cut faces pale pink-white with a white fat rim at one end | Front of the plate, strips slightly fanned, cut faces toward camera |
    | Shredded cabbage | 1 mm threads, heap ~10 cm tall | 1 large heap (~70 g) | Pale green-white, airy | Behind the cutlet |
    | Karashi (mustard) | A ~1.5 cm dab | 1 | Bright yellow, matte | Plate edge |
    | Lemon / tomato | wedge ~4–5 cm | 1 | Yellow / red | At the cabbage |
    | Tonkatsu sauce | In a small pot or brushed thinly on 2–3 strips | — | Dark brown, glossy | Pot beside; never flooding the plate |

  - **Miso-katsu variant**: the same strips on the cabbage, but covered
    in a **thick, dark reddish-brown miso sauce** poured in a band across
    the middle of the strips, often with white sesame; the panko shows at
    the ends. [MEDIUM — look not independently re-checked]
  - **Arrangement**: cutlet reassembled in its original shape, cut
    faces angled up, in front of the cabbage.
  - **State cues**: just fried: the panko dry and bristly, a faint
    steam from the cut faces.
  - **Absent on purpose**: gravy, cheese, a knife and fork (it is
    pre-cut for chopsticks), fries, a thin pounded cutlet.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A thick pork cutlet about the can's height
    long, cut crosswise into seven strips and set back in shape: coarse,
    bristly, pale-gold panko crust, cut faces showing juicy pale
    pink-white meat with a thin white fat edge; behind it a tall airy
    heap of finely shredded cabbage; a dab of yellow mustard and a lemon
    wedge; a small pot of dark brown sauce beside. No gravy, no fries."

#### Curry rice (karē raisu)

- **Category**: Everyday: arguably the most common home dinner, school
  lunch, canteens, curry chains.
- **Lineage**: Yōshoku (British-Indian curry via the Meiji navy);
  everyday home food since instant roux blocks appeared after the
  Second World War [MEDIUM: ja.wikipedia 日本のカレー (via search)].
  Not Indian curry, not Thai curry.
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **pork curry (Kantō, the east) vs. beef curry
  (Kansai)**. House Foods' 2010 survey found beef most common in the
  Kinki prefectures and pork from Chūbu eastward; in Kansai "curry"
  means beef curry by default [HIGH: SUUMO Journal reporting House Foods,
  Tabizine, TownWork]; **katsu curry** (a tonkatsu on top); **Hokkaidō
  soup curry** (zone 7; different dish); **curry udon**. **Default when
  unspecified**: pork curry (zone 1 default).
- **Condiments**: **fukujinzuke** (red-brown sweet pickle of mixed
  vegetables: daikon, aubergine, lotus root, cucumber and more, at least
  5 kinds by the MAFF definition) is the most common; **rakkyō** (small
  pickled shallots, white and glossy) second [HIGH: Kurashiru, Lettuce
  Club, DIME (via search)].
- **Vessel & scale**: a shallow oval or round plate ~24–27 cm; **rice
  on one side, roux poured on the other half** so both show; a large
  spoon. [MEDIUM — the half-and-half convention is widely photographed,
  not independently re-checked]
- **Texture & finish**: roux thick, glossy, smooth, a medium-to-dark
  brown (not yellow, not orange), coating the back of a spoon; chunks
  of potato, carrot and onion softened and rounded; meat pieces
  half-submerged; rice white and glossy where it is not covered.
- **Model failure**: yellow Thai curry; Indian curry with naan; thin
  sauce; rice and curry in separate bowls; coriander garnish.
- **Confidence**: HIGH for lineage, regional meat and condiments;
  MEDIUM for plating.
- **Sources**: [ja.wikipedia — 日本のカレー](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%97%A5%E6%9C%AC%E3%81%AE%E3%82%AB%E3%83%AC%E3%83%BC);
  [SUUMO Journal — 東日本は豚で西日本は牛](https://suumo.jp/journal/2012/08/02/26000/);
  [House Foods — カレー白書2020](https://housefoods.jp/data/curryhouse/enjoy/research_2020.html).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: one plate.
  - **What dominates**: **roux ~50%** of the plate surface, **rice
    ~40%**, rim and pickles ~10%. The vegetable and meat chunks are
    **accents inside the roux (~15% of the roux area)**, not a pile.
    [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Rice | ~200–250 g; a rounded bank ~12 cm across, ~4 cm high | 1 | Glossy white | Left half of the plate |
    | Roux | ~200 mL | — | Thick, glossy, smooth mid-to-dark brown | Right half, lapping against the rice |
    | Potato chunks | ~2.5–3 cm, edges softened | 3–5 visible | Pale yellow, matte, rounded | Half-submerged in roux |
    | Carrot chunks | ~2 cm irregular (rangiri) | 3–4 visible | Bright orange | Half-submerged |
    | Pork (or beef) pieces | ~2–3 cm thin slices or cubes | 3–5 visible | Brown, soft | Half-submerged |
    | Onion | soft slivers, mostly melted | a few | Translucent amber | In the roux |
    | Fukujinzuke | Small chopped pieces ~5–8 mm | a ~3 cm heap | Glossy red-brown | Plate edge at the rice |
    | Spoon | Large tablespoon ~18 cm | 1 | Steel | Right of the plate |

  - **Arrangement**: diagonal boundary between white rice and brown
    roux; the pickle at the rice's far edge.
  - **Vessel fill**: ~80% of a 25 cm shallow plate covered; a clean rim.
  - **State cues**: steam; a glossy skin just forming on the roux edge.
  - **Absent on purpose**: coriander, naan, yellow colour, coconut milk,
    separate sauce bowl (except at some Western-style restaurants: a
    gravy boat, not default), chopsticks.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A shallow white oval plate about twice the
    can's height long: glossy white short-grain rice banked on the
    left, thick smooth mid-brown curry sauce poured over the right half
    with a few soft, rounded potato and carrot chunks and small pork
    pieces half-sunk in it, gentle steam; a small heap of glossy
    red-brown chopped pickle at the rice edge; a steel spoon beside. No
    herbs, not yellow."

#### Hambāgu (hamburg steak)

- **Category**: Everyday: home dinner (a children's favourite),
  family restaurants, bento.
- **Lineage**: Yōshoku. Not a hamburger (no bun), not Salisbury steak
  in gravy in the US sense, though related.
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **demi-glace sauce** (default), **wafū**
  (grated daikon and ponzu), cheese-in, a fried egg on top, served on
  a hot iron plate at restaurants. [MEDIUM — not independently
  re-checked]
- **Vessel & scale**: an oval patty **~10–12 cm long, ~7–8 cm wide,
  ~2.5–3 cm thick** (150–200 g; not re-checked), on a 24 cm plate or
  a wooden-based iron sizzle plate; sides: a small heap of glazed
  carrot batons, a few sautéed green beans or corn kernels, a potato
  or spaghetti salad; rice served separately in a bowl or on a flat
  plate.
- **Texture & finish**: plump, domed, browned patty with fine cracks on
  top; glossy dark-brown demi-glace poured over the top half and pooling;
  a cut shows a juicy, fine-textured interior.
- **Model failure**: a flat burger patty in a bun; a meatloaf slab.
- **Confidence**: MEDIUM (LOW for exact dimensions).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: one plate.
  - **What dominates**: **patty ~45%** of the plate, sauce ~15%,
    sides ~25%, rim ~15%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|

#### Omurice

#### Gyōza (pan-fried dumplings)

- **Category**: Everyday: home dinner, ramen-shop side, gyōza
  specialists.
- **Lineage**: Chinese-derived (jiaozi), Japanized after WWII:
  pan-fried (yaki) rather than boiled, thin wrapper, garlicky pork and
  cabbage filling. [MEDIUM — not independently re-checked]
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **yaki-gyōza** (default); **hanetsuki** (joined
  by a lacy crisp "wing" skirt); **Utsunomiya** (zone 1 north) and
  **Hamamatsu** (zone 3: arranged in a circle around a heap of boiled
  bean sprouts; not re-checked). **Default**: six yaki-gyōza in a row.
- **Vessel & scale**: **6 per plate** is the norm; **~20–25 g each**
  (some 30 g) [HIGH: Karitto Gyoza blog, Eiyō-keisan, Chiebukuro citing
  a chain at ~25 g]; each ~7 cm long, ~3 cm wide (EDITORIAL); a small
  dipping dish of soy, vinegar and chilli oil.
- **Texture & finish**: pale, thin, slightly translucent pleated tops,
  soft and matte-satin; bottoms fried to a deep golden-brown crisp
  crust; served **browned side up**, in a row, stuck together.
- **Model failure**: Chinese boiled dumplings in a bamboo steamer;
  thick doughy wrappers; bao buns.
- **Confidence**: HIGH for count and weight; MEDIUM for form.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: one plate of six.
  - **What dominates**: **gyōza ~65%** of an oval ~20 cm plate; rim
    shows around them. [EDITORIAL]

#### Nabe (hot pot) — yosenabe and sukiyaki

- **Category**: Seasonal: winter home dinner, gatherings.
- **Lineage**: Native Japanese.
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **yosenabe** (mixed pot of chicken, fish,
  vegetables, tofu in a light dashi; default), **sukiyaki** (thin beef
  simmered in a sweet soy broth in a shallow iron pan, dipped in beaten

  **motsunabe** (zone 5), **ishikari nabe** (zone 7, salmon and miso;

  re-checked]
- **Vessel & scale**: a glazed clay **donabe ~24–27 cm** on a portable
  gas burner in the centre of the table; small bowls (~12 cm torizara
  bowls) at each place; ladle and serving chopsticks. [LOW-MEDIUM — size
  not re-checked]
- **Texture & finish**: a bubbling pale-gold broth; ingredients
  arranged in wedges around the pot (napa cabbage, tofu blocks,
  shiitake with a cross cut into the cap, enoki bundles, green
  shungiku, chicken pieces, carrot flowers).
- **Model failure**: a Chinese hot pot with a red chilli divider;

- **Confidence**: MEDIUM.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: the pot at the start of the
  meal.
  - **What dominates**: **napa cabbage and tofu ~50%** of the pot's

#### Nikujaga and the home side dishes (compact)

- **Nikujaga** (meat and potatoes simmered in sweet soy-dashi):
  potatoes ~4 cm, onion wedges, carrot, thin meat slices, shirataki
  noodles, snow peas; **pork in the east, beef in Kansai** (same line as
  curry [HIGH: SUUMO/House Foods, Daigo]). Served in a ~15 cm bowl or a
  shallow dish, glossy pale-amber, potato edges softened. [MEDIUM]
  **Composition & proportions (§4.7), compact**:
  - *Dominates*: potato ~45%, onion ~20%, meat ~20%, carrot and snow
    peas ~10%, shirataki ~5% of the visible surface. [EDITORIAL]
  - *Components*: potato chunks ~4 cm, 4–5 per bowl, golden, edges
    softened and slightly crumbling; onion wedges ~3 cm, 3–4,
    translucent amber; thin meat slices ~6 cm, 3–4, brown, draped;
    carrot rangiri ~2.5 cm, 2–3, orange; snow peas ~6 cm, 2, bright
    green, laid on top last; shirataki a small knot, translucent grey.
  - *Vessel fill*: heaped in a ~15 cm bowl, ~1 cm of thin amber broth
    pooled at the bottom, the rim clear.
  - *Served portion*: a shared bowl in the middle; each person takes
    2–3 pieces onto a torizara.
  - *State cues*: gentle steam, a light glaze on the potato tops.
  - *Absent*: thick gravy, herbs, a Western stew look, cubes cut
    perfectly square.
  - *Prompt-ready line*: "A small bowl a little wider than the can is

- **Tamagoyaki** (rolled omelette): a block ~12 × 5 × 3.5 cm cut into
  2 cm slices showing a spiral of thin yellow layers; sweet in Kantō,

  **Composition & proportions (§4.7), compact**:

  - *Arrangement*: slices leaning in a row, cut faces to camera; ~40%
    of the dish showing.
  - *State cues*: warm or room temperature, moist (dashimaki may weep a

  - *Absent*: a folded Western omelette, browning all over, fillings,

  - *Prompt-ready line*: "Three slices of rolled Japanese omelette on a

  ginger, green onion and bonito flakes [MEDIUM].
  **Composition & proportions (§4.7), compact**:
  - *Dominates*: the white tofu block (~70% of the dish surface); the
    toppings cover only its top centre.
  - *Components*: one silken or cotton tofu block ~7 × 7 × 3.5 cm,
    smooth, wet, bright white (cotton tofu faintly textured); a ~1.5 cm

    bonito flakes; a thin drizzle of soy pooling at the base.

  - *Absent*: fried tofu, sauce flooding the dish, chilli oil, sesame.
  - *Prompt-ready line*: "A cool, bright-white block of tofu about the

### B. Rice bowls, onigiri and bento

#### Donburi — gyūdon (default) and oyakodon

- **Category**: Everyday: fast lunch at gyūdon chains, home lunch.
- **Lineage**: Native Japanese (gyūdon from Meiji-era beef stews).
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **gyūdon** (simmered thin beef and onion over
  rice, with red pickled ginger); **oyakodon** (chicken and onion
  bound with soft-set egg, mitsuba leaf); **katsudon**; **tendon**;
  **kaisendon** (zone 7, raw seafood). **Default**: gyūdon at a counter.
- **Vessel & scale**: a **~16 cm (5 sun) donburi, ~600 mL**; regular
  portion **200–260 g of rice** by chain [HIGH: Watts; Kettou-ojisan
  clinic citing chains]; a small miso soup on the side; a
  **beni-shōga** (red ginger) tub on the counter.
- **Texture & finish**: gyūdon beef thin (~1–2 mm), loose ribbons,
  glossy light-brown with fat translucent, onion soft and amber, broth
  soaking into the rice at the edges; oyakodon egg half-set, glossy
  yellow and white, with runny patches.
- **Model failure**: Korean bibimbap; beef in thick chunks; a
  Western stew in a bowl.
- **Confidence**: HIGH (vessel, rice weight); MEDIUM (look).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: one regular gyūdon.
  - **What dominates**: from above, **beef ~70%** of the surface,
    onion ~15%, rice showing at the rim ~10%, red ginger ~5%.
    [EDITORIAL]

#### Onigiri (rice balls)

- **Category**: Everyday: konbini, home bento, picnics, hanami.
- **Lineage**: Native Japanese.
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **konbini triangle** with a separate film so the
  nori stays crisp (default); **home-made** (nori wrapped and soft,
  sometimes round or barrel-shaped, or no nori); fillings: salted
  salmon, tuna-mayo, umeboshi (pickled plum), kombu, mentaiko;
  **yaki-onigiri** (grilled, soy-glazed); **pōku tamago onigiri**
  (zone 6; not re-checked). **Default**: two triangle onigiri with nori.
- **Vessel & scale**: **~100–110 g each** with filling; premium lines
  120 g+; "bomb" size 200 g+ [HIGH: Grill Kajitsu, CVS365, Enomusubi];
  triangle ~8–9 cm on a side, ~3–4 cm thick (EDITORIAL estimate).
- **Texture & finish**: rice grains visible and distinct, packed but
  not smushed, glossy; konbini nori crisp, matte-dark with a faint
  sheen, cleanly wrapped around the base and sides; home-made nori
  softened and clinging.
- **Model failure**: a round rice ball with sesame (Korean
  jumeokbap); sushi; oversized "rice mountains"; plastic wrapper with
  legible labels.
- **Confidence**: HIGH for weight.
- **Sources**: [Grill Kajitsu — コンビニおにぎりは何グラム](https://www.grillkajitsu.jp/onigiri/89/);
  [CVS365 — おにぎりのグラム](https://www.cvs365.com/cvstore-riceball-grams/).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: two konbini onigiri, unwrapped,
  on a plate or their film.
  - **What dominates**: **white rice ~55%** of the visible surface,
    **nori ~45%** (covering the base and lower two-thirds of both
    faces). [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |

    | Rice triangle | ~8.5 cm sides, ~3.5 cm thick, rounded corners | 2 | Glossy distinct white grains | Standing upright or lying flat, point up |

  - **State cues**: room temperature, no steam; nori crisp.
  - **Absent on purpose**: printed wrappers (text), sesame coating,
    a hand holding one, a bite mark unless briefed.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "Two triangular rice balls, each about
    two-thirds the can's height on a side, one standing and one lying on

#### Bento (home, konbini, ekiben, hanami)

- **Category**: Everyday (lunch) to occasion (hanami, travel).
- **Lineage**: Native Japanese.
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **home bento** in a reusable box (one or two
  tiers, or a bent-cedar **magewappa**); **konbini bento** in a black or
  clear plastic tray; **ekiben** (station bento, regional contents);
  **makunouchi** (compartmented, many small items); **hanami/picnic
  jūbako** (stacked tiers to share). **Default**: home bento in a
  two-compartment box.
- **Vessel & scale**: box **~600–700 mL for an adult woman, ~900 mL+
  for a man**; a guideline packs **staple : main : side = 3 : 1 : 2**
  by volume, and a 600 mL box holds about one rice bowl of rice in half
  its space [MEDIUM: Bento&co, Fumakilla, Angers (via search)]; a
  600 mL box is roughly 17 × 11 × 5 cm (EDITORIAL).
- **Texture & finish**: food packed tightly with no gaps, dividers of
  green baran (plastic grass) or lettuce, small silicone cups; rice
  flat-packed, often with one red umeboshi in the centre or a sprinkle of
  furikake; colours balanced red/yellow/green/brown/white.
- **Model failure**: a Western lunchbox with a sandwich; a sushi
  platter; food loose and sparse; a cartoon "kyaraben" (character
  bento) unless briefed.
- **Confidence**: MEDIUM.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: one 600 mL home bento, lid off.
  - **What dominates**: **rice ~50% of the box area**, mains ~20%,
    sides and vegetables ~30%. [MEDIUM ratio source; layout EDITORIAL]

    | Divider | baran leaf or lettuce | 1–2 | Green | Between items |

  - **Arrangement**: packed tight, items upright, no empty space.
  - **State cues**: room temperature, no steam; slight gloss.
  - **Absent on purpose**: steam, sauces pooling, legible stickers, a

  - **Prompt-ready line**: "An open rectangular lunch box a little

    a small cup of stir-fried burdock; a leaf divider; chopsticks in a

#### Sushi — conveyor-belt nigiri (default) and temaki at home

- **Category**: Everyday treat (kaitenzushi, supermarket packs) to
  special occasion (counter sushi, family gatherings, Obon and New
  Year platters).
- **Lineage**: Native Japanese (Edomae nigiri). The California roll and
  uramaki with sauces are not Japanese defaults.
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **kaitenzushi** (conveyor belt, **two pieces
  per small plate**, default); **counter sushi** (one or two pieces at a
  time on a wooden geta board); **temaki-zushi at home** (hand rolls
  assembled at the table from half nori sheets, rice and fillings);
  **chirashi** (scattered toppings on rice); **ehōmaki** (Setsubun).
- **Vessel & scale**: shari **~15–18 g** per piece at big conveyor
  chains (hand-made ~15–20 g), topping ~10 g, whole piece 26–30 g
  [HIGH: Daily Shinchō measurement, Fish-neta, Kgmg]; a piece ~6–7 cm
  long, ~2.5 cm wide, ~2.5–3 cm tall (EDITORIAL); conveyor plates small,
  ~13–15 cm (not sourced).
- **Texture & finish**: rice a compact but airy oblong, grains
  distinct, slightly glossy, faintly off-white from vinegar; topping
  draped over it and hanging slightly past both ends, glistening:
  tuna deep red, salmon orange with white fat lines, yellowtail pale
  pink, shrimp striped orange-white, tamago yellow block with a nori
  belt.
- **Model failure**: huge nigiri; maki rolls with sauces and sesame;
  sushi as every Japanese meal; chopsticks in the rice; a soy dish
  overflowing.
- **Confidence**: HIGH for weights; MEDIUM for plating.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: three conveyor plates (six
  pieces) in front of one diner.
  - **What dominates**: **the toppings' colour** (~60% of what the
    camera sees on the pieces), rice ~40%; plates show ~50% empty
    surface around each pair. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Nigiri | ~6.5 × 2.5 × 2.8 cm | 2 per plate, 6 total | Topping draped and glossy; rice oblong, distinct grains | Side by side, parallel, on each plate |
    | Plates | ~14 cm round (EDITORIAL), colour-coded | 3, one stacked | Plain coloured rims (no text) | In front of the diner; a short stack of 2 empties at the side |
    | Gari (pickled ginger) | thin slices ~3 cm | a small heap | Pale pink-beige, translucent | A small dish |
    | Soy dish | ~8 cm | 1 | A thin film of dark soy | Front-right |

  - **Temaki at home (variant)**: a large central platter with rows of
    fillings (tuna strips, salmon, cucumber batons, tamago sticks,
    avocado, imitation crab), a rice tub (a wooden hangiri, ~30–40 cm),
    a stack of **half nori sheets ~19 × 10 cm**; finished cones about
    the can's height long lie on each person's plate. [EDITORIAL]
  - **State cues**: fish glistening, rice not steaming (room temperature).
  - **Absent on purpose**: sauces drizzled on top, sesame, cream cheese,
    tempura flakes, a green-tea cup (always served at kaitenzushi:
    exclude by name), a sake cup.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "On a counter by a conveyor belt, three small
    round plates each holding two pieces of hand-pressed sushi, each
    piece about the can's width long: compact oblongs of distinct
    vinegared rice under glossy draped slices of deep-red tuna,
    orange salmon with white fat lines, and pale yellowtail; a few
    slices of pale pink pickled ginger and a small dish of soy sauce.
    No tea cup, no rolls, no sauces."

### C. Noodles

#### Ramen — regional index and full entries (Tokyo shōyu default)

- **Category**: Everyday: counter lunch, late dinner, instant at home.
- **Lineage**: Chinese-derived wheat noodles in soup, fully Japanese

  | Variant | Soup | Noodles | Toppings | Zone | Confidence |

### D. Konamon (batter food) and grilled street food

#### Okonomiyaki — Osaka (mixed) and Hiroshima (layered)

- **Category**: Everyday: teppan restaurants, home hot plates, festival
  stalls.
- **Lineage**: Native Japanese konamon ("flour food").
- **Variants (§4.6)**: two coexisting forms; **offer both**, do not
  pick silently:
  - **Osaka / Kansai (zone 2)**: batter (flour, dashi, egg, grated yam),
    chopped cabbage and tenkasu (tempura bits) **mixed together** and
    griddled as one thick fluffy cake with pork slices on top. [HIGH:
    Tenpos, Rakuten Recipe, Ouchigohan Lab; MAFF lists Osaka konamon]
  - **Hiroshima (zone 4)**: **layered**: a thin crêpe of batter, a tall
    heap of shredded cabbage, bean sprouts, pork belly, then a layer of
    **fried soba (or udon) noodles** and an egg underneath, flipped;
    **more cabbage** than Osaka's, and noodles essential [HIGH: same
    sources].
  - **Default when unspecified**: Osaka style. [EDITORIAL]
- **Vessel & scale**: Osaka: **~18–22 cm across, ~2–3 cm thick**;
  Hiroshima: ~20 cm across, **~4–6 cm tall** (both EDITORIAL; not
  sourced); eaten from the **teppan** with a small metal hera, or
  cut into wedges on a plate at home.
- **Texture & finish**: both: glossy dark-brown sauce brushed over the
  top, **thin zigzag lines of pale mayonnaise** (Osaka, common; less so
  Hiroshima, not re-checked), **green aonori powder**, **bonito flakes**
  that curl and wave in the heat. Osaka's cut face is fluffy, pale with
  green-white cabbage flecks; Hiroshima's cut face shows distinct
  strata: thin crêpe, cabbage, pork, a band of browned noodles, egg.
- **Model failure**: a pizza with cheese; a Korean pajeon; Hiroshima
  drawn without noodles, or Osaka drawn with noodles.
- **Confidence**: HIGH for the structural difference; sizes EDITORIAL.
- **Sources**: [Tenpos Food Media — 大阪と広島のお好み焼きの違い](https://foodmedia.tenpos.com/newstrend/trend/66433/);
  [Rekishi Kaidō — 広島vs.大阪](https://rekishikaido.php.co.jp/detail/2545).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: one Osaka okonomiyaki on the
  teppan, one wedge cut.
  - **What dominates**: from above, **sauce-covered top ~85%** (dark
    brown), mayo lines ~5%, aonori and bonito ~10%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Cake | ~20 cm round, ~2.5 cm thick | 1 | Golden-brown crisp edge; fluffy pale interior with cabbage flecks | On the teppan |

    | Aonori | powder | a dusting | Green | Over all |
    | Hera | ~10 cm metal spatula | 1–2 | Steel | Resting on the griddle edge |

  - **Hiroshima (variant block)**: a taller disc, the **top a thin
    browned crêpe or egg layer**, sauce brushed; the cut face shows a

### E. Regional signatures

#### Jingisukan (zone 7 — Hokkaidō)

- **Category**: Everyday-to-gathering: home tabletop grill, dedicated
  restaurants, outdoor BBQ and hanami in Hokkaidō.
- **Lineage**: Hokkaidō regional dish (grilled mutton or lamb); the name
  is a Japanese coinage, not a Mongolian dish.
- **Form**: lamb or mutton, raw or pre-marinated in a sweet soy-fruit
  sauce, grilled with **onion, bean sprouts, green pepper and carrot**
  on a **dedicated domed iron pan**: the centre rises and has grooves,
  so the meat juices run down onto the vegetables around the lower rim
  [HIGH: MAFF うちの郷土料理 Hokkaidō jingisukan entry; ja.wikipedia;
  Bell Foods (via search)]. Raw-then-dip (Sapporo) vs. pre-marinated
  (Takikawa) styles coexist (not re-checked).
- **Vessel & scale**: pan ~25–30 cm across, domed ~4–5 cm (EDITORIAL;
  not sourced); on a tabletop burner; small bowls of dipping sauce and
  rice at each place.
- **Texture & finish**: lamb slices ~5 mm, browned at the edges and
  still pink-juicy in places, glistening; bean sprouts wilting and
  glossy with juices at the rim; onion rings soft and caramelised.
- **Model failure**: Korean bulgogi on a flat grill; Mongolian stir-fry;
  skewered lamb.
- **Confidence**: HIGH for the pan and ingredients; sizes EDITORIAL.
- **Sources**: [MAFF — ジンギスカン 北海道](https://www.maff.go.jp/j/keikaku/syokubunka/k_ryouri/search_menu/menu/jingisukan_hokkaido.html).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: the pan mid-meal.
  - **What dominates**: **lamb ~40%** (on the dome), **bean sprouts and
    onion ~45%** (around the lower rim), pepper and carrot ~15%.
    [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count (pan) | Look | Where it sits |

#### Gyūtan teishoku (zone 8 — Sendai)

- **Category**: Regional speciality set meal (lunch or dinner out).
- **Lineage**: Sendai, from the post-war period; a city-branded dish.
- **Form**: thick-cut grilled beef tongue with **mugimeshi** (rice
  cooked with barley), **tail soup** (clear oxtail broth with green
  onion) and **nanban-miso** (green chilli pickled in miso) and
  pickled cabbage or cucumber on the side [HIGH: Fujisaki department
  store gyūtan history page; City of Sendai page (via search)].
- **Vessel & scale**: tongue slices ~8–10 cm long, ~1 cm thick
  (EDITORIAL; "thick-cut" is sourced), usually 3–6 slices on a
  rectangular plate; barley rice in a 12 cm bowl; tail soup in a
  lacquer or ceramic bowl.
- **Texture & finish**: slices grilled over charcoal, scored with a
  fine knife pattern, browned and slightly charred at the edges, pale
  grey-pink inside, faintly glossy with salt and fat; barley rice white
  with darker grains, each barley grain showing a brown line.
- **Model failure**: thin Korean-BBQ tongue slices; a steak; a dark
  stew.
- **Confidence**: HIGH for the set; MEDIUM for sizes.
- **Sources**: [Fujisaki — 仙台牛たん定食](https://www.fujisaki.co.jp/gyutan-selection/history02.html).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: one set on a tray.
  - **What dominates**: on the main plate **tongue ~60%**, pickles and
    nanban-miso ~25%, plate ~15%; on the tray, rice and soup each ~20%.
    [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|

### F. Festive and seasonal tables

#### Osechi (New Year) and ozōni

- **Category**: Annual ritual: 1–3 January family meal.
- **Lineage**: Native Japanese.
- **Form**: auspicious dishes packed in **tiered lacquer boxes
  (jūbako)**. **Tier one** holds the **iwai-zakana** (celebratory
  starters) and **kuchitori**: **kuromame** (glossy black soybeans),
  **kazunoko** (herring roe), **tazukuri** (small candied dried
  sardines), **datemaki** (a rolled sweet omelette with fish paste),
  **kuri-kinton** (sweet chestnut and sweet-potato paste), **kōbu-maki**,
  **red-and-white kamaboko**. Tier two: vinegared and grilled dishes
  (vinegared lotus root, red-and-white namasu, prawns, sea bream,
  yellowtail). Tier three: **nishime / chikuzen-ni** (simmered root
  vegetables). The set balances **five colours: red, green, yellow,
  black, white**. The three iwai-zakana are **kazunoko, tazukuri and
  kuromame in Kantō**, with **tataki-gobō** (pounded burdock) replacing
  kuromame in Kansai [HIGH: NHK Kyō no Ryōri, Kamaboko.com, Daimaru
  Matsuzakaya, Dinos (via search)]. Many households now buy osechi
  ready-made or eat a reduced set (not re-checked).
- **Ozōni (form-changing, §4.2)**: **east of Sekigahara: square grilled
  mochi in a clear soy-dashi broth (sumashi)**; **Kansai and eastern
  Shikoku: round mochi in white-miso broth**; **the rest of western
  Japan: round mochi in clear broth**; exceptions include Shōnai and
  Ichinoseki (round in the east) and Kōchi and Kagoshima (square in the
  west) [HIGH: MAFF aff magazine "地域で違う餅の形", Weathernews,
  Otonanswer]. **Default when unspecified**: square mochi, clear broth
  (zone 1).
- **Vessel & scale**: jūbako tiers ~18–21 cm square (MEDIUM — not
  re-checked), black lacquer outside, red inside; ozōni in a lidded
  lacquer bowl ~12 cm; **iwai-bashi** (celebration chopsticks, tapered
  at both ends, in a paper sleeve) [MEDIUM — not re-checked].
- **Model failure**: a Chinese New Year spread (dumplings, red
  envelopes, lanterns); sushi platter in a box; random colourful bento.
- **Confidence**: HIGH for contents and regional rules.
- **Sources**: [NHK きょうの料理 — 祝い肴](https://www.kyounoryouri.jp/contents/89770);
  [Kamaboko.com — おせち料理の定番](https://www.kamaboko.com/column/43633/);
  [MAFF — 地域で違う餅の形](https://www.maff.go.jp/j/pr/aff/2001/spe2_02.html).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: the top tier, open, seen from
  above.
  - **What dominates**: the box is divided into a **3 × 3 grid of
    compartments** (or diagonal sections), each ~6 × 6 cm, packed
    full: no single item dominates; colours alternate. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Kuromame | beans ~1.2 cm | ~30 in a small cup | Glossy jet black, wrinkle-free | One compartment, often in a small dish |

    | Tazukuri | fish ~4–5 cm | ~15 | Glossy dark amber, candied, with sesame | One compartment |

    | Garnish | nandina or bamboo leaves | a few | Green | Between compartments |

  - **Ozōni (served portion)**: square mochi ~5 × 4 cm, grilled with a
    puffed, golden-blistered surface, 1–2 per bowl, in clear pale broth
    with a piece of chicken, a slice of kamaboko, komatsuna greens and a
    twist of yuzu peel; the mochi soft and slightly melted at the edges.
  - **State cues**: osechi served cold, glossy; ozōni steaming.

    Chinese New Year décor, shrine imagery, legible greeting cards.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "An open square black lacquer box with a red
