# Indonesia

## FILE ROLE & METHOD

This file is Indonesia's country file: a single national staging brief
with six labeled internal zones. One TCCC hero beverage per scene (named
by the brief — see HERO PRODUCT SLOT), staged against real Indonesian
dishes, vessels and settings, with the full drinks landscape — es teh
manis, kopi, es jeruk, es campur, bottled tea, jamu, and the alcohol
that exists in Bali and the Christian east — documented as context even
where a drink is never itself staged.

**Scope.** Lunch, dinner, snacks, street food and the **Ramadan
buka puasa (breaking-the-fast) table** are in scope. **Breakfast (nasi
uduk, bubur ayam, nasi kuning) sits in an OPTIONAL MORNING MODULE, off
by default**, the same pattern as `thailand.md`, `brazil.md` and
`india.md` — a scope call **flagged for Fernando's confirmation** (§1.2;
log in `DECISIONS.md`). Rice is eaten at every meal, so the morning dishes
are real meals, not pastries; **sahur** (the pre-dawn Ramadan meal) is
also morning-module material. No beverage other than the hero TCCC
product is staged; the others are documented in ICONIC BEVERAGES.

### Hard staging rules specific to Indonesia (read before any scene)

1. **Halal is the default. No pork and no alcohol in any scene unless the
   brief explicitly names a documented exception.** Indonesia is **~87%
   Muslim** (Ministry of Home Affairs 2022: 87% Muslim, 7.4% Protestant,
   3.0% Catholic, 1.7% Hindu, 0.7% Buddhist) [HIGH — US State Dept 2023
   International Religious Freedom report; Wikipedia "Indonesia"].
   **Every scene with no zone or community named is halal**: proteins are
   chicken, beef, goat/mutton, fish, prawn, squid, egg, tofu and tempeh;
   **no pork in any form** (no babi, no lard, no pork-skin crackling), **no
   alcohol anywhere in frame**, and nothing that reads as either (no
   glistening pink roast with crackling, no beer bottle, no wine glass).
   [HIGH for demography; EDITORIAL for the rule]
2. **The documented exceptions — staged only when a brief asks for them by
   name.** **Bali** is ~90% Hindu and eats pork openly — **babi guling**
   (spit-roast suckling pig) and **lawar** are Balinese signatures [HIGH
   for the Hindu share — US State Dept 2023; dishes MEDIUM — not
   independently re-checked]. The **Christian-majority provinces** — **North
   Sulawesi** (Manado), **Papua** and its new provinces, and **East Nusa
   Tenggara** — and the **Batak** (largely Christian) communities of North
   Sumatra also cook pork (Batak **babi panggang Karo**, Manado pork dishes)
   [HIGH for the Christian majorities — US State Dept 2023; pork dishes
   MEDIUM — not independently re-checked]. **Rules**: (a) a Bali, Manado,
   Papua or Batak brief does **not** default to pork — Muslim Balinese,
   Muslim Manadonese and halal warungs are everywhere there too; pork
   appears only if the brief names a pork dish or a non-Muslim household;
   (b) **never stage pork and the hero together in a scene coded as
   Muslim** (hijab, peci, mosque, Ramadan, Lebaran); (c) even in Bali,
   **no alcohol in frame** (project rule).
3. **Eat with spoon and fork — or the right hand, never shown.** The
   urban default is a **spoon in the right hand and a fork in the left**;
   the spoon goes to the mouth, the fork pushes; **no table knife** [HIGH —
   factsanddetails, Twinkl, Indoindians, Wikipedia "Eating utensil
   etiquette" agree]. **Eating with the right hand (makan pakai tangan)** is
   equally real — at Padang restaurants, pecel lele and lalapan stalls, and
   home meals — with a **finger bowl (kobokan)** of water and a lime slice
   on the table; **the left hand never touches food** [HIGH — same
   sources]. **Project rule: no hand in frame.** Signal hand-eating with a
   kobokan beside the plate and no cutlery, never with a hand in the food.
   **Chopsticks only for Chinese-Indonesian noodle and bakmi dishes and
   bakso** (and even there a spoon dominates) [MEDIUM — not independently
   re-checked].
4. **Rice is the meal.** "Kalau belum makan nasi, belum makan" — if you
   haven't eaten rice, you haven't eaten; rice is eaten two to three times
   a day [MEDIUM — expat.or.id, Remitly (tier 3)]. A lunch or dinner scene
   without rice (or lontong/ketupat, or noodles) reads as a snack.
5. **Sambal and krupuk are near-universal.** Almost every rice plate
   carries a **dab or saucer of sambal** (chilli relish) and a few
   **krupuk** (puffed crackers — pale prawn crackers, white tapioca
   crackers, or small emping) on the side or from a tin on the table
   [MEDIUM — not independently re-checked; uncontested]. A rice plate
   without sambal looks unfinished; a giant fan of crackers looks like a
   restaurant gimmick — use 2–4 pieces.
6. **Buka puasa staging is editorial and sensitive — read FESTIVALS
   before any Ramadan scene.** **Never show the hero product as the first
   thing that breaks the fast.** Islamic practice and Indonesian habit
   break the fast with **dates, water, or a sweet takjil** (kolak, es
   buah) first; the meal comes after maghrib prayer. Stage the table
   **before sunset, untouched, food covered or waiting**, or **later in the
   meal**, with dates and takjil already in place and visibly first.
   **No one shown mid-sip; no countdown/adhan cue with the product in
   focus.** [EDITORIAL — flagged for Fernando and for TCCC Indonesia
   sign-off; see FESTIVALS and GAP LOG]
7. **Religious imagery stays out of frame beside the product.** No TCCC
   product on or beside a Qur'an, prayer mat (sajadah), mosque interior,
   Balinese **canang sari** offering, family shrine (sanggah/merajan),
   temple, or church altar. Canang sari (small palm-leaf trays of flowers
   and rice) sit on Balinese pavements, shop steps and dashboards — **never
   step the product onto, beside or behind one**; keep them out of frame.
   [EDITORIAL; canang ubiquity MEDIUM — not independently re-checked]
8. **No alcohol staged, and never a TCCC product as a mixer with alcohol (non-alcoholic TCCC-brand mixes are allowed, schema §5.5).** Bintang
   beer, arak Bali, tuak (palm wine) and Manado **cap tikus** exist (see
   ICONIC BEVERAGES). **Also never as a non-alcoholic mixer**: **soda
   gembira** (Fanta poured over sweetened condensed milk) is a beloved
   Indonesian drink and a Ramadan classic, but it presents a TCCC product
   as an ingredient — **not staged** unless Fernando/TCCC Indonesia
   explicitly rules it in [EDITORIAL — flagged; see GAP LOG].
9. **Iced sweet tea is the intruder.** **Es teh manis** (sweet iced tea in
   a tall glass) is the default drink at every warung and rumah makan, and
   **a glass or jug of plain water or warm tea** is on every home table.
   Name them in the negative. Bottled tea (Teh Botol Sosro) and Pocari
   Sweat are the commonest packaged intruders.
10. **General project rules**: no legible text anywhere (Latin-script
    Bahasa signs and banners, Arabic script, Javanese/Balinese script,
    Chinese characters included); nothing held in a hand; no drinks other
    than the hero; never a TCCC product as a mixer with alcohol (non-alcoholic TCCC-brand mixes are allowed, schema §5.5).

### Structural decision: one file, six zones (recommendation — reviewer has final say)

The `country-file-schema.md` §1.1 swap test, applied with this pass's
evidence:

- **A real national core exists.** **Nasi goreng, mie goreng, satay,
  gado-gado, soto, bakso, ayam goreng/ayam geprek, martabak, gorengan and
  nasi Padang** are sold in every Indonesian city; Padang restaurants
  (rumah makan Padang) are a nationwide chain-like institution [HIGH for
  nasi Padang's national spread — Wikipedia "Padang cuisine", "Nasi
  padang"]. A plate of nasi goreng with a fried egg and krupuk reads
  correctly anywhere from Medan to Makassar.
- **The flavour and the plate change by island.** Central Java is sweet
  (gudeg, kecap-heavy satay); West Java (Sunda) eats raw vegetables
  (lalapan) with sambal; East Java is dark and savoury (rawon, petis);
  West Sumatra is rich, coconut-and-chilli, served hidang-style on a
  dozen saucers; Aceh is spiced like India's west coast (mie Aceh, kari);
  Bali is Hindu and pork-permissive; Makassar eats beef soups (coto,
  konro); the eastern islands eat sago (papeda), fish and corn. Swapping
  a Padang hidang spread into a Yogyakarta angkringan, or babi guling
  into a Jakarta office lunch, is a visible — and in the second case
  offensive — error.
- **Several dishes are form-changing**: satay (Madura black-kecap vs
  Padang yellow gravy vs Balinese sate lilit), soto (clear Madura/Lamongan
  yellow vs coconut Betawi vs dark Makassar coto), nasi campur (Javanese
  vs Balinese), sambal (dozens).
- **The environment changes**: a Jakarta kampung row house, cluster
  estate or high-rise apartment; a Javanese joglo-roofed house with a
  pendopo; a Minangkabau rumah gadang with horn-shaped roofs; a Balinese
  family compound with carved gates and shrines; a Makassar or Manado
  wooden stilt house; a Papuan honai.

**Recommendation: one file with six zones** (the Spain/Türkiye/Thailand
pattern), not a US-style split — with **Bali** and **Sumatra/Padang**
both flagged as **spinout candidates** if a brief volume ever justifies
it (Bali because its religion and pork change the hard rules; Padang
because hidang service is its own composition system). **A
recommendation for the reviewer, not a decision** (§7).

### Default when no zone is named

Fall back to **zone 1 (Jakarta) at the everyday register**, halal: a
**nasi goreng or nasi campur lunch** at a **warung or warteg** (a small
open-fronted eatery with a glass display of ready dishes, plain tables
and benches), or **ayam geprek** at a cheap rumah makan; at home, a
landed row house or cluster house in Jabodetabek with a table of rice,
two or three shared lauk (dishes), sambal and krupuk. [EDITORIAL

## METHOD NOTE (read first)

**Build method, this pass.** Drafted directly from model knowledge and
checked with ~40 WebSearch queries, prioritising the claims a scene
visibly depends on: pack formats and the bottler, the halal default and
its exceptions, housing, table etiquette, Ramadan and Lebaran 2027
dates, and dish forms and sizes. Tags mean:

- **[HIGH] / [MEDIUM] / [LOW]** — schema §6 tags earned this pass (HIGH =
  2+ independent corroborating sources; MEDIUM = 1 credible source).
- **[… — not independently re-checked this pass]** — model knowledge that
  is uncontested general culinary or cultural knowledge but was not
  individually searched. Plausible, not verified.
- **[EDITORIAL]** — a judgment call (defaults, portion counts, surface
  shares, caricature-avoidance guidance, Ramadan staging), never a factual
  claim.
- **[HIGH — first-party test]** — this project's own image-generation
  findings (`coca-cola-guidelines.md` §1, `country-file-schema.md` §7.5).
  **No Indonesia-specific image tests have been run.**
- **"(via search)"** — the source page itself was blocked by the egress
  proxy; the claim rests on the search-result snippet.

**Writing principle.** Describe what the camera sees: the near-black,
oily, fibrous crust of rendang; the lacy brown skirt of a telur ceplok;
the turmeric-yellow gloss of soto broth; the matte grey-green of a
banana-leaf parcel; the charred, kecap-lacquered edges of sate Madura;
and real-world size.

**File-wide rules for every scene built from this file:**

1. **Text as atmosphere.** Warung banners (spanduk), menu boards, the
   printed paper (kertas nasi) that rice is wrapped in, condiment-bottle
   labels, minimarket bags (Indomaret/Alfamart) and delivery-app bags
   (GoFood/GrabFood jackets and boxes) appear only as heavily blurred,
   unreadable colour. Any readable word, number or brand mark means
   reject or retouch. Blur instructions are known to fail [HIGH —
   first-party test; `country-file-schema.md` §7.5]. Indonesian street
   stalls carry a **very heavy printed-banner prior** (painted carts with
   the dish name in large letters) — frame tight, or turn the cart side-on.
2. **The hero product is a TCCC beverage named by the brief** (HERO
   PRODUCT SLOT). Branding is composited in post
   (`coca-cola-guidelines.md` §1–2).
3. **No alcohol in any scene**, never a TCCC product as a mixer with alcohol (non-alcoholic TCCC-brand mixes are allowed, schema §5.5).
   Soda gembira and TCCC Indonesia's Fanta buka-puasa mixes may be staged
   when the brief calls for them (Fernando, 2026-10-01).
4. **No drinks in frame other than the hero product** unless the brief
   explicitly allows a named non-alcoholic companion. Name the likely
   intruders in the negative: a tall glass of es teh manis with ice, a
   glass of plain water, a plastic cup-sealed drink (gelas plastik with a
   printed lid film), a bottle of Teh Botol, a glass of es jeruk, a cup of
   kopi tubruk, a plastic bag of iced drink with a straw.
5. **Nothing held in a hand.** Satay, gorengan, martabak and street snacks
   rest on a plate, banana leaf, woven bamboo tray (tampah), paper-lined
   rattan plate, or styrofoam/paper box laid on a counter
   (`country-file-schema.md` §7.5).
6. **Halal, exception, Ramadan and religious-imagery rules** (hard rules
   1, 2, 6, 7) apply to every scene.

## QUICK-REFERENCE: GENERIC SCENE REGISTERS

| Generic scene type | What to draw on |
|---|---|
| **Warung (small family eatery)** | Open-fronted, often a single room on a street: a long wooden or tiled counter, plain tables with benches, a glass display cabinet (etalase) of ready lauk in enamel or steel trays; a kettle; a jar of krupuk; a calendar on the wall (blurred); tarp or tin awning. |
| **Warteg (warung Tegal)** | The Jakarta working-lunch canteen: a glass-fronted display of 15–30 dishes in shallow trays (tempeh orek, sayur lodeh, telur balado, ikan goreng, perkedel), the diner points, the server spoons onto a plate of rice; a long counter bench along the glass; tiled walls, bright tube light. |
| **Kaki lima (street cart)** | A wheeled wooden or aluminium cart (gerobak) with a glass case, a gas burner or charcoal, a tarp roof; plastic stools and a low bench or a folding table; bakso, nasi goreng, sate, martabak, siomay; banner text kept unreadable. |
| **Angkringan (Yogyakarta/Solo night cart)** | A wooden cart under a tarp lit by a single bulb or oil lamp, three clay-and-charcoal kettles (anglo), the cart top crowded with tiny banana-leaf packets of **nasi kucing**, skewers of quail eggs, chicken intestine and tofu satay, gorengan in a pile; customers on wooden benches around three sides, or lesehan mats on the pavement. |
| **Rumah makan Padang** | A bright, clean room, a **front window stacked with pyramids of dishes** in enamel plates (the display), formica tables; at the table, hidang: **10–15 small plates** set down at once, rice on a separate plate per person, a kobokan finger bowl. |
| **Lesehan** | Diners seated on a woven mat (tikar) or low raised platform (balé), shoes off, low table, often by a fishpond or rice field in Sundanese and Javanese restaurants; ikan bakar, gurame goreng, lalapan. |
| **Home meal (Java)** | A dining table with a plastic-patterned or lace tablecloth, a **rice pot or rice cooker**, 2–4 lauk-pauk in serving bowls (a vegetable dish, a fried protein, tempeh/tofu), a saucer of sambal, a jar or tin of krupuk, plates and spoon-and-fork per person, a **tudung saji** (woven or plastic food cover dome) nearby. |
| **Mall food court / café** | Air-conditioned, tidy, melamine or white china; a coffee-shop register (kopi susu) for young adults — es kopi susu is an intruder. |

## TRUSTED CONTENT

### HERO PRODUCT SLOT

Every scene carries one TCCC hero product, **named by the brief**. This
file uses `south-africa.md`'s generalised slot; since 2026-09-27 that is
the project-wide rule (the brief dictates the SKU, never the region — see
`DECISIONS.md` and `country-file-schema.md` §5.4). **If a brief names no
product, ask for one.** Nothing below is a default.

**Template:**
> [HERO PRODUCT]: {brand and variant exactly as named on pack}, in
> {format and size}, {dominant pack colour and material cue},
> {negated lookalikes}. {Position: standing upright on the surface,
> label facing camera or turned slightly}. Pack text will be composited
> in post.

**Rules:**
- **Name the variant; negate the closest lookalike.** CCEP Indonesia
  lists **Coca-Cola (Original)**, **Coca-Cola Zero Sugar** and
  **Coca-Cola Light** [MEDIUM — CCEP "Our Drinks" Indonesia and
  coca-cola.com/id brand page, via search]. An unspecified "Coca-Cola can"
  rendered as the wrong variant in 2 of 3 generations [HIGH — first-party
  test, `coca-cola-guidelines.md` §1]. Whether a reduced-sugar red
  variant exists in Indonesia (as in Thailand) was not found [LOW — not
  resolved].
- **Negate the local competitors — and note that Pepsi is gone.**
  **PepsiCo left Indonesia in October 2019** (its bottler Anugerah
  Indofood Barokah Makmur stopped production on 10 Oct 2019); before the
  exit Coca-Cola held ~42% of carbonated soft drinks against Pepsi's ~32%
  [HIGH for the exit — Tempo, ANTARA, Mini Me Insights; MEDIUM for the
  shares — YouGov, single source]. The live competitors are **Big Cola**
  (AJE), private-label and regional colas, and the much bigger
  non-carbonated rivals — **Teh Botol Sosro**, **Pocari Sweat**, bottled
  water (Aqua) [MEDIUM — YouGov; Big Cola from Wikipedia "Ajegroup"].
  Still write "not Pepsi, not Big Cola, not any other cola brand" — a
  model does not know Pepsi left.
- **Indonesia is a 330 mL standard-can market, with a 250 mL slim can
  alongside.** Retail lists **Coca-Cola 330 mL cans, singly and in
  6-packs** (~Rp34,000–40,000 a six; Coke Zero 330 mL six on Blibli) and
  CCEP Indonesia's own Coke Zero Sugar page lists a **330 mL can** [HIGH —
  Blibli, Tokopedia, mbizmarket, Sulsel provincial e-catalogue (330 mL ×
  24), CCEP Coke Zero page via search]. A **250 mL slim can ("Coca-Cola
  Slim 250 mL")** is sold at Indomaret (~Rp6,300) and by the 24-can case
  [HIGH — klikindomaret, mentimun, Panen Square listings]. **This agrees
  with `coca-cola-guidelines.md` §4.3's 330 mL non-US default** for the
  standard can (115.2 × 66.1 mm). **The slim can's dimensions were not
  found** — it is visibly taller and narrower than the 330 mL can (a
  sleek-can silhouette) [LOW — not sourced; inference from "slim"].
- **Formats confirmed current in Indonesia this pass** (reference for
  whoever writes the brief — never a default):
  - **Cans**: **330 mL** standard [HIGH]; **250 mL slim** [HIGH].
  - **PET**: **250 mL, 390 mL** (the everyday single-serve bottle,
    ~Rp5,000–6,000 at Indomaret), **1 L** (~Rp10,000) and **1.5 L**
    [HIGH for 390 mL — klikindomaret, CCEP via search, Mini Me Insights
    (390 mL and 1.25 L introduced 2015); MEDIUM for 250 mL PET and 1 L —
    CCEP via search and retail aggregators; HIGH for 1.5 L — retail
    listings, CCEP via search]. **1.25 L** was launched in 2015 and may
    still circulate [LOW — 2015 source only]. **No 500 mL PET was
    found** — the personal bottle is **390 mL** (another mismatch with
    `coca-cola-guidelines.md` §4.4's "500mL"). **100% rPET** bottles
    (390 mL and others) were launched nationally in 2023 [HIGH —
    packaging-gateway, CCEP news].
  - **Returnable glass ("botol kaca"/"botol beling")**: the small contour
    bottle in **24-bottle crates (krat)** that mix Coca-Cola, Sprite and
    Fanta is a warung and old-style rumah makan sight; the commonest size
    is reported as **~192–193 mL**, with a 250 mL glass also mentioned
    [LOW — Tokopedia/BigGo listings and low-tier explainer pages; no CCEP
    confirmation; may be partly collector stock]. CCEP's 2024 investment
    plan mentions **new Indonesian lines making returnable glass** [MEDIUM
    — CCEP investment news, via search]. **Treat glass as plausible but
    unconfirmed** — check with CCEP Indonesia before briefing it.
  - **Fountain**: not researched.
- **Which formats fit which setting** (reference only, not a default):
  - warung, warteg, kaki lima, angkringan, Padang restaurant: a **390 mL
    PET** or a **330 mL can**; a small glass bottle if confirmed current
  - minimarket / on the go / office desk / ojek stop: a 250 mL slim can,
    a 330 mL can, a 390 mL PET
  - family dinner, buka puasa table, Lebaran open house, arisan: a **1 L
    or 1.5 L PET** in the midground with a glass of ice at each place
  - café or mall: a can, poured over ice into a plain glass
- **Ice**: Indonesians drink soft drinks cold, often over ice (es batu)
  in a plain glass [MEDIUM — not independently re-checked]. A glass of ice
  beside the can or bottle reads local; no second drink.
- **One hero product per scene** unless the brief asks for several.
- **Fanta and Sprite matter more here than Coca-Cola.** CCEP says
  **Sprite is usually Indonesia's most popular sparkling brand, and Fanta
  takes the top spot during Ramadan**; sparkling drinks are over 60% of
  CCEP Indonesia's volume [MEDIUM — CCEP "Meet the Markets: Indonesia",
  via search]. **Fanta Fruit Punch** (a green-pack Ramadan edition) has
  returned for Ramadan 2025 and 2026 [HIGH — coca-cola.com/id media
  centre, infobrand, Suara Merdeka].

eaten through the day — in Java the day's lauk are often cooked in the

steams; the lauk do not**. [MEDIUM direction — not independently
re-checked; EDITORIAL staging]

- **One plate of rice per person, shared lauk in the middle**, served
  with a serving spoon onto your own rice; the plate is a flat or

  description of nasi campur; plate size LOW, not re-checked].

### ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES

#### General environmental norms

- **Indonesia lives in landed houses; Jakarta is the partial exception.**
  **84.95% of Indonesian households owned their home in March 2024**,
  but **DKI Jakarta only 54.44%** — the lowest in the country, with
  renting (kontrak/sewa) common [HIGH — BPS via Databoks/Katadata and BPS
  Mataram]. No national census split of house vs apartment was found
  [LOW — gap]; but even in Jakarta's new-supply market **landed homes are
  ~55–60% against ~35–40% apartments**, and "high-rise housing is still a
  hard sell" — Indonesians strongly prefer landed housing with outdoor
  space [MEDIUM — JLL "The Indonesian dream: landed housing over
  condominiums"; Jakarta Post Feb 2026 citing JLL; Mordor Intelligence
  (tier 3)]. **So**: a **landed house** — a single-storey or two-storey
  row house in a **perumahan** (housing estate) or a **kampung** lane
  house — is the default everywhere; a **high-rise apartment** is a
  Jakarta/Surabaya young-professional option, not the norm; a **kos**
  (rented single room in a boarding house) is the student and
  young-worker default. [EDITORIAL inference]
- **§5.2 contrast**: like Thailand, Mexico and India, a landed-house
  country — unlike Türkiye or Spain — and with an even weaker apartment
  culture than Bangkok's.

- **17 August (Independence Day) neighbourhood**: lomba games, red-white
  bunting, a **tumpeng** (cone of yellow rice ringed by lauk) at the
  syukuran [MEDIUM — not re-checked].
- **§5.2 contrast**: grilling is real but not a backyard identity like
  the braai or carne asada — Indonesians buy grilled food (sate, ayam
  bakar) from stalls far more than they grill at home.

#### Scenario: Meal on the go — 1 person

**A plate of nasi goreng at a kaki lima cart** on a plastic stool at
night; **gorengan** on a sheet of paper on a cart ledge; **nasi bungkus**
opened on its paper on a bench; **bakso** at a gerobak; a **minimarket
(Indomaret/Alfamart) stop** — a 250 mL slim can and a packaged roti on a
bench outside (packaging unreadable); **nasi kucing** at an angkringan in
Yogyakarta. Hero (from the brief; formats that fit): a 330 mL or 250 mL
slim can, or a 390 mL PET on the counter or bench. **§5.2 contrast**: as
in Thailand and Mexico, street food is the everyday on-the-go meal, and
the **ojek (motorbike taxi) and delivery-rider** culture makes eating
bought food at home or work the norm. [MEDIUM — uncontested; EDITORIAL
composition]

#### Scenario: Away from home — 1 person at a restaurant/café

A **warteg** at 12:15: a plate of rice with three chosen lauk on a steel
or formica counter; a **Padang restaurant** with the hidang saucers in
front of one diner and a plate of rice (they would take two or three);
a **soto or bakso shop** with the condiment set (sambal, kecap, lime,
salt); a **mall food court** with ayam geprek. Hero: a can or a 390 mL
PET with a glass of ice. [EDITORIAL]

#### Scenario: Away from home — 2–3 people

Friends or family at a **rumah makan Padang** (the full hidang spread —
see the catalog), a **seafood or ikan bakar warung tenda** at night, a
**lesehan** Sundanese restaurant by a fishpond (gurame goreng, sayur
asem, lalapan, sambal), or a **sate stall**. Hero: two or three cans, or
a 1 L PET with glasses of ice. No beer, no es teh. [EDITORIAL]

by calculation in advance — the two sometimes differ by a day [HIGH —
detik, CNN Indonesia, Kompas]. As of drafting (2026-10-01) the
government's **SKB 3 Menteri for 2027 holidays was signed on 15 Sep
2026** [MEDIUM — Radar Semarang/Jawa Pos, Tirto, Tempo, detik

### Ramadan and buka puasa — staging guidance (EDITORIAL, flagged)

**What happens** [MEDIUM unless tagged]:
- Fasting from dawn (**imsak**, before subuh prayer ~04:30) to sunset
  (**maghrib**, ~18:00 in Jakarta — near the equator it barely moves
  through the year).
- **Ngabuburit** — the late-afternoon wait for maghrib; people stroll to
  **takjil markets** to buy sweets and snacks [MEDIUM — not re-checked].
- **Buka puasa** — at the maghrib call, the fast is broken **first with
  something light and sweet: dates (kurma), water, and a takjil** —
  **kolak** (banana, sweet potato and chewy **kolang-kaling** palm-fruit
  strips in palm-sugar coconut milk), **es buah** (mixed fruit in
  syrup and ice), **es campur**, **bubur sumsum**, **kurma**; kolak is
  "always" a Ramadan takjil and "pairs with dates" [HIGH for kolak as
  takjil — Wikipedia "Kolak (food)", detik, Tokopedia, Liputan6,
  ANTARA]. Then maghrib prayer, then the **main meal** (rice and lauk,
  often gorengan, soup, ayam, and the family's favourite dishes)
  [MEDIUM — not re-checked; uncontested].
- **Bukber (buka bersama)** — breaking the fast together with friends,
  colleagues or schoolmates at restaurants and cafés is a huge social
  occasion; restaurants are fully booked at 17:30 [MEDIUM — not
  re-checked].
- **Sahur** — the pre-dawn meal (~03:00–04:00): rice, eggs, simple lauk
  (morning module).
- **TCCC's own Ramadan role**: Fanta is the brand that takes the top spot
  in Ramadan, with a special Fanta Fruit Punch edition and buka puasa
  "mix" campaigns (see ICONIC BEVERAGES) [HIGH].

**Staging rules for any Ramadan scene (EDITORIAL — needs Fernando and
TCCC Indonesia sign-off):**
1. **Never the product as the first thing that breaks the fast.** Do not
   show the hero opened, poured or in focus at the moment of the adhan,
   and never alone on a table where dates/water/takjil are absent.
   **Dates, a takjil bowl (kolak or es buah) and the meal must be
   visibly present and visibly first** in the composition; the hero sits
   among the main-meal dishes, not in front of the dates.
2. **Two safe moments**: (a) **just before maghrib** — the table set and
   untouched, food covered with a tudung saji or cling film, the light
   golden-orange at dusk through the window, nobody eating or drinking;
   or (b) **later in the meal, after prayer** — the takjil bowls already
   half-finished, the family at the main course, the hero unopened or in
   glasses of ice among rice plates.
3. **Nobody shown eating or drinking in daylight** in a Ramadan-coded
   scene; no product in hand (project rule anyway).
4. **No prayer in progress, no mosque interior, no Qur'an, no prayer
   mat** near the product (hard rule 7). Mosque domes or a lit minaret as
   soft background bokeh through a window are acceptable only if the
   brief asks [EDITORIAL].
5. **Dress**: family in everyday Muslim dress (baju koko, hijab, peci,
   sarung) — not costume; mixed with plain T-shirts and housedresses
   (daster) at home. [EDITORIAL]
6. **Takjil sizes** (for §4.7 consistency): kolak in a small bowl ~10–12
   cm, es buah in a glass bowl or tall glass — **a tall glass of es buah
   is a drink-like intruder; prefer a bowl**; dates in a small saucer,
   5–7 dates [EDITORIAL].

### Lebaran (Idul Fitri)

- **Mudik** — tens of millions travel home to their villages; then
  **Shalat Ied** in the morning, **sungkem / halal bihalal** (asking
  forgiveness of elders, kissing hands), and an **open house** of visits
  [MEDIUM — Wikipedia "Lebaran"; practice uncontested].
- **The Lebaran table**: **ketupat** (rice cooked in woven young-coconut-
  leaf diamonds, cut open into pale pressed-rice cubes), **opor ayam**
  (chicken in pale yellow-white coconut-milk curry), **sambal goreng ati
  kentang** (red-brown chilli-fried beef liver and potato cubes), **sayur
  labu siam/lodeh** (chayote in coconut broth), **rendang**, **emping**
  and krupuk; and jars of **kue kering** — **nastar** (pineapple-jam
  tarts), **kastengel** (cheese sticks), **putri salju** (sugar-dusted
  crescents) — in lidded plastic jars on the living-room table [HIGH for
  the core dishes — Wikipedia "Lebaran", "Opor ayam", "Lontong"; Tempo,
  IDN Times recipes; kue kering MEDIUM — not re-checked].
- **Staging**: the living-room or dining table with the ketupat spread,
  family in matching new clothes (baju Lebaran) softly in the
  background; a **1.5 L PET** fits the open-house register; **no sungkem
  ritual in focus** with the product. **Ketupat hangs in bunches as
  decoration** — real, photogenic. [EDITORIAL]

### Other occasions

- **Idul Adha** — qurban animals slaughtered at mosques; families cook
  **sate kambing** and **gulai kambing**; **never stage the sacrifice or
  raw meat distribution**; the evening sate on the teras is fine.
  [EDITORIAL; practice MEDIUM — not re-checked]
- **Imlek** — Chinese-Indonesian family dinner (lapis legit, kue
  keranjang, bandeng, mie panjang umur), red lanterns, oranges; a
  barongsai (lion dance) in soft background; **never an altar**. Pork
  is possible in Chinese-Indonesian homes — only if the brief names it.
  [LOW-MEDIUM — not re-checked]
- **Nyepi (Bali)** — the day itself is silent and dark: **no staging on
  Nyepi day**; the **ogoh-ogoh** evening before is spectacle (keep the
  product away from the effigies); the day after (**Ngembak Geni**) is
  family visiting. [EDITORIAL]
- **Galungan (Bali)** — penjor bamboo poles along every road, temple
  visits in white and sarong; **lawar and babi guling** are the feast
  (pork exception — brief must name it); **never the product near a
  temple, offering or penjor base**. [EDITORIAL]
- **Christmas (Manado, Batak, Papua, NTT)** — open houses, kue, and in
  non-Muslim homes pork dishes; brief must name the household (hard
  rule 2). [LOW-MEDIUM — not re-checked]
- **17 Agustus** — **tumpeng** (a cone of yellow turmeric rice on a
  rattan tray lined with banana leaf, ringed by ayam goreng, perkedel,
  urap, telur, tempe) cut at a syukuran; kampung games (krupuk-eating
  races, sack races, panjat pinang); red-white everywhere. [MEDIUM — not
  re-checked]
- **Football and badminton on TV** — nonton bareng (watching together)
  at a warung kopi is a genuine occasion; no team logos legible.
  [LOW — not re-checked]

### A. National core — Jakarta and Java everyday

#### Nasi goreng (Indonesian fried rice)

- **Category**: Everyday — any meal; the night kaki lima dish; breakfast
  from yesterday's rice. **Halal status**: **halal** (chicken, egg,
  prawn, beef; Chinese-Indonesian carts may use pork/lard — never in a
  default scene).
- **Lineage**: Chinese-derived fried rice localised with **kecap manis**
  (sweet soy) and/or **terasi** (shrimp paste) and chilli — "stronger and
  spicier than Chinese fried rice" [HIGH — id.wikipedia "Nasi goreng
  (Indonesia)"; recipe sources].
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **nasi goreng kecap/biasa** (brown, sweet-soy —
  the Jakarta cart default); **nasi goreng merah** (Makassar, red from
  tomato/chilli sauce); **nasi goreng kampung** (terasi, paler, chilli);
  **nasi goreng Semarang** (light kecap); **nasi goreng gila** (Jakarta,
  topped with sausage, meatball and veg); **nasi goreng kambing**
  (Jakarta, goat, Middle-Eastern spices); **nasi goreng ikan asin**.
  **Default when unspecified**: kecap-brown, chicken, with telur ceplok.
  [EDITORIAL]
- **Format**: one plate.
- **Vessel & scale**: white china or melamine plate ~24–26 cm; at carts,
  on a plate lined with brown paper; takeaway in kertas nasi.
- **Texture & finish**: rice grains **separate, glossy, mottled
  caramel-to-mid-brown** from kecap (not uniformly dark), wok-scorched
  bits, small shreds of chicken, scrambled egg flecks, sliced spring
  onion; topped with a **telur ceplok** (crisp lacy edges, runny yolk)
  and a scatter of **crisp golden fried shallots**; at the side **2–3
  krupuk**, **acar** (cubed cucumber, carrot, shallot and chilli in
  vinegar — pale) and cucumber and tomato slices [HIGH for egg, krupuk,
  acar, fried shallots — id.wikipedia; recipe sources].
- **Model failure**: Chinese yang-chow fried rice with peas and diced
  carrots (pale, yellow); Thai khao pad with a lime wedge; a pineapple-
  boat "nasi goreng"; a mound so dark it reads as burnt; satay skewers
  on top (a hotel buffet convention — only if briefed).
- **Confusable**: Malaysian nasi goreng (near-identical — the
  differentiator is the setting and the krupuk-and-acar sides, not the
  rice) [EDITORIAL].
- **Confidence**: HIGH (form); LOW (sizes).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one plate.
  - **What dominates**: rice ~60% of the visible plate; fried egg ~20%;
    krupuk ~10%; acar and cucumber ~10%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Fried rice | heap or bowl-moulded dome ~14 cm across, ~5–6 cm high (~300 g) | 1 | Caramel-brown mottled grains, some darker scorched, spring-onion green flecks, small chicken shreds (1–2 cm), egg flecks | Centre |
    | Telur ceplok | ~10–12 cm with frill; yolk ~4 cm | 1 | Lacy crisp brown edge, white blistered, glossy orange yolk | On top of the rice, slightly off-centre |
    | Fried shallots | flakes 3–8 mm | a pinch (~1 tbsp) | Crisp, golden-brown, curled | Scattered over rice and egg |
    | Krupuk udang | ~8–10 cm, irregular | 2–3 | Pale pink-white, puffed, honeycombed | Tucked at the rim, leaning on the rice |
    | Acar | cubes ~1 cm | ~2 tbsp | Translucent pale green cucumber, orange carrot, a red chilli | Small heap at the plate edge |
    | Cucumber & tomato | slices ~3–4 cm | 3 + 2 | Fresh green rind; red | Along the rim |
    | Spoon and fork | — | 1 each | Stainless | On the rim |

  - **Arrangement**: rice mound centre, egg crowning it, crackers and
    acar on one side; white plate visible around 25–30% of the rim.
  - **Vessel fill**: ~70% of the plate.
  - **Served portion vs. whole**: one person's plate is the whole dish.
  - **State cues**: steam rising; oil sheen on the egg frill; crackers
    crisp, not soggy (not touching the egg yolk).
  - **Absent on purpose**: peas and diced carrot in the rice, pineapple,
    lime wedge, chopsticks, knife, prawn crackers as a big fan of ten.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A white plate a little over twice the can's
    height across: a heaped mound of fried rice, mottled caramel brown
    with darker scorched grains, small chicken shreds and spring onion,
    crowned with a fried egg about the can's height across, its edges
    lacy and crisp brown, yolk runny; crisp golden fried shallots
    scattered over; two puffed pale prawn crackers leaning on the rice;
    a small heap of pale pickled cucumber and carrot and cucumber slices
    at the edge; spoon and fork on the rim."

#### Mie goreng (fried noodles) — compact

- **Category**: Everyday — cart, warung, home; instant **Indomie Mi
  Goreng** at home and at **warmindo** (instant-noodle warung) is a
  genuine national meal [MEDIUM — not re-checked; uncontested].
  **Halal status**: halal (chicken, egg).
- **Form**: yellow wheat noodles (mie kuning) stir-fried with kecap
  manis, garlic, cabbage, sawi (choy sum), egg, chicken; fried shallots,
  krupuk and acar; **mie goreng Jawa** cooked over charcoal is wetter and
  smoky. Instant version: curly noodles with packet seasoning, often a
  telur ceplok and sliced cabbage/chilli.
- **Model failure**: Chinese chow mein, Thai pad see ew (wide noodles),
  lo mein.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)**: noodles ~65%, egg/fried egg
  ~15%, greens ~10%, krupuk/acar ~10%; noodles round, ~2 mm thick,
  glossy mid-brown, a tangled mound ~14 cm across × 6 cm high on a 24 cm
  plate; cabbage shreds and sawi leaves 2–4 cm, scattered; chicken
  shreds 1–2 cm; a fried egg on top optional; fried shallots; 2 krupuk;
  **absent**: bean sprouts heap, chopsticks (spoon and fork), lime. **Prompt
  line**: "A plate a little over twice the can's height across with a
  tangled mound of thin, round, glossy mid-brown fried wheat noodles,
  green cabbage and choy sum leaves, small chicken shreds and flecks of
  egg, crisp fried shallots scattered on top, two pale prawn crackers
  and a little pickled cucumber at the side, spoon and fork on the rim."
  [EDITORIAL]

  delivery favourite. **Halal status**: **halal**.

  sambal; related to East Javanese **ayam penyet** [HIGH — Wikipedia
  "Ayam geprek", nomadette, Spice Island Indonesia].
- **Form**: battered, **shatteringly crisp fried chicken smashed** with a
  pestle into a raw **sambal bawang** (bird's-eye chilli, garlic,
  shallot, salt) in a cobek or on the plate; served with rice, sliced
  cucumber, sometimes lalapan, fried tofu/tempeh; modern versions add
  melted mozzarella ("geprek keju") [HIGH for the core — same sources;

  more or less red; **geprek in the cobek** vs. on a plate or rattan

  melamine plate ~24 cm; at the famous stalls, served **in the cobek**
  (~18 cm).

#### Bakso (meatball soup)

- **Category**: Everyday — the national cart snack-meal; afternoon and
  evening. **Halal status**: **halal** (beef or chicken; pork bakso
  exists in Chinese-Indonesian and Balinese contexts — never default).
- **Lineage**: Chinese-Indonesian origin (from Hokkien "bak-so"),
  fully nationalised [MEDIUM — Wikipedia "Bakso"].
- **Form**: **springy beef meatballs** in **clear, light-gold beef
  broth** with **yellow noodles and/or rice vermicelli (bihun)**, sawi
  greens, celery leaf, fried shallots; extras: fried wonton (pangsit),
  tofu (tahu bakso), and a **big bakso urat** (with tendon) or **bakso
  telur** (egg inside); table condiments: **sambal** (bright orange-red
  liquid chilli), **kecap manis**, **saus** (tomato-chilli sauce), lime
  [HIGH for meatball sizes — Wikipedia "Bakso" ("marble to ping-pong";
  tennis-ball bakso urat/telur); others MEDIUM].
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **bakso biasa** (5–8 small balls), **bakso urat**
  (one large knobbly ball with tendon bits), **bakso Malang** (East Java,
  with fried wonton and siomay), **bakso beranak** (a giant ball with
  small balls inside), **bakso bakar** (grilled). **Default**: bakso
  biasa with one bakso urat. [EDITORIAL]
- **Vessel & scale**: a ~16–18 cm bowl, often white with a printed
  rooster or flower (keep vague); a porcelain or steel spoon and a fork.
- **Texture & finish**: meatballs **pale grey-brown, smooth and
  slightly bouncy-looking** (fine, uniform, not crumbly), bakso urat
  **rougher, knobbly with flecks of tendon**; broth clear with fat
  droplets on the surface; noodles yellow; bihun translucent white.
- **Model failure**: Italian meatballs in tomato sauce; Swedish
  meatballs in cream; Vietnamese pho with herbs and lime basket; ramen
  with chashu and egg.
- **Confidence**: HIGH (form and ball size); LOW (bowl size).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one bowl.
  - **What dominates**: broth surface ~40%; meatballs ~30%; noodles
    ~20%; greens and garnish ~10%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Small bakso | **2.5–3 cm** | 5–6 | Smooth, pale grey-brown, round | Half-submerged, clustered |
    | Bakso urat (optional) | **~5–6 cm** | 1 | Knobbly, darker, tendon flecks | Centre, standing proud of the broth |
    | Broth | ~7 cm deep | — | Clear, light gold, fat droplets | Fills the bowl to ~1.5 cm below the rim |
    | Yellow noodles + bihun | small nests | 1 + 1 | Yellow; translucent white | Under the balls, ends visible |
    | Sawi greens | leaves 4–5 cm | 2–3 | Bright green | At one side |
    | Celery leaf, fried shallots | flakes | pinch | Green specks; golden flakes | Floating |
    | Tahu / pangsit goreng (optional) | 4–5 cm | 1–2 | Golden puffed | Edge |

  - **Arrangement**: balls clustered in the centre, the big ball
    highest; greens on one side; garnish scattered.
  - **Vessel fill**: broth to ~85% of the bowl depth.
  - **Served portion vs. whole**: one bowl per person.
  - **State cues**: steam rising; fat droplets glinting; condiment
    bottles (unlabelled) and a saucer of chilli at the side.

  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A white soup bowl about 1.5 times the can's

#### Martabak manis (terang bulan — thick sweet pancake)

- **Category**: Everyday — night street snack, family treat brought
  home, Ramadan. **Halal status**: **halal**.
- **Lineage**: Indonesian (Bangka/Chinese-Indonesian roots); called
  **martabak manis** in Jakarta/west, **terang bulan** in East Java and
  elsewhere — same food [HIGH — Wikipedia "Martabak manis"; RRI;
  Kompasiana; viva].
- **Form**: a yeasted batter cooked in a **heavy round pan** until thick,
  **spongy with many air holes (a honeycomb crumb)** and crisp-edged;
  brushed generously with butter/margarine, sprinkled with sugar and
  toppings — **chocolate sprinkles (meses), crushed peanuts, sweetened
  condensed milk, grated cheese** (classic combos: cokelat-kacang,
  keju-susu) — modern: Nutella, Oreo, matcha, red velvet; **folded in
  half** and cut into pieces [HIGH for the form — Wikipedia, RRI,
  Radar Banyuwangi; sizes LOW].
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **tebal** (thick, default) vs **tipis kering**
  (thin crispy); classic vs modern toppings. **Default**: thick, half
  chocolate sprinkle-peanut, half cheese.
- **Vessel & scale**: whole martabak ~25–30 cm across before folding;
  sold in a **cardboard box** (printed — keep plain); at home tipped
  onto a plate or eaten from the open box.
- **Texture & finish**: **cut face shows a thick (~3 cm) golden crumb
  full of vertical tunnel-holes**, a **dark-brown caramelised bottom
  crust**, a **glistening layer of melted butter** at the fold, the
  filling (dark chocolate sprinkles and peanut rubble, or white grated
  cheese and condensed milk) sandwiched in the fold.
- **Model failure**: American pancake stack; Dutch poffertjes; a thin
  crêpe; a pizza.
- **Confidence**: HIGH (form); LOW (sizes).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — a half martabak, cut, on a
  plate or in its box.
  - **What dominates**: golden cake crumb (cut faces and top) ~65%;

#### Padang hidang spread (rumah makan Padang table service)

- **Category**: Everyday to celebratory — the national eat-out meal.
  **Halal status**: **halal** (Minang cuisine is strictly halal).
- **Lineage**: Minangkabau; Padang restaurants are everywhere in
  Indonesia and beyond [HIGH — Wikipedia "Padang cuisine", "Nasi
  padang"].
- **Form — hidang service**: after seating, **waiters bring a dozen or
  so small plates unprompted, carrying many stacked on one arm
  (manatiang — 10+ plates)**; diners eat what they want and **pay only
  for what they touched**; untouched plates go back [HIGH — Wikipedia,
  note.com (Tai), live2makan, homhaute]. **The usual dozen**: **beef
  rendang, gulai ikan (curried fish), gulai daun singkong (cassava
  leaves in coconut), terung balado (chilli eggplant), gulai otak/
  liver, gulai tunjang (tendon) or babat (tripe), paru goreng (crisp
  fried beef lung), ayam pop / ayam goreng, dendeng balado (crisp beef
  with chilli), telur dadar (thick omelette), perkedel, and sambal
  (sambal ijo green and sambal merah)** [HIGH for the list — Wikipedia
  "Nasi padang"]. **Rice comes on a separate plate per person**, and
  **gulai sauce, cassava leaves and sambal ijo are spooned on the rice**
  by default [MEDIUM — not re-checked].
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **hidang** (the table of plates — default for a
  sit-down scene) vs **pesan / ramas** (one plate: rice with 2–3 chosen
  lauk and sauces poured) vs **nasi bungkus** (takeaway in paper, often
  with more rice). **Nasi kapau** (Bukittinggi) is a cousin with dishes
  ladled from long-handled spoons at a stall [MEDIUM — Wikipedia "Nasi
  kapau"].
- **Vessel & scale**: **small white plates, oval or round, ~15–18 cm**,
  some with a blue or green rim; a kobokan bowl; a pitcher of water or

- **Lineage**: Javanese-Indonesian Lebaran tradition [HIGH — Wikipedia
  "Lebaran", "Opor ayam", "Lontong"; Tempo, IDN Times].

  young-coconut-leaf pouches and boiled until pressed), **opor ayam**

  ati kentang** (beef liver and potato cubes fried in **red-brown chilli

#### Tumpeng (celebratory rice cone) — compact

- Javanese **syukuran** (thanksgiving) centrepiece — 17 August,
  birthdays, office openings: a **cone of yellow turmeric rice (nasi
  kuning) or uduk rice** on a **round rattan tray (tampah) lined with
  banana leaf**, ringed by **ayam goreng, perkedel, telur balado or
  sliced omelette, tempe orek, urap (vegetables with spiced coconut),

  cone ~40% (cone ~25–30 cm tall, ~20 cm base — about 2.5 cans tall),

#### Bubur ayam (chicken rice porridge) — compact
