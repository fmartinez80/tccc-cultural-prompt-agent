## FILE ROLE & METHOD

This is the ninth regional file built under the 13-file US structure. `us.md`'s FILE ROLE & METHOD table records why New Mexico was split out of the original `us-desert-southwest.md` slot: a dedicated Wikipedia article/category for New Mexican cuisine distinct from both Tex-Mex and Sonoran-style Mexican food, a specialist culinary school (Santa Fe School of Cooking) explicitly framing the comparison as "Tex-Mex vs. New Mexican" — not "vs. Desert Southwest" — and Arizona's own Sonoran style being independently documented as its own distinct thing. This file's job is to build out that distinctness with full research depth, confirming the split was the right call.

It holds full, authoritative depth for `us.md`'s existing pointer:

1. **New Mexican enchiladas** (`us.md` Mexican-American/Enchiladas section) — `us.md` already carries substantial, HIGH-confidence New Mexico-specific depth (stacked construction, red/green/Christmas chile, the fried-egg crown, blue corn tortillas in northern NM, the "Red or Green?" state question). This file builds on, not re-derives, that existing depth — adding real-world scale (mandatory per `country-file-schema.md` §4.5), additional texture/visual detail, and the Hatch-chile geographic-designation depth `us.md`'s comparison-focused entry didn't need in full.

It also adds a large body of New Mexico-native dishes with no variant elsewhere in the US and therefore no `us.md` pointer at all (the same treatment `us-texas.md`'s fajitas/queso/chicken-fried-steak/kolaches got): sopapillas, posole, carne adovada, green chile stew, the green chile cheeseburger, biscochitos, tamales, and Pueblo horno bread — plus New Mexico's own side of the Frito pie origin dispute, written to be consistent with (not contradicting) `us-texas.md`'s existing entry.

**Scope inherited from `us.md`**: lunch, dinner, and snacks only (breakfast excluded); inclusion by staging relevance, not raw consumption frequency. Biscochitos (a cookie/snack, near-universally a holiday item but also sold and eaten as an everyday bakery item) and sopapillas (served with both entrees and as dessert, not confined to any single meal occasion) both clear this scope on the same all-day/multi-occasion logic `us-texas.md` used for kolaches.

**Structural decision — one file, no internal zone split, but a real, sourced, documented internal north/south gradient carried as a callout, not a further split.** Unlike Texas's BBQ four-way split (a structurally different case — different proteins, methods, and occasions, resolved in `us-texas.md` as a dish-level §4.6 variant table specifically because it stayed within one dish family without becoming a whole second cuisine), New Mexico's north/south variation stays within the same dish family (chile-sauced New Mexican cooking generally) and the same basic plate grammar (stacked enchiladas, posole, chile-smothered everything) — it's a difference of *which chile landrace and corn color*, not a different cuisine. This is documented via the enchilada entry's own variant table and the Hatch-chile/Chimayó-chile geographic-designation entry, following the identical §4.6 non-hierarchical-variant treatment `us-texas.md` established. This decision should be revisited if a future, more deeply sourced pass finds the internal variation runs deeper than a shared-dish-family gradient — see CANDIDATE QUEUE.

**A note on Indigenous-heritage sourcing discipline, following this project's own established precedent** (`us-appalachia.md`'s ramps/leather-britches entries): where a claim attributes a food tradition to a specific Pueblo, Navajo, or Apache origin, this file states the claim's evidentiary tier honestly — distinguishing tribally-affiliated or academic sourcing from journalism/enthusiast-tier retellings — rather than treating a repeated claim as settled fact merely because it recurs across food-blog-tier sources. ## ZONE CHARACTERIZATION

New Mexico clears the "would swapping this region's norms into Texas or Arizona look visibly wrong" test on multiple independent, causally-explained grounds — not merely a different chile preference layered onto the same underlying cuisine.

**A distinct, earlier, and differently-structured Spanish colonial settlement than Texas's.** Juan de Oñate led the first Spanish colonizing expedition into present-day New Mexico in 1598, founding a permanent colonial presence and blazing El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro, a 1,500-mile royal road connecting Santa Fe to Mexico City that operated for roughly 300 years (1598–1885) and is itself a US National Historic Trail. Santa Fe was founded as the colonial capital in 1610 by Governor Pedro de Peralta, making it the oldest continuously-occupied state capital in the United States — its Palace of the Governors (1610) is the oldest continuously-used public building constructed by European settlers in the US. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [NPS — El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/el-camino-real-de-tierra-adentro-timeline.htm); [Legends of America — Santa Fe, New Mexico](https://www.legendsofamerica.com/nm-santafe/); [Britannica: Santa Fe](https://www.britannica.com/place/Santa-Fe-New-Mexico)] This is a genuinely different settlement timeline and mechanism from Texas's: Texas's own Spanish mission era (San Antonio's missions, founded from 1718) and its defining Anglo-American ranching/cattle-drive economy came more than a century later and followed a different trajectory (Texas Revolution 1836, US annexation 1845) than New Mexico's continuous, much-earlier Spanish colonial province. New Mexico's Spanish-colonial food and material culture (adobe construction, wheat introduced by the Spanish, the horno oven, biscochitos' Spanish-cookie ancestry) is a much older, more continuously-layered inheritance than Tex-Mex's 19th-century Tejano-adaptation story. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]

**Pueblo/Native heritage predates Spanish contact and remains a living, present-day culinary and architectural force, not a historical footnote.** New Mexico is home to 19 Pueblo Nations (plus the Navajo Nation and Apache tribes/nations), each with its own distinct culinary traditions — corn (including the specifically sacred/ceremonial status of blue corn), beans, squash, and chile cultivation by Indigenous peoples in the region predates the Spanish arrival, and nixtamalized corn (hominy, the basis of posole) is itself a pre-Spanish Indigenous food-processing technology, not a Spanish import. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Tomasitas — History of New Mexican Cuisine](https://tomasitas.com/the-history-of-new-mexican-cuisine-how-chile-corn-and-tradition-built-a-regional-food-identity/); [Beyond Mere Sustenance — Chimayo, New Mexico and Red Chile Posole](https://beyondmeresustenance.com/chimayo-new-mexico-and-red-chile-posole/)] The horno (outdoor adobe bread oven) tradition remains an actively practiced, present-tense feature of Pueblo life today, not a museum artifact — bakers still fire hornos with cedar for family, feast-day, and market bread. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Atlas Obscura — In New Mexico, Bakers Keep the Tradition of Pueblo Bread Alive](https://www.atlasobscura.com/foods/pueblo-bread)] Neither Texas nor Arizona has an equivalent, this-specific combination of 19 distinct, present-day Pueblo nations shaping the state's food and architectural identity — Arizona's Indigenous culinary influence (Tohono O'odham, among others) is real but feeds into the distinctly different Sonoran-style tradition documented separately in `us-desert-southwest.md`.

**A specific, unique, and official state-level acknowledgment of the chile fork as the state's defining culinary marker.** "Red or Green?" was adopted by the New Mexico State Legislature in 1996 as the state's official question — the only official state question of any US state — explicitly to recognize the chile industry's economic and cultural importance. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [statesymbolsusa.org — New Mexico State Question](https://statesymbolsusa.org/symbol-official-item/new-mexico/uncategorized/new-mexico-state-question)] New Mexico separately designated chile and frijoles (pinto beans) as joint official state vegetables in 1965 — the first state in the nation to designate an official state vegetable at all, originating from a high-school student's legislative proposal. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [statesymbolsusa.org — Chile and Frijoles](https://statesymbolsusa.org/symbol-official-item/new-mexico/state-food-agriculture-symbol/chile-and-frijoles); [Santa Fe New Mexican — Legislative battle 50 years ago](https://www.santafenewmexican.com/life/features/legislative-battle-50-years-ago-ended-in-compromise-chile-and-beans-as-official-state-veggies/article_a51b6818-1194-5b39-89da-7a11f77cb661.html)] No comparably specific, chile-centered official state symbol exists for Texas or Arizona — Texas's official state dish is chili (a beef stew that specifically excludes chile pods as a sauce base; see `us-texas.md`), a structurally different food-identity marker.

**A specific chile-growing agricultural economy tied to a named, geographically-bounded valley.** New Mexico produces roughly a third of America's total chile pepper production (33%), with 2025 production valued at approximately $54.8 million across roughly 8,775 harvested acres (green and red combined). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [USDA NASS — 2025 New Mexico Chile Production](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/New_Mexico/Publications/Special_Interest_Reports/NM-2025%20Chile%20Production-03062026.pdf)] This is a genuine, checkable economic-geographic fact (not just a cultural preference) that has no equivalent in Texas's or Arizona's food economies at the same specificity and scale.

**Internal variation, validated rather than assumed: a real northern/southern gradient, but not a structural cuisine split.**
- **Northern New Mexico** (Santa Fe, Taos, Chimayó, Española Valley, the Rio Grande's upper valley): blue corn tortillas are specifically a northern New Mexico phenomenon, long grown as a Native American staple crop and prized for its earthy flavor; northern restaurants use blue corn tortillas in enchiladas and other dishes in a way "never seen" in the south. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — a single detailed source, but internally consistent with the broader landrace-chile geography below] [SOURCE: [New Mexico Magazine — Not Mexican food. Not Tex-Mex.](https://www.newmexicomagazine.org/blog/post/best-new-mexico-foods/)] Chimayó red chile — a specific landrace pepper tied to the soil and climate of north-central New Mexico, one of at least sixteen recognized New Mexico landrace chiles (others include Alcalde, Cochiti Pueblo, Escondida, Jemez Pueblo, Velarde, Zia Pueblo — several directly named for specific Pueblo communities), each developed over 400+ years of seed-saving by Native American Pueblo and Hispano communities — carries its own Geographic Indication designation and USPTO certification mark (granted 2009), distinct from and predating Hatch's own certification effort. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: Chimayo pepper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chimayo_pepper); [NMSU — The Landrace Chiles of Northern New Mexico](https://pubs.nmsu.edu/_circulars/CR679/)]
- **Southern New Mexico** (Hatch Valley, Las Cruces, the lower Rio Grande valley): Hatch green chile — the state's single most nationally famous chile export — is grown specifically in the Hatch Valley and is most closely associated with cultivars released by New Mexico State University in Las Cruces; this is a chile-farming-economy-driven register (irrigation agriculture, processing/packing infrastructure, the NMSU chile-breeding program) distinct from the north's older, smaller-scale landrace/heirloom-seed tradition. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [New Mexico Magazine — Not Mexican food. Not Tex-Mex.](https://www.newmexicomagazine.org/blog/post/best-new-mexico-foods/)]
- **This gradient does not rise to a structural zone split** on the same test `us-texas.md` applied to its own internal BBQ variation: northern and southern New Mexico share the same core dish grammar (stacked enchiladas, chile-smothered plates, posole, sopapillas, the "red or green?" ritual itself) — the difference is which chile landrace and which corn color, a variant-table-level distinction (per `country-file-schema.md` §4.6), not a different cuisine, different primary protein, or different serving format the way Texas's four BBQ regions were. Documented as an explicit callout inside the relevant dish entries (Enchiladas, Hatch/Chimayó chile) rather than a further split — see CANDIDATE QUEUE for what would change this.

**What this zone characterization is not claiming.** New Mexico is not internally uniform, and it should not be flattened into a single "Southwest" register with Texas or Arizona, nor should its own internal north/south gradient be flattened into one one-size-fits-all "New Mexican" default. **Caricature-avoidance note (editorial judgment, not a sourced claim):** not every New Mexico scene should default to a rural desert/adobe-hacienda register — Albuquerque is a large, modern metro area (pop. ~560,000) with ordinary suburban and urban housing stock alongside its historic and Pueblo-Revival-styled buildings, and a contemporary Albuquerque strip-mall New Mexican restaurant is at least as representative a setting as a Santa Fe adobe courtyard. Equally, avoid over-correcting into treating New Mexican food as interchangeable with Tex-Mex or Sonoran-style Mexican food just because all three involve chile and tortillas — the specific, sourced distinctions above (stacked vs. rolled construction, chile-pod sauce vs. roux-based gravy, landrace/GI-protected chile varieties, the state-level "Red or Green?" institutional marker) are what make New Mexico visibly wrong to swap with either neighbor.

## TRUSTED CONTENT

- **"Red or Green?" is a real, near-universal ordering ritual in New Mexican restaurants, not a rare or ceremonial phrase — expect a server to ask it, or a diner to volunteer "Christmas," whenever a chile-sauced dish is ordered.** [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [statesymbolsusa.org — New Mexico State Question](https://statesymbolsusa.org/symbol-official-item/new-mexico/uncategorized/new-mexico-state-question)]
- **Chile in New Mexican cooking functions as the dish's structural center, not a condiment or accent — a genuine, sourced point of contrast with Tex-Mex.** New Mexican chile is "simmered into sauces, slow-roasted, and layered," forming the base of the dish itself, whereas Tex-Mex leans on the jalapeño as a "sharp accent" added to an otherwise dairy/cumin-forward dish. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Food Republic — New Mexican Vs Traditional Mexican Cuisine](https://www.foodrepublic.com/1738314/new-mexico-vs-traditional-mexico-cuisine-differences/)]
- **Red and green chile are the same plant at two different points of ripeness, not two different varieties — a fact that should govern how both are depicted relative to each other in any "Christmas" scene.** Green chile is harvested while still unripe (typically picked in mid-to-late summer); left on the plant, the same pepper turns red as it fully ripens — chlorophyll breaks down and carotenoids develop, producing the color change, while capsaicin and natural sugar levels both rise with ripening (red chile is generally regarded as hotter and sweeter/richer than green). Red chile is typically dried (on the vine, or later strung into ristras — see Environment section) rather than used fresh, while green chile is used fresh/roasted; a single field can yield an early green harvest and a later red harvest from chiles left to mature past frost. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Hatch Chile Store — Green Chile vs. Red Chile: What Changes When Chile Ripens](https://www.hatch-green-chile.com/blogs/hatch/green-chile-vs-red-chile-ripening-guide); [NMSU — H-230: Growing Chiles in New Mexico](https://pubs.nmsu.edu/_h/H230/)]
- **Blue corn is a nutritionally and culturally distinct ingredient from yellow/white corn, not a cosmetic color choice.** It is higher in protein than yellow or white corn, considered sacred among Southwestern Indigenous communities (symbolizing strength, harmony, and long life in Navajo tradition specifically), and traditionally prepared with juniper ash (which also imparts a blue-gray color shift and adds calcium). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the Navajo cultural framing; MEDIUM for how directly this specific symbolism carries into New Mexican-restaurant blue-corn tortilla use, which is a more commercialized, less ceremonial context] [SOURCE: [Tastes From The Road — Blue Corn Mush](https://www.tastesfromtheroad.com/post/blue-corn-mush-a-taste-of-the-navajo-nation); [Episcopal Church — Traditional Diné Blue Maize Mush](https://www.episcopalchurch.org/evangelisminitiatives/traditional-dine-blue-maize-mush/)]
- **Posole's hominy base is a pre-Spanish Indigenous food-processing technology (nixtamalization), not a Spanish-introduced ingredient — pork and European spices were what the Spanish added to an already-Indigenous dish, not the reverse.** [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Beyond Mere Sustenance — Chimayo, New Mexico and Red Chile Posole](https://beyondmeresustenance.com/chimayo-new-mexico-and-red-chile-posole/)]

## VISUAL & PLATING NORMS

- **New Mexican plating reads as more monochromatic and chile-pod-forward than Tex-Mex's dairy-and-cheddar palette** — a thin, matte, earthy brick-red (red chile) or a chunky, bright yellowish-green (green chile) sauce dominates the plate, rather than Tex-Mex's glossy reddish-brown roux gravy blanketed under melted yellow cheddar. New Mexican dishes show more visible chile-pod texture (flecks of roasted chile skin, visible seeds) and less uniform "smothered" gloss.
- **The stacked (not rolled) construction is New Mexico's single most checkable enchilada authenticity marker** — see `us.md`'s existing entry and the Enchiladas dish entry below for the full cross-section detail. A New Mexican enchilada plate should never show tightly rolled cylinders; it should show a flat, layered, lasagna-like stack with visible tortilla-layer edges.
- **The fried egg crown, when present, should be sunny-side-up with a runny-to-just-set yolk** — a New Mexican-specific enchilada topping with no Tex-Mex equivalent (see `us.md`).
- **"Christmas" (both sauces on one plate) should show a genuine, visible two-tone division** — red chile pooled on one portion of the dish, green chile on another, not blended into a single orange-brown color; the two sauces are typically kept in visually distinct zones (e.g., ladled over different halves of a stacked-enchilada plate, or served in separate small ramekins alongside a shared entree) rather than mixed.
- **Texture/finish specifically**: red chile sauce is thin, matte, and slightly grainy (made from dried ground or reconstituted whole chile pods, not a roux) — it should look like a thinned purée, not a glossy, thickened gravy. Green chile sauce is chunkier and more visibly textured, showing small pieces of roasted, charred chile flesh (sometimes visible thin black char marks on individual chile pieces) suspended in a lighter, less opaque sauce. Sopapillas should show a matte, slightly domed, evenly golden-brown fried surface with visible small irregular blisters, not a glossy or perfectly smooth surface, and a soft, hollow, almost bread-like (not crisp/craggy) crumb-plus-crust structure when broken open — a texture more like a yeasted popover than a crisp fried chip. Biscochitos should show a pale, matte, sandy-textured surface (not glossy or crisp-shiny) with visible flecks of cinnamon sugar. Green chile cheeseburger toppings should show visibly charred/blistered strips of roasted chile (dark, blackened patches on otherwise pale-green flesh), not a smooth diced relish.
- **Doneness convention**: carne adovada's pork should be fall-apart tender (long-braised, not seared/pink), fully saturated in a deep, matte brick-red sauce that clings rather than pools thin; posole's hominy kernels should be fully bloomed/burst open (roughly triple the size of a raw corn kernel, per the hominy-preparation detail below), not tight/unpopped, and the pork should be visibly shredded or in small tender chunks, not large intact pieces.

## ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES — zone deltas from `us.md`'s national baseline

- **General architecture note**: New Mexico's built environment is genuinely distinct from both Texas's and Arizona's, and this section documents that distinctness explicitly rather than deferring to a generic "Southwest" default. **Adobe and Pueblo Revival architecture** (real adobe brick, or brick/stucco built to simulate it) is the single most identifying New Mexico architectural register: thick, rounded-corner or battered (sloped) walls, flat roofs, rounded parapets, and — the most distinctive, easily-staged detail — **projecting round viga beam-ends** along the roofline (peeled pine log beams that support the roof structure, their cut ends visibly protruding past the wall face rather than flush with it), often paired with a shaded **portal** (a covered porch/walkway) fronting a courtyard. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: Pueblo Revival architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pueblo_Revival_architecture); [Wikipedia: Viga (architecture)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viga_(architecture))] **Per the trademark/landmark-avoidance rule**, describe this as a generic architectural category — thick adobe-style walls, exposed viga beam-ends, a shaded portal — rather than naming or depicting any specific named building.
- **Chile ristras (strings of drying red chile pods) hanging against an adobe wall are a real, sourced, highly photogenic seasonal/decorative detail**, not an invented stereotype — traditionally a preservation method (the pods dry in the sun and are stored on the string for winter use), though today the large majority (an estimated 90%) of ristras sold are purely decorative. A ristra's dull, deep red color reads distinctly against a pale tan/earth-toned adobe wall — a genuine, sourced visual pairing specific to New Mexico (and, to a lesser extent, southern Arizona). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: Ristra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ristra); [Farmers Chile Market — All About Chile Ristras](https://www.farmerschilemarket.com/chile-ristras/)]
- **Fall chile-roasting scene (Aug–Sept) — a genuine, dramatic, sourced staging register specific to New Mexico, distinct from anything documented in `us.md`'s national baseline or in Texas/Arizona.** Throughout August and September, grocery-store parking lots and farm stands across New Mexico set up large, rotating cylindrical steel-mesh drums ("roasters" or "cages") over open propane flames; fresh-picked green chiles tumble inside as the skins blister and blacken, producing visible smoke and a strong roasted aroma. Shoppers line up with their own bags, boxes, or coolers, buying chile by the bushel to freeze for months of use. This is a real, well-documented, highly visual, and genuinely dramatic seasonal ritual — a strong staging candidate for an outdoor/roadside/farm-stand scene specifically set in late summer/early fall. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Visit Albuquerque — Turning Up the Heat: Chile Roasting Season in Albuquerque](https://www.visitalbuquerque.org/abq365/blog/post/chile-roasting-season-in-albuquerque/)]
- **The Green Chile Cheeseburger Trail is a real, official, dated tourism institution** — created in 2009 by the New Mexico State Tourism Department in partnership with a named James Beard Award-winning cookbook author, and updated to include 66 participating restaurants, drive-ins, diners, roadside stands, and even bowling alleys statewide, many along the historic Route 66 corridor. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [New Mexico True — Green Chile Cheeseburger Trail](https://www.newmexico.org/things-to-do/cuisine/culinary-trails/green-chile-cheeseburger-trail/); [Cowboys & Indians — The Green Chile Cheeseburger Trail](https://www.cowboysindians.com/2019/08/the-green-chile-cheeseburger-trail/)] **Per the trademark-avoidance rule**, stage this as a generic small roadside burger stand or diner — an old, single-story walk-up counter or diner storefront along a two-lane highway — never naming or depicting a specific real chain's branding.
- **Pueblo horno-bread scene**: a dome/beehive-shaped outdoor adobe oven, built and fired with wood (cedar is specifically documented as the traditional fuel, valued for its slow burn and fragrant smoke), from which round, oblong, or distinctively-shaped loaves (Laguna Pueblo's traditional "elephant toe" knobbed loaves are one named, documented regional shape among several) are removed once the embers/ash are cleared — a real, present-day, actively-practiced tradition across New Mexico's 19 Pueblo communities, each with its own distinct bread shapes. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the horno's existence, construction, and active present-day use; MEDIUM for the level of detail on which specific Pueblo uses which specific loaf shape, sourced to a single detailed magazine-tier account] [SOURCE: [Atlas Obscura — In New Mexico, Bakers Keep the Tradition of Pueblo Bread Alive](https://www.atlasobscura.com/foods/pueblo-bread)]

### Dish: New Mexican stacked enchiladas (full authoritative entry — supersedes `us.md`'s national-index Enchiladas pointer for the New Mexican variant; builds on, does not duplicate, `us.md`'s existing depth)

- Category: Everyday to special-occasion — arguably New Mexico's single most foundational, identity-defining dish, on par with the fajita's role in Tex-Mex (per `us.md`'s own framing).
- Cuisine lineage: New Mexican, itself a fusion of Pueblo/Indigenous corn-and-chile tradition with Spanish colonial technique (see ZONE CHARACTERIZATION above) — not a lesser or hybrid version of either Mexican-interior or Tex-Mex enchiladas, but an independently-evolved sibling.
- Regional form variation: `us.md` already documents the core form-changing distinction against Tex-Mex enchiladas at HIGH confidence (stacked vs. rolled; chile-pod sauce vs. roux-based chili gravy) — not re-derived here. **A genuine internal New Mexico variant exists and should be surfaced as a choice, not silently defaulted, per `country-file-schema.md` §4.6:**

| Attribute | Northern NM (Santa Fe/Taos/Chimayó — default when unspecified) | Southern NM (Hatch Valley/Las Cruces) |
|---|---|---|
| Tortilla corn color | Blue corn is common and locally distinctive | Yellow corn is the norm; blue corn "never seen" |
| Signature chile | Chimayó red chile (a GI-protected, USPTO-certified landrace) | Hatch green chile (GI-protected, trademark-litigated) |
| Overall register | Older, smaller-scale heirloom/landrace-seed tradition, more Pueblo/Hispano-influenced | Larger-scale chile-farming-economy tradition, tied to NMSU's chile-breeding program |

  [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — single detailed source for the core north/south contrast, corroborated by independently-sourced facts about each named chile variety] [SOURCE: [New Mexico Magazine — Not Mexican food. Not Tex-Mex.](https://www.newmexicomagazine.org/blog/post/best-new-mexico-foods/)]

### Dish: Hatch chile and Chimayó chile — geographic-designation depth (a cross-cutting ingredient entry, not a standalone plated dish, referenced by multiple dish entries above and below)

- Category: N/A (a defining ingredient, not a dish in its own right) — documented as its own entry because of its cross-cutting staging importance and the specific geographic-designation claim `us.md` flagged for expansion.
- **Hatch chile's geographic-designation status, verified and expanded from `us.md`'s existing flag.** "Hatch chile" refers specifically to New Mexico pod-type chiles grown within the Hatch Valley (a roughly 300-square-mile growing region along the Rio Grande in and around the town of Hatch, NM) — botanically the same chile variety as Anaheim, other New Mexico, or California chiles, but distinguished commercially and legally by *where* it's grown, exactly like Champagne (grape-growing region in France) or Vidalia onions (a specific part of Georgia). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH — this comparison recurs consistently across multiple independent sources and is not a loose analogy but the explicit basis of formal trademark/certification-mark litigation] [SOURCE: [Hatch Green Chile Store — What Is Hatch Chile?](https://www.hatch-green-chile.com/pages/what-are-hatch-chiles)] The valley's specific growing conditions — mineral-rich Rio Grande soil, 300+ days of intense sunshine annually, and roughly 40-degree day/night temperature swings — are cited as producing measurably higher flavor-compound concentration than chile grown elsewhere, the same terroir-based logic behind Champagne's and Vidalia onions' own protected-name status. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — the specific "23% more flavor compounds" figure comes from a single commercial (Hatch-grower-affiliated) source and should be treated as a marketing claim pending independent corroboration, though the underlying terroir logic itself is corroborated across multiple sources] [SOURCE: [Hatch Green Chile Store](https://www.hatch-green-chile.com/pages/what-are-hatch-chiles)]
- **This has actually been litigated, not just informally asserted.** A private company (Hatch Chile Company, Inc.) attempted to trademark "Hatch" for its own exclusive commercial use; the Hatch Chile Association and an allied distributor (El Encanto, Inc.) opposed this before the USPTO's Trademark Trial and Appeal Board, arguing "Hatch" is a geographic term that any chile genuinely grown in the valley should be able to use, and separately sought a certification mark to let consumers verify true Hatch-Valley origin. The dispute reached the U.S. 10th Circuit Court of Appeals (which sided with the Association's efforts to obtain evidence) before the parties settled in September 2017 — the Hatch Chile Company agreed not to oppose the Association's certification mark and to pay a per-ton fee on green chile used in its products. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH — multiple independent legal-news and trade-press sources corroborate the litigation's existence, parties, and resolution] [SOURCE: [Certified Hatch — The Hatch Chile Lawsuit](https://certifiedhatch.com/lawsuit/); [Santa Fe New Mexican — Court sides with efforts to certify Hatch chiles](https://www.santafenewmexican.com/news/local_news/court-sides-with-efforts-to-certify-hatch-chiles/article_cbe545f0-ffd4-50fc-9e41-719bd7cc06ef.html)] A brief specifying "Hatch chile" by name is therefore making a real, legally-contested, narrower geographic claim than "New Mexico chile" or "green chile" generically — peppers from Hatch seed stock grown elsewhere are properly labeled "Hatch-style," not "Hatch."
- **Chimayó chile is a parallel, independently-protected northern New Mexico landrace**, not a lesser-known Hatch sub-variety — see ZONE CHARACTERIZATION above for its own Geographic Indication and 2009 USPTO certification-mark history, and its place among at least sixteen recognized New Mexico landrace chiles overall, several (Cochiti Pueblo, Jemez Pueblo, Zia Pueblo) directly named for and grown by specific Pueblo communities. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: Chimayo pepper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chimayo_pepper)]
- Visual/plating characteristics: Fresh green Hatch/NM chile — long, tapering, medium-green pods, typically roasted until the skin blisters and blackens in patches (a genuine, sourced visual: charred dark patches on otherwise medium-green flesh, skin often peeled back or split after roasting to reveal glossy pale-green flesh underneath), then chopped into irregular, moderately chunky pieces (not finely diced/uniform) for use in sauces, stews, or as a burger topping. Dried red chile — deep brick-red, wrinkled, leathery pods, either left whole (as on a ristra) or ground into a coarse-to-fine red powder used to make the thin, matte red sauce.
- Confidence: HIGH overall for the geographic-designation status and litigation history; MEDIUM for the specific flavor-compound marketing claim, flagged individually above.

### Dish: Sopapillas

- Category: Everyday — served both as a savory bread accompaniment to a chile-sauced meal and, drizzled with honey, as a dessert; staged here across both occasions since neither is confined to a single meal.
- Cuisine lineage: **A genuine, important, sourced distinction from Navajo/Native American fry bread — the two are related but structurally and historically distinct, and this file documents that distinction carefully rather than collapsing them, per this project's Indigenous-heritage sourcing discipline (see FILE ROLE & METHOD above).** Sopapilla traces to a Spanish sopaipa tradition brought by Spanish settlers to New Mexico, where it blended with Pueblo and Hispano cooking to become a table bread and dessert. Navajo fry bread has a distinctly different, and distinctly harder, origin: it emerged from hardship following the Long Walk (the 1860s forced relocation of the Navajo people) and the US government's subsequent commodity-rationing system, which supplied flour, lard, sugar, and salt — ingredients Navajo families adapted into fry bread as a means of survival, not a leisurely table tradition. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — consistently described across multiple sources, though at food-blog/culinary-explainer tier rather than academic or tribally-authored tier; flagged per this project's Indigenous-sourcing discipline] [SOURCE: [Richardson's Canal House — Fry Bread Vs Sopapilla](https://richardsonscanalhouse.com/fry-bread-vs-sopapilla/); [Whisk Taker — What's the difference between sopapilla and fry bread?](https://www.whisk-taker.com/whats-the-difference-between-sopapilla-and-fry-bread/)]
- Regional form variation: None within New Mexico — nationally/regionally uniform in NM.
- **Serving-format/construction distinction from fry bread (a sibling-dish comparison, not a regional variant)**: a sopapilla is made from dough rolled flat and cut into squares or triangles, then deep-fried, puffing up into a light, **hollow**, pillow-like shape with a crisp shell and a mostly air-filled interior. Navajo fry bread, by contrast, is shaped by hand from individual balls of dough pressed into a thin round, then fried — it stays **flat and solid** throughout (no hollow puff), is typically larger in diameter, and has a denser, breadier chew rather than sopapilla's "biting into warm, edible air" lightness. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH — multiple consistent, detailed sources on the specific texture/construction distinction] [SOURCE: [Richardson's Canal House — Fry Bread Vs Sopapilla](https://richardsonscanalhouse.com/fry-bread-vs-sopapilla/)] **This is a real, checkable, important-to-get-right authenticity distinction for any New Mexico scene**: depicting a flat, solid, dense fry bread and labeling it a sopapilla (or vice versa) would misrepresent both the dish and the two food traditions' genuinely different histories.
- Serving format: Handheld/plate — small squares or triangles, served warm, typically 2–4 per order alongside a meal or as a dessert plate.
- Side dishes/condiments: Honey (drizzled over or served alongside in a small pitcher for dipping) and/or a dusting of cinnamon sugar for the dessert register; served plain, as bread, alongside a chile-sauced entree for the savory register.
- Serving vessel: A small bread basket or plate for the savory register; a dessert plate with a honey pitcher/ramekin for the dessert register.
- Real-world size (§4.5): Sopapilla dough is traditionally cut into roughly 2-inch squares before frying [SOURCE: [Tasty Recipes Chef — Sopapillas: Fluffy Treats from New Mexico](https://tastyrecipeschef.com/sopapillas/)]; once fried and puffed, a finished sopapilla roughly doubles in volume, yielding a piece comparable in each dimension to a golf ball flattened into a pillow shape, or a bit smaller across than the Coca-Cola can's 2.6-inch diameter — small enough that 2–3 fit easily on a small side plate without touching. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — the finished-size inference is this file's own reasonable synthesis from the pre-fry dimension and general "doubles in size when puffed" fry-dough behavior, not an independently measured finished-product figure]
- Visual/plating characteristics: A matte, evenly golden-brown, slightly domed/pillowed fried-dough shape (triangular or square, following its pre-fry cut), with small irregular surface blisters from frying — importantly **not** flat or solid; when broken open, it should reveal a mostly hollow, air-filled interior with only a thin, slightly moist dough lining the inside surface, not a dense bready crumb. A drizzle of honey pools and glistens on the exterior surface and in any surface dimples, contrasting with the matte fried dough.
- Common confusion: **Navajo fry bread** — see the construction/texture distinction above; the single clearest checkable difference is hollow-and-puffed (sopapilla) vs. flat-and-solid (fry bread). Not a beignet (`us-gulf-south.md`) — beignets are a yeasted, powdered-sugar-dusted French-derived pastry with a different dough and no honey-drizzle convention, and are square-cut but not shaped to visibly puff into a rounded pillow the way a sopapilla does.
- Confidence: HIGH for the fry-bread distinction and general description; MEDIUM for the exact size figures.
- Sources: [Richardson's Canal House — Fry Bread Vs Sopapilla](https://richardsonscanalhouse.com/fry-bread-vs-sopapilla/); [Whisk Taker — What's the difference between sopapilla and fry bread?](https://www.whisk-taker.com/whats-the-difference-between-sopapilla-and-fry-bread/); [Tasty Recipes Chef — Sopapillas: Fluffy Treats from New Mexico](https://tastyrecipeschef.com/sopapillas/); [Sadie's of New Mexico — The Sopapilla Story](https://www.sadiesofnewmexico.com/2025/08/12/the-sopapilla-story-a-staple-of-new-mexican-flavor-and-tradition/)

### Dish: Posole (pork and hominy stew, red or green chile)

- Category: Everyday to special-occasion — a standing menu item at New Mexican restaurants, and specifically a documented holiday-season dish (New Mexicans traditionally eat posole and tamales together between Christmas and New Year's).
- Cuisine lineage: A genuinely layered Indigenous-and-Spanish dish, honestly disclosed as such rather than credited to one tradition alone — hominy (nixtamalized corn) is one of New Mexico's oldest foods and an Indigenous food-processing technology predating Spanish arrival; Spanish settlers introduced the pork and European spices that were blended into the already-Indigenous hominy-and-chile base. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Beyond Mere Sustenance — Chimayo, New Mexico and Red Chile Posole](https://beyondmeresustenance.com/chimayo-new-mexico-and-red-chile-posole/)]

New Mexico's version is also documented as simpler than Mexican-interior pozole, which often adds tomatillos, cabbage, and radish garnishes not typically used in the New Mexican version (hominy, pork, red or green chile, with comparatively few other add-ins). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Food Folks and Fun — New Mexico Posole Recipe](https://foodfolksandfun.net/new-mexico-posole/)]
- Serving format: Bowl — a soup/stew course, either a starter/side portion alongside an entree or a full bowl as a standalone meal.
- Primary protein/composition: Pork (chunked, not ground), simmered long enough to become fork-tender, combined with dried/nixtamalized hominy corn kernels and either red or green chile.
- Side dishes/condiments: Often served with warm tortillas or sopapillas on the side; a garnish of shredded cabbage, chopped onion, oregano, or lime is common at the table, added by the diner rather than pre-mixed in.
- Serving vessel: A deep individual bowl, per `tableware-composition-reference.md` §2's side-bowl-medium dimension (15–18cm) for a starter portion, or a larger soup bowl for a main-course portion.
- Real-world size (§4.5): Hominy kernels, once cooked, swell to roughly the size of a garbanzo bean/chickpea — approximately triple the size of a raw sweet-corn kernel — a genuine, checkable visual detail: individual kernels in the bowl should read as plump, puffed, and roughly pea-to-chickpea-sized, not small or tightly kernel-shaped like fresh corn. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — a consistent, specific comparison across multiple food-reference sources] [SOURCE: [Food Network — What Is Hominy?](https://www.foodnetwork.com/how-to/packages/food-network-essentials/what-is-hominy)] Per the vessel fallback, a main-course bowl should be filled to within roughly 1–2cm of the rim, thick enough with visible solid pieces (hominy and pork chunks) that the stew doesn't read as a thin, clear broth.
- Visual/plating characteristics: Red posole shows the same thin, matte, earthy brick-red broth as the enchilada's red chile sauce, with pale ivory-white, plump, burst-open hominy kernels and visible shredded/chunked pork suspended throughout — a genuine color and texture contrast between the pale hominy and the dark red broth. Green posole shows a lighter, more yellowish-green, chunkier broth with visible roasted green chile pieces alongside the same pale hominy and pork. **Texture/finish**: hominy kernels should look plump, glossy-wet, and slightly burst/split at one end (not tight or intact-kernel-shaped), a checkable sign of proper cooking.
- Common confusion: Mexican-interior pozole (typically garnished with shredded cabbage, radish, and lime as standard rather than optional add-ons, and sometimes made with hominy that hasn't been simmered in a chile-forward broth the same way) — the New Mexican version's simpler, chile-broth-forward presentation without those garnishes pre-added is the checkable difference. Not menudo (a different offal-based soup, not hominy-and-pork).
- Confidence: HIGH for composition and cultural/holiday significance; MEDIUM-HIGH for the specific visual/scale synthesis.

### Dish: Carne adovada

- Category: Everyday to special-occasion — a common Sunday-dinner, holiday, and fiesta dish, per multiple sources.
- Cuisine lineage: Spanish colonial in name and technique (adovada derives from the Spanish adobar, "to marinate/preserve"), adapted over generations by Pueblo peoples and later New Mexican settlers — colonial-era New Mexicans preserved pork in red chile sauce after slaughter, a practice that doubled as both flavoring and preservation before refrigeration. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Highlands Ranch Foodie — Authentic Carne Adovada Recipe](https://highlandsranchfoodie.com/carne-adovada-recipe-2/); [Bodega Badia — Carne Adovada: New Mexico's Red Chile Braised Pork](https://bodega.badiaspices.com/pages/new-mexico-carne-adovada)]
- Regional form variation: None identified as form-changing within New Mexico — the dish is consistently red-chile-only (unlike posole/enchiladas, which offer a genuine red/green choice); authentic versions specifically favor milder, earthier Chimayó or Hatch-adjacent "Año" chile rather than a fiery-hot chile, a real, sourced point worth noting since a viewer might otherwise assume "New Mexico chile dish" implies extreme heat.
- Serving format: Plate — served as a standalone entree with sides, or as a filling for burritos/tacos in a more casual register.
- Primary protein/composition: Pork, cut into strips or cubes, marinated (traditionally overnight) in red chile (powder or minced pods), garlic, oregano, cumin, and an acid (lime or vinegar), then slow-baked, typically wrapped in foil or in a covered dish, until fall-apart tender.
- Side dishes/condiments: Commonly served with pinto beans, Spanish rice, and warm flour or corn tortillas, or sopapillas.
- Serving vessel: A standard round entree plate (26–28cm) or, in a more rustic home-style presentation, straight from a covered casserole/baking dish.
- Real-world size (§4.5): Pork pieces are typically cut into 1–1.5 inch cubes or strips before marinating (comparable to a large ice cube or a AA battery in length) — per the vessel fallback, a standard entree portion should fill roughly one-third to one-half of a 26–28cm plate, mounded rather than spread thin, with visible chunks distinguishable from each other rather than shredded into a uniform mass (a checkable difference from the more shredded/pulled texture of, e.g., barbacoa in `us-texas.md`). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — this file's own reasonable synthesis from recipe-tier sourcing describing "strips or cubes," not an independently measured figure]
- Visual/plating characteristics: Deep, glossy-matte brick-red pork chunks fully coated in a thick, clinging red chile sauce (thicker and more adherent than the thin posole/enchilada red sauce, since the sauce here is also the marinade/braising liquid, reduced during the long bake) — the meat should show visible fall-apart tenderness (strands separating slightly at the edges of each piece) rather than a firm, intact sear. No visible raw pink at any cut surface; the color should read a uniform deep reddish-brown throughout, distinguishing it from a rare/pink-centered cut of meat.
- Common confusion: Not carnitas (a Mexican-interior dish, fried/confited rather than chile-braised, with a browned/crisped exterior on some pieces and no red chile coating). Not Tex-Mex chili (`us-texas.md`) — chili is a beef stew served in a thin bowl of liquid chile-based sauce as its own dish; carne adovada is pork, always chile-coated rather than swimming in a thin broth, and served as a plated entree with rice/beans rather than in a soup bowl.
- Confidence: HIGH for composition and colonial-era history; MEDIUM for the specific size/portion synthesis.
- Sources: [Highlands Ranch Foodie — Authentic Carne Adovada Recipe](https://highlandsranchfoodie.com/carne-adovada-recipe-2/); [Bodega Badia — Carne Adovada: New Mexico's Red Chile Braised Pork](https://bodega.badiaspices.com/pages/new-mexico-carne-adovada); [New Mexico Magazine — Centennial Cookbook Recipe: Carne Adovada](https://www.newmexicomagazine.org/blog/post/centennial-cookbook-recipe-carne-adovada/)

### Dish: Green chile cheeseburger

- Category: Everyday — a genuine, dated, widely-institutionalized New Mexico phenomenon, not a niche or recent trend.
- Cuisine lineage: Native-New-Mexican-tradition, a direct fusion of the American hamburger with the state's own signature ingredient. The specific, sourced origin traces to Blake's Lotaburger, founded by WWII veteran Blake Chanslor in Albuquerque on July 9, 1952 — the green chile topping was reportedly added only after Chanslor noticed customers repeatedly bringing their own green chile to smother onto the plain burgers he was already selling, adapting his menu in response. Blake's is widely credited (by restaurant-industry retrospectives, including a 2006 National Geographic recognition for "Best Green Chile Cheeseburger in the World") as the first chain to market the combination, though — following the same honest-disclosure practice this file applies to other contested-origin dishes — this file treats "first" claims cautiously rather than as fully settled fact. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — a consistent, detailed, multi-source account, though ultimately traceable to the company's own told history rather than independent contemporaneous documentation] [SOURCE: [New Mexico Magazine — How Blake's Lotaburger Became Legendary](https://www.newmexicomagazine.org/blog/post/blakes-lotaburger/); [Wikipedia: Blake's Lotaburger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blake%27s_Lotaburger)]
- Regional form variation: None — a single, nationally-recognized (within New Mexico) form; the specific chile used (Hatch vs. a local/other NM green chile) may vary by establishment but doesn't change the dish's visual construction.
- **Institutional/tourism significance**: the Green Chile Cheeseburger Trail (see ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES above) is a real, official, 2009-established New Mexico State Tourism Department program spanning 66 participating restaurants statewide — a genuine, dated tourism institution built around this one dish, comparable in kind (though a different mechanism) to Texas's BBQ-joint culture.
- Serving format: Sandwich/handheld — a standard burger construction with the chile as the defining topping.
- Primary protein/composition: A standard ground-beef burger patty, topped with melted cheese (typically American or cheddar) and a generous layer of roasted, peeled, chopped green chile (Hatch or other New Mexico green chile), on a standard burger bun.
- Side dishes/condiments: Standard American burger condiments (lettuce, tomato, onion, mustard/mayo/ketchup) are common but secondary — the green chile is the dish's defining, non-negotiable element, not an optional add-on the way it might be marketed as a novelty topping elsewhere in the US.
- Serving vessel: Paper wrap/basket (fast-food/roadside-stand register) or plate (sit-down diner register) — see `us.md`'s general Cheeseburger entry for the shared burger-construction baseline.
- Real-world size (§4.5): Standard burger-patty and bun dimensions apply (see `us.md`'s Cheeseburger entry) — the differentiating visual element is the chile topping itself: roasted green chile strips/pieces should be visibly piled in a layer roughly ¼–½ inch thick across the patty's surface (thick enough to see chile pieces mounded above the cheese line, not a thin scattering), extending to or slightly past the patty's edge. [CONFIDENCE: LOW-MEDIUM — this file's own visual inference, not independently sourced as an exact measurement]
- Visual/plating characteristics: The defining, checkable visual marker is **visibly charred, blistered, irregular strips or pieces of roasted green chile** — dark blackened patches on medium-green flesh, glossy from the roasting oil, piled thickly on top of the melted cheese layer — as distinct from a smooth green relish, a raw diced jalapeño, or a uniform green sauce, none of which would be authentic. ### Dish: Biscochitos (official state cookie)

- Category: Everyday to special-occasion — traditionally associated with weddings, baptisms, and Christmas, but also sold and eaten as an everyday bakery item, clearing this file's inherited scope on the same all-day-availability logic as `us-texas.md`'s kolaches.
- Cuisine lineage: Spanish colonial — introduced by early Spanish colonists to what was then Santa Fe de Nuevo México in the 16th century, and developed over subsequent centuries with influence from local Indigenous customs and later Hispanic immigrant recipes. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [statesymbolsusa.org — New Mexico State Cookie: Biscochito](https://statesymbolsusa.org/symbol-official-item/new-mexico/state-food-agriculture-symbol/biscochito)]
- **Official status**: New Mexico designated the biscochito its official state cookie in 1989 — the first US state to designate an official state cookie of any kind. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [statesymbolsusa.org — New Mexico State Cookie: Biscochito](https://statesymbolsusa.org/symbol-official-item/new-mexico/state-food-agriculture-symbol/biscochito)]
- Regional form variation: None identified as form-changing.
- Serving format: Handheld cookie/snack, sold individually or by the dozen at bakeries, or homemade for holidays.
- Primary composition: A crisp shortbread-style cookie made with lard (traditional; butter is a common modern substitute), flavored with anise seed and finished with a cinnamon-sugar coating.
- Side dishes/condiments: None — eaten plain, often with coffee or hot chocolate.
- Serving vessel: Loose in a bakery case, a cookie tin, or a plate for a holiday spread — no formal plated presentation.
- Real-world size (§4.5): Traditionally cut with a fleur-de-lis-shaped cutter (a documented traditional shape, alongside crosses, stars, and simple scalloped rounds), commercial fleur-de-lis cutters for this purpose commonly measuring 4–4.75 inches — though a smaller 2–2.25 inch round or scalloped cutter is also a common, more everyday-bakery-scale alternative. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — the specific cutter-size figures are drawn from commercial baking-supply listings rather than a dedicated culinary-history measurement of the "correct" biscochito size, and real-world finished cookies likely run smaller than the full cutter dimension once dough shrinkage during baking is accounted for; treat the 2–3 inch range as the more representative finished-cookie estimate, comparable to a small coaster or slightly larger than the Coca-Cola can's 2.6-inch diameter] [SOURCE: [The Cookie Countess — Fleur de Lis Cookie Cutter listings](https://www.thecookiecountess.com/products/fleur-de-lis-cutter)]
- Visual/plating characteristics: A pale, sandy, matte-golden (not glossy or deeply browned) cookie with a flat, even surface and visible small flecks of cinnamon sugar dusted or sprinkled across the top; the fleur-de-lis (or star/cross) shape, when used, gives a distinctive silhouette with pointed or scalloped edges rather than a plain round — a visually distinguishing detail from a generic round sugar cookie. The texture reads crisp and slightly crumbly (shortbread-like), not soft or chewy.
- Common confusion: A generic round sugar cookie or Mexican wedding cookie (powdered-sugar-coated, round, no anise flavor, no fleur-de-lis shape) — the anise flavor (not independently visually checkable, but worth noting) and the cinnamon-sugar dusting plus fleur-de-lis/star/cross shape are the checkable visual differentiators.

### Dish: Tamales (New Mexico Christmas tradition)

- Category: Special-occasion — specifically and consistently documented as a Christmas Eve/Christmas Day/New Year's holiday food, paired with posole; less prominent as an everyday item than the other dishes in this catalog, included here for its strong staging relevance to a specific, well-documented holiday occasion.
- Cuisine lineage: New Mexican, part of the broader Mexican/Southwestern tamal tradition but specifically embedded in New Mexico's own holiday calendar and communal-preparation ritual (the "tamalada").
- Regional form variation: None identified as New-Mexico-internal; standard corn-husk-wrapped tamal construction.
- **The tamalada as a staging-relevant social ritual**: families gather to make large batches together — one person soaks corn husks, another spreads masa, others fill/fold/steam — a genuinely communal, multi-person kitchen scene distinct from most of this file's other individually-plated dishes, and a plausible basis for a multi-person "meal prep" or "family gathering" staging scenario specifically tied to the Christmas season. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Loma Vista Products — The Tamalada Tradition](https://www.lomavistaproducts.com/the-tamalada-tradition-a-new-mexican-christmas-gathering/)]
- Serving format: Handheld/plate — corn husk unwrapped at the table, tamales eaten with a fork or by hand.
- Primary protein/composition: Masa (corn dough) filled with red chile pork (or chicken, or a cheese/chile vegetarian filling), wrapped in a softened dried corn husk, and steamed.
- Side dishes/condiments: Served alongside posole as the standard holiday pairing (see Posole entry above); red or green chile sauce may be spooned over an unwrapped tamale at the table.
- Serving vessel: Plate, tamales typically served in a small stack of 2–3, husks partially peeled back to reveal the masa.
- Real-world size (§4.5): A standard tamale is roughly 4–6 inches long and 1.5–2 inches in diameter once wrapped (comparable in length to the Coca-Cola can's 4.83-inch height, though noticeably slimmer than the can's 2.6-inch diameter). Served husk-still-on, with the top of the husk peeled/folded open to expose roughly the top third of the masa cylinder. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — a general tamale-sizing convention, not independently sourced as New-Mexico-specific]
- Visual/plating characteristics: A pale, matte, slightly glossy corn-husk wrapper (tan-to-golden dried-husk color, faintly ridged with visible fiber lines), tied or folded closed at both ends; once opened, the masa itself is pale yellow or white, dense and moist (not fluffy or dry), with the red chile pork filling visible as a distinct darker-red streak or pocket through the center when the tamale is split lengthwise.
- Common confusion: Not a Mexican-interior tamale of a different regional style (banana-leaf-wrapped Oaxacan tamales, for instance, are a different, non-New-Mexican wrapper convention) — corn-husk wrapping specifically is the standard, checkable New Mexico/greater-Southwest convention.
- Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH for the holiday tradition and tamalada ritual; MEDIUM for exact size figures.
- Sources: [Loma Vista Products — The Tamalada Tradition](https://www.lomavistaproducts.com/the-tamalada-tradition-a-new-mexican-christmas-gathering/); [New Mexico Magazine — Tamales, Empanaditas, and Farolitos: Christmas Traditions in New Mexico](https://www.newmexicomagazine.org/blog/post/christmas-memories-and-traditions-in-new-mexico/); [Tomasitas — Why New Mexican Posole Is a Holiday Tradition](https://tomasitas.com/why-new-mexican-posole-is-a-holiday-tradition/)

### Dish: Pueblo bread (horno-baked) — Indigenous/Pueblo culinary and architectural feature

- Category: Special-occasion to everyday, depending on community and context — baked in large batches for family use, feast days, and market/roadside sale.
- Cuisine lineage: **Pueblo/Indigenous, with an important, honestly-disclosed layered history**: the horno oven itself (the dome-shaped adobe structure) and wheat flour were introduced by Spanish colonists, but Pueblo bakers adopted and adapted both into a now-distinctly-Pueblo tradition, with each of New Mexico's 19 Pueblo Nations developing its own characteristic bread shapes over generations. This file follows the sourcing-tier discipline established elsewhere in this project (see FILE ROLE & METHOD above and `us-appalachia.md`'s precedent): the horno's Spanish-introduction and the bread-shapes' Pueblo-specific development are both stated as sourced claims at the confidence level the evidence supports, not elevated to more certainty than the sourcing (magazine/journalism-tier, not tribally-authored or academic) actually provides. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — consistent across multiple sources for the horno's basic construction and active use; MEDIUM for the specific claim that the Spanish "introduced" the horno and wheat rather than these being independently or jointly developed, since no tribally-authored or academic primary source distinguishing the claim more precisely was located] [SOURCE: [Atlas Obscura — In New Mexico, Bakers Keep the Tradition of Pueblo Bread Alive](https://www.atlasobscura.com/foods/pueblo-bread)]
- Regional form variation: **Genuinely form-changing across individual Pueblo communities, not a single uniform "Pueblo bread"** — sources describe distinctly different shapes: bulbous, flower-like loaves with a crisp golden crust; thick oblong slabs that puff in the oven; and Laguna Pueblo's specific "elephant toes" — knobbed loaves named for their shape. This file does not have sourcing detailed enough to assign every shape to its specific originating Pueblo with confidence, and flags this as a gap (see GAP LOG) rather than guessing.
- Serving format: Whole loaves, typically torn/broken by hand rather than sliced, shared communally.
- Primary composition: Wheat flour, butter or lard, water, and salt, kneaded and left to rise overnight, then baked in the wood-fired horno.
- Serving vessel: No formal plated presentation — loaves are typically displayed and sold whole, or torn and shared directly from a basket or cloth-lined surface.
- Real-world size (§4.5): Not confidently sourced to a specific dimension this pass — described qualitatively (large, round-to-oblong loaves) but no specific diameter/weight figure was found. Flagged in GAP LOG as needing a dedicated follow-up pass rather than an invented figure. As a rough, low-confidence visual anchor only: a horno-baked loaf reads as noticeably larger than a standard dinner roll, closer in scale to a small artisan boule (roughly 8–10 inches across) based on general descriptions of "large batches" and family/market-scale baking, but this specific figure is this file's own inference, not a sourced measurement. [CONFIDENCE: LOW — flagged explicitly rather than presented as settled]
- Visual/plating characteristics: A round or oblong loaf with a crisp, deep golden-brown crust (from the horno's high, radiant dry heat) and — per the "elephant toes" description — sometimes a distinctly knobbed, irregular, multi-lobed surface rather than a single smooth dome; texture reads as a dense, chewy crumb typical of a wood-fired hearth bread rather than a soft, airy sandwich-loaf crumb. The horno itself (see ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES above) is the more visually distinctive, staging-relevant element: a rounded, beehive-shaped, whitewashed-or-natural-earth-toned adobe structure with a single low arched opening, often with visible smoke/heat shimmer during firing and a long wooden paddle used to place/remove loaves.
- Common confusion: Not a standard artisan bakery boule or rustic European hearth loaf (which would lack the horno's distinctive above-ground beehive-oven staging context and the community/feast-day framing); the oven itself, not just the bread's shape, is the primary unmistakable visual marker.
- Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH for the horno's construction and active present-day use; LOW-MEDIUM for shape-to-specific-Pueblo attribution and exact loaf dimensions — both flagged in GAP LOG.
- Sources: [Atlas Obscura — In New Mexico, Bakers Keep the Tradition of Pueblo Bread Alive](https://www.atlasobscura.com/foods/pueblo-bread); [Wikipedia: Adobe bread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe_bread); [Perry Null Trading — How to Build a Pueblo Bread Oven](https://turquoisetraders.com/blogs/perry-null-trading/how-to-build-a-pueblo-bread-oven)
