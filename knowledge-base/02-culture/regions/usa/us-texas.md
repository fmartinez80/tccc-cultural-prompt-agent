## FILE ROLE & METHOD

This is the eighth regional file built under the 13-file US structure. It holds full, authoritative depth for `us.md`'s four existing pointers to `us-texas.md`:

1. **Texas chili** (`us.md` Chili section) — the beanless "bowl of red," Texas's official state dish.
2. **Tex-Mex tacos** (`us.md` Mexican-American/Tacos section).
3. **Tex-Mex enchiladas** (`us.md` Mexican-American/Enchiladas section) — `us.md` already carries substantial sourced Tex-Mex-vs-New-Mexican comparison detail (rolled vs. stacked, chili-gravy vs. chile-pod sauce); this file builds on, not duplicates, that existing depth, adding the chili gravy's own composition history and additional visual/scale detail `us.md` didn't need for its comparison purpose.
4. **Texas BBQ** (`us.md` BBQ section) — beef-focused, brisket-anchored, dry rub, minimal-to-no sauce, Central Texas specifically salt/pepper/post-oak.

It also adds a large body of new, Texas-native dishes not pointed to from `us.md` at all (none needed, per `country-file-schema.md`'s placement rule — these dishes have no variant elsewhere in the US, the same treatment `us-gulf-south.md`'s po'boy/muffuletta/beignets got): fajitas, chile con queso, chicken-fried steak, kolaches/klobasnek, barbacoa, cabrito, and Frito pie.

**Structural decision — one file, no internal zone split**, resolved during this merge rather than left as the open question the research pass flagged. Texas has two independent axes of real internal variation:

1. **Tex-Mex intensity/style (South Texas/Rio Grande Valley vs. North Texas/Dallas–Fort Worth vs. El Paso/West Texas)** — a genuine gradient along one axis (proximity to the Mexican border and each region's own settlement history), not four structurally different dishes. This confirms `us.md`'s own working assumption (FILE ROLE & METHOD table, row 8: "internal North/South/West Texas Tex-Mex variation noted as a callout, not a further split").
2. **BBQ regional style (Central/East/South-barbacoa/West Texas)** — a more structurally distinct four-way case: different primary protein, cooking method, sauce convention, and settlement history per variant.

**Decision**: unlike `us-midwest.md`'s Kansas City/St. Louis split — which separated two cities carrying two entirely different whole food identities (barbecue vs. Italian-American/German-American, each with its own multi-dish catalog) — Texas's internal BBQ variation stays *within* one already-coherent dish family. It is the same kind of internal variation this project already handles at the dish level without a zone split elsewhere: Chicago's three coexisting pizza registers, Michigan's Detroit-vs-Flint coney split, and Kansas City's own brisket-vs-pork-belly burnt ends are all real, sourced, non-hierarchical variants documented via `country-file-schema.md` §4.6 variant tables inside a single dish entry, not promoted to separate zones. Texas BBQ's four-way split gets the identical treatment: one dish entry, a full variant table, Central Texas as the stated default when a brief is unspecified. **Barbacoa is the one partial exception**, given its own full dish entry rather than a row in the BBQ variant table — not because it merits zone status, but because it differs from the other three in protein (beef head/cheek, not brisket or pork), method (underground pit, not a smoker), serving format (a taco filling, not a sauced platter), and occasion (Sunday morning, not an evening BBQ order) enough that folding it into the platter's variant table would misrepresent it as a sauced-platter sibling it isn't. Cabrito gets the same "distinct enough for its own entry" treatment for the same reason.

**Scope inherited from `us.md`**: lunch, dinner, and snacks only (breakfast excluded); inclusion by staging relevance, not raw consumption frequency. **Breakfast tacos were explicitly checked against this exclusion and clear it**, the same way `us-gulf-south.md` checked beignets and `us-midwest.md` checked goetta: breakfast tacos are consistently documented as an all-day food in Austin/San Antonio specifically — sold at taquerias, gas stations, and food trucks well past breakfast hours, and eaten as a lunch item, a post-bar-close snack, and a tailgate/game-day food, not confined to a morning meal occasion. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — consistently described as an all-day food across multiple Austin/San Antonio food-culture sources, though no single source quantifies what share of consumption is genuinely midday/snack vs. morning] [SOURCE: [ATXtoday — The delicious history of breakfast tacos](https://atxtoday.6amcity.com/food/delicious-history-breakfast-tacos); [CNN Travel — A short history of the long fight over breakfast tacos](https://www.cnn.com/travel/article/breakfast-tacos-origins-mexico-texas/index.html)] It is staged below as a lunch/snack/on-the-go item specifically, never as a morning-plate scene. Kolaches/klobasnek clear the same exclusion on the same all-day-bakery-availability logic.

**Typical use note** (carried forward from prior regional files' own calibration): expect the realistic use case to skew toward general, archetypal scenes — a Central Texas BBQ meat market, a Tex-Mex restaurant with a sizzling fajita platter, a Hill Country kolache bakery — more than hyper-local block-level staging.

## ZONE CHARACTERIZATION

Texas as a whole clears the "would swapping this region's norms into another region look visibly wrong" test on multiple independent, causally-explained grounds — and, per the structural decision above, carries real *internal* variation that is documented via dish-level variant tables and dedicated entries rather than a further zone split.

**Cattle-ranching economy.** Texas's identity as a beef-culture state is not branding — it is a direct, traceable economic history. After the Civil War left Texas cash-poor but holding millions of nearly worthless longhorn cattle, the Chisholm Trail and its sibling trails moved roughly 1.5 million head north to rail markets (1867–1871 alone), building the cattle-drive economy that made beef, not pork, the state's structural protein — a direct, sourced explanation for why Texas BBQ centers on beef brisket while Memphis/Carolina BBQ (`us-south.md`) centers on pork. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [TSHA — The Chisholm Trail](https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/chisholm-trail); [Cato Institute — Cowboys and Entrepreneurs in the Cattle Kingdom](https://www.cato.org/regulation/winter-2015-2016/chisholm-trail)]

**German and Czech immigration into the Texas Hill Country.** German immigrants arrived in significant numbers from the 1840s, founding Fredericksburg (1846), New Braunfels (1845), Boerne, and Castroville, and building a persistent regional identity around beer brewing, sausage-making, and butcher-shop culture. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Texas Hill Country Resort — The History Behind Texas Hill Country's German Roots](https://www.txhillcountryresort.com/blog/the-history-behind-texas-hill-countrys-german-roots/)] Czech immigration followed a similar timeline and geography (1840s–1910s, concentrated on the Central Texas Blackland Prairie towns of West, Hallettsville, Schulenburg, and La Grange). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Colorado River Land Trust — Kolache Road Trip](https://www.coloradoriverlandtrust.org/kolache-road-trip-tracing-the-sweet-buttery-roots-of-texas/); [UTSA LibGuides — Czech Texans](https://libguides.utsa.edu/c.php?amp=&g=937839&p=6768533)] This is not a decorative footnote — it is the **direct causal mechanism** behind two of this file's anchor dishes: Central Texas BBQ's meat-market tradition (German/Czech immigrant butcher shops in Lockhart, Taylor, and Luling smoking unsold weekday meat for weekend sale) and chicken-fried steak (widely credited to German/Austrian immigrants adapting Wiener schnitzel to Texas's cheap, abundant beef). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the meat-market mechanism; MEDIUM-HIGH for chicken-fried steak's specific German lineage, given a genuinely competing origin legend — see that dish's own entry] [SOURCE: [Black's BBQ — A Guide to Central Texas Barbecue Traditions](https://www.blacksbbq.com/resources/blog/central-texas-bbq-guide); [Texas Historical Commission — Bringing Texas Barbecue History to the Table](https://thc.texas.gov/blog/bringing-texas-barbecue-history-table)]

**Tejano heritage and the Texas Revolution-era Mexican-Texan population underlying Tex-Mex.** Texas declared independence from Mexico in 1836 and joined the US in 1845; the Tejano population (Texans of Mexican descent who predate and persist through that political transition) is the direct lineage of Tex-Mex cuisine, not a later immigrant-fusion story layered onto an already-formed "American" Texas. Tejano home cooks incorporated newly-available cheddar cheese, wheat flour, and cumin into Mexican recipe traditions, producing wheat-flour tortillas, chili con carne, and other now-canonical Tex-Mex forms. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [HISTORY — Tracing the History of Tex-Mex](https://www.history.com/articles/tracing-the-history-of-tex-mex)] San Antonio's "chili queens" — women who sold carne con chile, tamales, enchiladas, and frijoles from open-air plazas from the 1880s onward — are the specific, dated, sourced mechanism by which this home-kitchen tradition reached a broader (including Anglo and tourist) audience and built San Antonio's enduring reputation as Tex-Mex's capital. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Texas Hill Country — San Antonio and Its Role in the History of Tex-Mex Cuisine](https://texashillcountry.com/san-antonio-history-tex-mex-cuisine/)] Diana Kennedy's 1972 *The Cuisines of Mexico* is the specific, dated mechanism by which "Tex-Mex" became a named, bounded, self-conscious category distinct from Mexican food *as a matter of public discourse* — she drew a firm line between food "authentic" to Mexico and the "mixed plates" served north of the border, inadvertently naming and crystallizing Tex-Mex as its own cuisine in the process. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [The Plaza Restaurant — How Tex-Mex Found Its Place As An American Cuisine](https://www.theplazarestaurant.com/how-tex-mex-found-its-place-as-an-american-cuisine/)]

**Internal variation, validated rather than assumed:**
- **Tex-Mex intensity/style is real but is a gradient along one axis, not four structurally different dishes**: **South Texas/Rio Grande Valley** stays closely tied to the cooking of Mexico's neighboring northern states — the least "Americanized" end of the spectrum, and the origin point of both fajitas and barbacoa (both covered as their own entries below). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM] [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: South Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Texas)] **North Texas/Dallas–Fort Worth** is a heavier, beef-and-ranching-forward register (fajitas, carne asada), bold cumin/chili-powder seasoning, and the register most associated nationally with the words "Tex-Mex." [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM] [SOURCE: [Mi Cocina — What Makes Tex-Mex Unique Across Regions?](https://www.micocina.com/articles/what-makes-tex-mex-unique-across-regions/)] **El Paso/West Texas** leans closer to fresh-salsa, mole-adjacent Mexican and Sonoran/Southwestern influence than to yellow-cheese-and-ground-beef Tex-Mex — a real, flagged overlap with `us-desert-southwest.md`'s Sonoran content worth a future cross-check once both files exist, though not strong enough evidence to move El Paso out of Texas's scope. [CONFIDENCE: LOW-MEDIUM — a single retail-blog-tier source] [SOURCE: [Mi Cocina — 3 Best Regional Variations of Tex-Mex Cuisine](https://www.micocina.com/articles/3-best-regional-variations-of-tex-mex-cuisine/)]
- **San Antonio holds a specific, sourced claim as "the capital of Tex-Mex,"** distinct from being merely one Texas city among several — both the chili queens' historical role and the puffy taco's origin (see Tacos entry) are San Antonio-specific, not generically Texan. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]

**What this zone characterization is not claiming.** Texas is not internally uniform, and this file does not treat it as such — see the internal-variation notes inside the BBQ, Tex-Mex, and taco entries below. **Caricature-avoidance note (editorial judgment, not a sourced claim):** not every Texas scene should default to a rural ranch/cowboy-hat register — Texas's major metros (Houston, Dallas–Fort Worth, San Antonio, Austin, El Paso) are large, diverse, heavily urban/suburban cities, and an urban Tex-Mex restaurant or a Houston strip-mall taqueria is at least as representative a Texas setting as a rural ranch scene. Equally, avoid over-correcting into treating Texas as generically indistinguishable from the rest of the Sun Belt South — the specific, sourced dishes and settlement histories above are what make Texas visibly wrong to swap with `us-south.md` or `us-desert-southwest.md` content.

## TRUSTED CONTENT

- **Beef, not pork, is Texas's structural protein**, in direct, sourced contrast to the pork-centered BBQ traditions of the Deep South (`us-south.md`) — see the cattle-ranching economic history above. This is the single most reliable "would this look wrong elsewhere" marker for Texas BBQ specifically. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- **Texas BBQ's meat-market retail format (order by the pound, sliced to order, served on butcher paper rather than a plate) is itself a real, causally-explained convention, not just a stylistic choice.** It traces directly to the German/Czech immigrant butcher-shop practice of smoking unsold weekday meat for weekend sale — customers bought barbecue by the weight and ate it off the paper it was wrapped in, with a knife, crackers, or pickles, not from a plate with a fork. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Black's BBQ — A Guide to Central Texas Barbecue Traditions](https://www.blacksbbq.com/resources/blog/central-texas-bbq-guide); [Authentic Texas — The History of Texas Barbecue, Brisket and Smoke](https://authentictexas.com/up-in-smoke/)]
- **Tex-Mex's defining departures from Mexican cuisine are specific, named, and traceable to Tejano home-kitchen adaptation, not vague "Americanization."** Yellow cheddar cheese (in place of Mexican cheeses), wheat-flour tortillas (alongside corn), ground cumin, and a flour-roux "gravy" technique (learned from German/Czech neighbors, per the chili-gravy entry below) in place of a blended fresh-chile sauce are the specific, checkable markers. Iceberg lettuce and sour cream are further near-universal Tex-Mex garnishes rarely seen on the same dishes in Mexico. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [HISTORY — Tracing the History of Tex-Mex](https://www.history.com/articles/tracing-the-history-of-tex-mex); [Homesick Texan — Essence of Tex-Mex](https://www.homesicktexan.com/essence-of-tex-mex/)]

## VISUAL & PLATING NORMS

- **Texas BBQ's defining visual marker is a dark, heavily-peppered "bark" exterior with little to no sauce sheen** — coarse black pepper mixed into the rub builds a nearly black, matte, deeply cracked crust distinct from a glossy, sauce-lacquered exterior. A properly rendered Central Texas brisket slice shows a thin, deep-pink "smoke ring" just under the bark when cut crosswise — a genuine, checkable doneness/smoke marker, not an artistic color choice. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Food Fidelity — Texas-style Slow Smoked Beef Brisket](https://www.foodfidelity.com/smoked-beef-brisket/)]
- **Tex-Mex's palette is warmer and more saturated than either Mexican or New Mexican plating** — melted yellow cheddar/Jack cheese, a uniform reddish-brown chili gravy, and bright-white sour cream/iceberg lettuce garnishes together read as more dairy-forward and less chile-pod-forward than a New Mexican plate (`us-new-mexico.md`), and more cheese-and-cumin forward than a plate from Mexico itself.
- **The sizzling cast-iron skillet is Tex-Mex's single most theatrical, checkable staging beat** — visible steam/smoke rising off the skillet as it's carried to the table is a real, sourced, dramatic serving convention specific to fajitas (see that entry), not a generic "hot food" cliché.
- **Doneness convention for Texas beef**: brisket and other slow-smoked BBQ meats should read as fully cooked through and fork-tender, not pink/rare at the center (the pink "smoke ring" noted above is a thin band just under the surface, not a pink interior throughout) — a genuinely different doneness signal than a grilled steak, where a pink/rare center is often the correct, desirable result.
- **Texture/finish specifically**: BBQ bark should read craggy, matte, and deeply cracked, not glossy; chicken-fried steak's crust should read shatteringly crisp and craggy under a matte flour dredge, not smooth or thin; a puffy taco's shell should show visible air-pocket blistering and an uneven, organic puffed shape, not a uniform machine-pressed hard shell; kolache dough should read soft, matte, and slightly domed, not glossy/egg-washed to a shine.

## ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES — zone deltas from `us.md`'s national baseline

- **The BBQ meat-market/smokehouse venue register** is Texas's single most distinct "away from home" environment, genuinely different from a Carolina/Memphis BBQ restaurant (`us-south.md`) or a KC BBQ joint (`us-midwest.md`): a walk-up order counter (often cafeteria-line style), meat sliced to order in full view of the customer, a roll of butcher/kraft paper at the register, meat served on paper on a metal cafeteria-style tray rather than a plate, long communal picnic-style tables (frequently indoors, under a tin or metal roof, with visible pit-room smoke haze), and minimal-to-no decor. **Per the trademark-avoidance rule**, describe the generic category — a real, historically-attested register (Kreuz Market, Smitty's Market, Southside Market, Louie Mueller Barbecue are the cited historical examples, never to be named or reproduced in an actual prompt). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Texas Monthly — My Hunt for the Oldest BBQ Joint in Texas Led Me to Kreuz Market](https://www.texasmonthly.com/bbq/kreuz-market-lockhart-history/); [KXAN — How did Lockhart become the Barbecue Capital of Texas?](https://www.kxan.com/kxan-documentaries/family-beef/how-did-lockhart-become-the-barbecue-capital-of-texas/)]
- **The Tex-Mex sit-down restaurant register**: chip-basket-and-salsa (or queso) service delivered unprompted at the start of the meal; a combination-plate menu format (multiple items — an enchilada, a taco, rice and beans — assembled as one numbered "combo" rather than ordered as fully separate courses); the sizzling fajita-skillet tableside delivery as the register's single most photogenic moment. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- **Hill Country German/Czech bakery and meat-market register**: a small-town storefront bakery counter (kolaches under glass) or a butcher-shop-style meat market with smoked-sausage links, evoking Fredericksburg, New Braunfels, West, La Grange, Caldwell, or Schulenburg — a genuinely distinct built environment and product mix from both the BBQ meat market above and a generic American bakery/deli. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- **Ranch/rural staging**: open rangeland, barbed-wire fencing, a windmill, and grazing cattle (longhorn cattle specifically are a real, sourced Texas visual identity, not an invented caricature, given the cattle-drive history above) are legitimate, well-supported rural Texas settings — but per the caricature-avoidance note above, should not be the default or only register for a generic "Texas" scene, given how urban/suburban most of the state's population actually is. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM for the visual convention]
- **Friday-night-football/county-fair Frito-pie concession stand — a genuine, sharp "meal on the go" contrast to the national car-centric default**, in the same spirit as `us-gulf-south.md`'s boudin-counter finding: Frito pie is specifically associated with Texas high-school football concession stands and county fairs, served directly out of a single-serving Fritos bag (chili and shredded cheese ladled straight into the opened bag, eaten with a plastic spoon while standing) rather than from a bowl. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Texas Standard — Frito pie, a mainstay under Friday night lights, is a Texas original](https://texasstandard.org/stories/frito-pie-history-texas/)]
- **South Texas/border register**: a to-go barbacoa counter doing brisk weekend-morning business (barbacoa is customarily a Sunday-morning-into-midday dish, sold by the pound for home consumption as much as eaten at a counter) — see the Barbacoa entry below for the full sourcing.

## DISH CATALOG

### Dish: Texas BBQ platter (full authoritative entry — supersedes `us.md`'s national-index BBQ pointer for Texas)

- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (a dedicated meat-market/BBQ-joint order as much as a cookout dish)
- Cuisine lineage: Native-Texan-tradition, with a directly sourced German/Czech immigrant-butcher-shop origin for the Central Texas style specifically (see ZONE CHARACTERIZATION above).
- Regional form variation: **Form-changing — a genuine four-way §4.6 coexisting-variants case within Texas itself, not resolved to a single default without disclosure.**

| Attribute | Central Texas (default/anchor) | East Texas | South Texas (barbacoa) | West Texas |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary protein | Beef brisket (also pork ribs, sausage) | Beef and pork equally; pulled pork common | Beef head/cheek (historically whole head) | Beef, cooked more like a grilled steak |
| Wood/method | Post oak (also pecan, mesquite), indirect smoke, low-and-slow (12–18 hrs) | Hickory, very long cook, "falling off the bone" | Underground pit, wrapped in maguey/agave leaves or burlap, slow-cooked overnight | Mesquite, direct heat — closer to grilling than smoking |
| Sauce | None to minimal; served on the side, never pre-applied | Sweet, tomato-based sauce, often marinated/basted | None — a taco filling, not a sauced platter | Minimal; char from direct heat is the flavor driver |
| Rub/seasoning | Salt and black pepper only (Central Texas specifically); other rubs elsewhere in the state | Sauce-forward rather than rub-forward | Salt; flavor comes from the slow pit-cook itself | Simple salt/pepper, direct-heat char |
| Serving format | Sliced meat by the pound on butcher paper | Plate or sandwich (pulled pork) | Shredded, folded into corn tortilla tacos | Plate, steak-like presentation |

  [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for Central Texas, East Texas, and West Texas descriptions; HIGH for barbacoa's protein/method, treated here as a cross-reference to its own full entry below rather than duplicated in full] [SOURCE: [MasterClass — A Guide to Texas Barbecue: 4 Styles of Texas BBQ](https://www.masterclass.com/articles/a-guide-to-texas-barbecue); [The Telegraph — Texas barbecue and the different styles per region of the state](https://www.thetelegraph.com/news/article/texas-barbecue-regions-18121156.php)]
- **Default when unspecified**: Central Texas style — the most nationally recognized and most heavily documented register, and the one `us.md`'s own pointer text already describes (dry rub, minimal sauce, post-oak smoke). This is an editorial default for an unspecified brief, not a suppression of the other three — per `country-file-schema.md` §4.6, a brief naming East Texas, South Texas, or West Texas should render that variant instead.
- Serving format: Plate/tray (sliced meat by the pound on butcher paper, Central Texas); sandwich (pulled pork, East Texas); taco (barbacoa, see separate entry); plate (West Texas, steak-like).
- Side dishes/condiments: White bread (plain sandwich bread, used to blot grease or make an impromptu sandwich, not toasted), dill pickle slices, raw sliced onion, and pickled jalapeños are near-universal, usually provided free rather than as a paid side; a smoked sausage link (often a coarse-ground "hot gut" style) is a common accompanying item rather than a garnish; potato salad, pinto beans, and coleslaw are common paid sides. Barbecue sauce, when offered at all in Central Texas, sits in a squeeze bottle on the table — never pre-applied by the cook. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Serving vessel: A sheet of butcher/kraft paper laid directly on a metal cafeteria-style tray — **not a plate** — for the Central Texas meat-market register; this is a checkable, non-substitutable authenticity marker.
- Real-world size (§4.5): Brisket is sliced to order — the flat cut roughly ¼ inch thick, the fattier point cut roughly ⅜ inch thick per slice, each slice's width spanning the width of the brisket muscle itself (roughly 3–5 inches). A standard individual order is sold by weight (commonly a half-pound to one pound of sliced meat) rather than a fixed plate-portion, so — per `country-file-schema.md` §4.5's vessel-fallback rule — a half-pound portion should visually cover roughly one-third to one-half of a standard cafeteria tray (~35×25cm, comparable to the "rectangular serving vessel" dimension in `tableware-composition-reference.md` §2), laid out in overlapping slices rather than stacked. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH for the slice thickness figures; LOW-MEDIUM for the tray-coverage inference, this file's own synthesis, not an independently sourced measurement] [SOURCE: [Food Fidelity — Texas-style Slow Smoked Beef Brisket](https://www.foodfidelity.com/smoked-beef-brisket/)]
- Visual/plating characteristics: See VISUAL & PLATING NORMS above for the bark/smoke-ring detail. Slices should be laid slightly overlapping, fanned across the paper rather than stacked in a tall pile; the paper itself should show visible grease translucency and rendered-fat staining underneath the meat — a real, checkable authenticity detail (a perfectly clean, dry paper reads as understaffed/inauthentic). Sausage links, when present, show a taut, deep mahogany-brown casing with a visible "snap" split when cut, distinct from brisket's loose muscle fibers.
- Common confusion: Carolina/Memphis BBQ (`us-south.md`) — pork-centered, visibly sauced (thin vinegar-clear or thick tomato-red/mustard-yellow), plated rather than paper-served. Kansas City BBQ (`us-midwest.md`) — thick, glossy, dark tomato-molasses sauce coating the meat, the visual opposite of Central Texas's dry, matte bark. East Texas's own sweet tomato-sauced style is the closest internal confusable to Kansas City's — the sauce color/thickness convention, not the meat choice, is the checkable differentiator there.
- Confidence: HIGH overall for the four-way regional structure and Central Texas's own detail; see individual cells above for finer-grained confidence.
- Sources: [Black's BBQ — A Guide to Central Texas Barbecue Traditions](https://www.blacksbbq.com/resources/blog/central-texas-bbq-guide); [Authentic Texas — The History of Texas Barbecue, Brisket and Smoke](https://authentictexas.com/up-in-smoke/); [Texas Historical Commission — Bringing Texas Barbecue History to the Table](https://thc.texas.gov/blog/bringing-texas-barbecue-history-table); [MasterClass — A Guide to Texas Barbecue](https://www.masterclass.com/articles/a-guide-to-texas-barbecue); [Texas Monthly — Kreuz Market history](https://www.texasmonthly.com/bbq/kreuz-market-lockhart-history/)

### Dish: Barbacoa (South Texas) — a genuine sibling to the BBQ platter above, given its own full entry rather than a variant-table row

- Category: Everyday to special-occasion — a Sunday-morning-into-midday tradition, not an evening BBQ-platter dish.
- Cuisine lineage: Tejano/vaquero ranch-hand tradition, tracing to the 19th century when Anglo cattle ranchers passed unwanted cow heads to their Mexican ranch hands, who developed the underground-pit cooking method still associated with the dish today. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Houston Chronicle — Barbacoa and the tradition of Tejano barbecue](https://www.houstonchronicle.com/entertainment/restaurants-bars/bbq/article/Barbacoa-and-the-tradition-of-Tejano-barbecue-6192235.php)]
- Regional form variation: South Texas-specific; not documented as practiced with the same whole-head/underground-pit method elsewhere in Texas.
- Primary protein: Traditionally a whole beef head, wrapped in maguey (agave) leaves or burlap and buried in a pit lined with hot coals, cooked slowly overnight (roughly a full day). **Modern practice has shifted almost entirely to beef cheeks** — regulations intended to prevent disease transmission now generally prohibit selling whole-head-cooked barbacoa commercially; only one still-operating Texas restaurant (in Brownsville) retains a specific historical exemption to sell the traditional whole-head version. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [AmazingRibs.com — This Whole Head, Pit Cooked Barbacoa Keeps Texas Traditions Alive](https://amazingribs.com/this-whole-head-pit-cooked-barbacoa-keeps-texas-traditions-alive/); [Texas Monthly — How to Cook Authentic Barbacoa](https://www.texasmonthly.com/food/how-to-cook-authentic-barbacoa/)]

- Side dishes/condiments: Chopped onion, cilantro, salsa, and lime are standard taco garnishes; no BBQ sauce of any kind — this is a Mexican taco-format dish, not a sauced American BBQ platter.
- Serving vessel: Small corn tortillas (street-taco-sized, roughly 4–5 inches diameter) as the handheld vessel, or a foil/paper-wrapped bulk portion sold by weight.
- Real-world size (§4.5): Individual tortillas roughly 4–5 inches across (comparable in width to a standard drink coaster, or a bit wider than the Coca-Cola can's own 2.6-inch diameter); a typical taco order is 2–3 tacos, each folded rather than rolled, with shredded meat visible along the open top edge rather than fully enclosed. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — the tortilla-size figure is this file's own inference from general "street taco" sizing conventions, not independently sourced for barbacoa specifically]
- Visual/plating characteristics: The meat itself is dark, deeply shredded/pulled (not sliced), with a moist, almost gelatinous sheen from the long, fatty, slow-cooked cut; visibly darker and more uniformly shredded than brisket's fibrous, sliceable structure. Served warm, piled loosely into small, warm, slightly charred-spotted corn tortillas.
- Common confusion: **Central Texas brisket** (above) — barbacoa is shredded/pulled and taco-format, never sliced or served on a platter/tray; brisket is sliced and never served in a small taco. **Mexican-interior barbacoa traditions** (lamb/goat, different regional method) are a related but distinct dish family outside this file's scope. **Carnitas** — a confusable at the "shredded meat taco" level, but carnitas is pork, fried/confited rather than pit-smoked, and has a crispier, browned exterior on some pieces; barbacoa is exclusively beef in the Texas tradition and uniformly moist/shredded with no fried crisp element.

### Dish: Texas chili — "bowl of red" (full authoritative entry — supersedes `us.md`'s national-index Chili pointer for Texas)

- Category: Everyday to special-occasion — a competition/cookoff dish as much as a home/restaurant dish, and Texas's official state dish.
- Cuisine lineage: Native-Texan-tradition, traced to San Antonio's "chili queens" — women who sold carne con chile (chili con carne) from open-air plazas from the 1880s onward, a dime a bowl with bread and water. Chili con carne was introduced to a national audience at the 1893 Chicago World's Fair via a "San Antonio Chili Stand." [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [statesymbolsusa.org — Texas State Dish: Chili](https://statesymbolsusa.org/symbol-official-item/texas/state-food-agriculture-symbol/chili)]
- Regional form variation: **Form-changing at the national level** (`us.md` already documents the Cincinnati-chili sibling); within Texas itself, the "bowl of red" is uniform in its beans-excluded, meat-forward identity — no further internal Texas split found.
- History/official status: Designated Texas's official state dish on May 11, 1977, when Governor Dolph Briscoe signed House Concurrent Resolution No. 18, explicitly stating recognition of "the fact that the only real 'bowl of red' is that prepared by Texans." [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- **Competition/cookoff culture**: The Original Terlingua International Championship Chili Cookoff, founded in 1967 as a publicity-stunt duel between New York humorist H. Allen Smith and Texas journalist Wick Fowler, is the world's oldest continuous chili competition and the direct origin of the modern chili-cookoff circuit; the first cookoff's judging ended in a contested tie. Beans were banned from the competition format essentially from the outset — beans are treated as a filler that can mask a weak chili recipe — and the ban persists at Terlingua and most competitive chili organizations today (a rift over exactly this issue split the founding group into two rival organizing bodies, CASI and the International Chili Society). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Texas Highways — A Look At Terlingua's Chili Origins](https://texashighways.com/culture/history/a-look-at-terlinguas-chili-origins/); [InsideHook — The First Rule of Competition-Style Texas Chili? No Beans.](https://www.insidehook.com/food-texas/competition-style-texas-chili-recipe-no-beans)]
- Primary protein/composition: Chunks of beef (traditionally chuck roast or a similar tougher cut, cut into cubes, not ground), simmered for a long time in a chile-pepper-and-spice-forward liquid (dried chile pods and/or chili powder, cumin, garlic) until fork-tender; **beans are strictly excluded** — a disqualifying ingredient in essentially every competitive Texas chili context, and considered inauthentic to the "bowl of red" concept even outside competition. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Side dishes/condiments: Shredded cheese, chopped raw onion, and crackers or cornbread are common at-the-table accompaniments; these sit beside, not stirred into, the chili itself.
- Serving vessel: A deep individual bowl — per `tableware-composition-reference.md` §2's side-bowl-medium dimension (15–18cm diameter) for a starter/side portion, or a slightly larger individual soup bowl for a main-course portion — filled generously to near the rim, not served as a thin garnish-sized portion.
- Real-world size (§4.5): Beef chunks should read distinctly larger than a ground-meat chili's crumbled texture — roughly ¾ to 1 inch cubes (comparable to a large sugar cube or a game die), visibly whole and falling-apart-tender rather than uniformly small/minced. Per the vessel fallback above, the chili should fill a 15–18cm bowl to within roughly 1cm of the rim, thick enough that a spoon stood upright in the center would not immediately fall over. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — the cube-size comparison is this file's own visual synthesis from recipe-tier sourcing describing "chunks," not an independently measured figure]
- Visual/plating characteristics: A thick, deep brick-red-to-reddish-brown stew, opaque rather than clear/brothy, with large visible chunks of beef breaking apart at the edges (not a smooth, uniform ground-meat suspension), suspended in a glossy, clinging (not thin/watery) sauce. No visible beans of any kind, and no visible pasta (which would suggest Cincinnati chili instead). **Texture/finish**: the sauce should look matte-glossy and thick enough to coat the back of a spoon, not thin or separated/oily on the surface.
- Common confusion: **Cincinnati chili** (`us-midwest.md`) — thinner, gravy-like, ground (not chunked) meat, served over spaghetti, a completely different serving format. **A generic ground-beef "chili con carne" with beans** (a common home-cooking/canned-product default nationally) — the absence of beans and the whole-meat-chunk texture (not ground) are the two checkable, defining differences from Texas chili specifically.
- Confidence: HIGH overall for history, official status, and composition.
- Sources: [statesymbolsusa.org — Texas State Dish: Chili](https://statesymbolsusa.org/symbol-official-item/texas/state-food-agriculture-symbol/chili); [Texas Highways — A Look At Terlingua's Chili Origins](https://texashighways.com/culture/history/a-look-at-terlinguas-chili-origins/); [Texas Hill Country — Texas Chili History](https://texashillcountry.com/texas-chili-history-state-dish/); [The Seasoned Mom — Texas Chili](https://www.theseasonedmom.com/texas-chili/)

### Dish: Tex-Mex tacos — hard-shell ground-beef style (full authoritative entry — supersedes `us.md`'s national-index Tacos pointer for the Tex-Mex variant)

- Category: Everyday
- Cuisine lineage: Tex-Mex, a Tejano-developed adaptation distinct from a Mexican-interior taco — see ZONE CHARACTERIZATION and TRUSTED CONTENT above for the specific ingredient substitutions (yellow cheese, sour cream, iceberg lettuce, ground beef, heavy cumin) that define the Tex-Mex form.
- Regional form variation: **Two genuine, coexisting Texas-internal variants per §4.6**, plus the breakfast taco as a distinct serving-occasion sibling (its own entry below): (1) **Hard-shell ground-beef taco (this entry's primary form)** — the most nationally recognized Tex-Mex taco format. (2) **Puffy taco (San Antonio-specific)** — see the note below; genuinely distinct enough in construction to warrant its own visual treatment even though it shares the same fillings.
- Serving format: Handheld.
- Primary protein: Ground beef, seasoned heavily with cumin, chili powder, and garlic — browned and lightly sauced, not a wet stew filling.
- Side dishes/condiments: Shredded cheddar (yellow, not a Mexican white cheese), shredded iceberg lettuce, diced tomato, and sour cream are the near-universal Tex-Mex taco toppings — all rare or absent on a Mexican-interior taco. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Serving vessel: Plate, typically 2–3 tacos per order, propped upright against each other or a taco holder rather than lying flat.
- Real-world size (§4.5): A hard corn-tortilla taco shell measures roughly 5–6 inches across before folding (comparable to the diameter of a small side plate, or a bit shorter than a standard smartphone's length) — folded into its U-shape, the finished shell stands roughly 3–4 inches tall and 5–6 inches long, distinctly shorter in length than the Coca-Cola can is tall (4.83in/12.3cm). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — the "street taco sized" comparison is drawn from recipe-tier sourcing, not an independently measured industry-standard figure]
- Visual/plating characteristics: A golden-brown, crisp-fried, U-shaped hard corn-tortilla shell (not a soft, pliable tortilla) with visible small blisters/bubbles on its surface from frying; browned, crumbled ground beef visible inside, topped with a layer of melted-to-softened bright-yellow shredded cheese, then a mound of bright-green shredded iceberg lettuce and diced red tomato piled toward the open top, often finished with a visible dollop of white sour cream. **Texture/finish**: the shell should show a matte, slightly craggy fried surface with occasional small cracks (a sign of proper crisp-frying), not a smooth, glossy, uniformly machine-extruded look.
- Common confusion: A Mexican-interior soft taco (small soft corn tortilla, no hard shell, no yellow cheese/sour cream/iceberg lettuce, simpler fresh-onion-and-cilantro garnish) is the primary confusable — the hard shell and the specific dairy/lettuce garnishes are the checkable Tex-Mex markers. A Baja fish taco (`us-west-coast.md`, `us.md`) is a more distant confusable ruled out by protein and garnish (battered fish and cabbage/crema, not ground beef and cheddar).
- **Puffy taco note (San Antonio-specific coexisting variant)**: invented in the 1950s at a San Antonio drive-in by cook Henry Lopez, who experimented with deep-frying uncooked masa dough rather than a pre-formed hard shell — the shell puffs up in the fryer into a rounded, domed, air-pocketed pillow rather than a flat, crisp, U-shaped shell. Filled most commonly with picadillo (seasoned ground beef with diced potato, bell pepper, and onion) or shredded chicken, then topped the same way as the hard-shell version (lettuce, tomato, cheese). Visually, a puffy taco shows an irregular, organically bulging, golden-brown fried surface with visible large air pockets when broken open — a distinctly different silhouette from the flat-sided, uniformly U-shaped hard-shell taco above, and should not be depicted interchangeably with it when a scene is specifically San Antonio-set. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the invention story and construction] [SOURCE: [Texas Monthly — The Delicate History of the Puffy Taco](https://www.texasmonthly.com/food/the-delicate-history-of-the-puffy-taco/)]
- Confidence: HIGH overall for the hard-shell Tex-Mex form and its distinguishing garnishes; HIGH for the puffy taco's origin and construction.
- Sources: [How to Feed a Loon — Authentic Tex-Mex Beef Tacos](https://howtofeedaloon.com/authentic-texmex-beef-tacos/); [Wikipedia: Hard-shell taco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard-shell_taco); [Texas Monthly — The Delicate History of the Puffy Taco](https://www.texasmonthly.com/food/the-delicate-history-of-the-puffy-taco/)

### Dish: Tex-Mex enchiladas (full authoritative entry — supersedes `us.md`'s national-index Enchiladas pointer for the Tex-Mex variant; builds on, does not duplicate, `us.md`'s existing Tex-Mex-vs-New-Mexican comparison)

- Category: Everyday to special-occasion
- Cuisine lineage: Tex-Mex, adapted by Tejano home cooks from a Mexican ancestor using ingredients available in general/country stores.
- Regional form variation: `us.md` already documents the core form-changing distinction against New Mexican enchiladas (rolled vs. stacked; chili-gravy vs. chile-pod sauce) at HIGH confidence — not re-derived here. This entry adds the **chili gravy's own composition and history**, which `us.md`'s comparison-focused entry didn't need in full depth.
- **Chili gravy — composition and history**: Tex-Mex chili gravy is built like an American brown gravy, not like a Mexican chile sauce: flour is cooked into melted fat (butter or oil) to form a roux, browned to a caramel/peanut-butter color, then chili powder and other dry spices (garlic powder, onion powder, oregano, cumin) are "bloomed" in the hot roux before broth and often a small amount of tomato paste are stirred in to thicken. Two specific, sourced, causal reasons this technique (rather than a blended fresh/dried-chile-pod sauce) became the Tex-Mex standard: (1) Tejano home cooks already knew how to make a roux from their German and Czech neighbors (the same immigrant populations behind this file's BBQ and chicken-fried-steak entries), and (2) pre-mixed commercial chili powder became available from Gebhardt's starting in 1894, making it easier to stock and use than whole dried chile pods. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — a specific, detailed, internally consistent causal account, sourced to a small number of Tex-Mex-food-history-tier sources rather than an academic/primary source] [SOURCE: [Homesick Texan — Cheese enchiladas: the essence of Tex-Mex](https://www.homesicktexan.com/essence-of-tex-mex/); [House of Yumm — Best Ever Enchilada Sauce Recipe (Tex Mex Chili Gravy)](https://houseofyumm.com/best-ever-enchilada-sauce-recipe/)]
- Serving format: Plate — see `us.md` for the rolled/tortilla-handling detail, not repeated here.
- Serving vessel/real-world size (§4.5): Per `tableware-composition-reference.md` §2's fallback, a standard order of 2–3 rolled corn tortillas (each roughly 4–5 inches long before rolling, becoming a cylinder roughly 1.5 inches in diameter once rolled and filled) arranged parallel to each other, occupies roughly half to two-thirds of a standard 26–28cm entree plate, fully blanketed in chili gravy and melted cheese so individual tortilla edges are not visible — a checkable difference from a New Mexican stacked presentation, where tortilla-layer edges remain visible in cross-section. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — the size figures are this file's own reasonable inference from standard corn-tortilla dimensions, not independently sourced for this specific dish]
- Visual/plating characteristics: See `us.md`'s existing entry for the full sauce-color/rolling comparison against New Mexican enchiladas — not re-derived here. Additional detail from this pass: the chili gravy's roux base gives it a slightly matte, opaque, uniformly thick consistency (closer to a warm brown/red gravy than a thin, translucent chile-pod sauce), and it should coat the tortillas heavily enough that no bare tortilla surface shows through before the cheese layer is added on top.
- Common confusion: See `us.md`'s existing entry (New Mexican stacked enchiladas) — not re-derived here.
- Confidence: HIGH for the rolled/chili-gravy form generally (per `us.md`); MEDIUM-HIGH for the chili gravy's specific causal-history account added in this pass.

### Dish: Fajitas (new full entry — a real, dated, Texas-specific invention)

- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (a common weeknight Tex-Mex restaurant order, but also a genuine "special/theatrical" order given its tableside presentation)
- Cuisine lineage: Tex-Mex, with a directly traceable South Texas ranch-hand (vaquero) origin, not a Mexican-interior dish.
- History: The earliest documented form traces to 1930s South/West Texas ranch lands, where skirt steak — a tough, low-value cut given to vaqueros as part of their pay when cattle were slaughtered for ranch-hand consumption during roundups — was grilled directly over a campfire. The name derives from the Spanish "faja" (belt/girdle), referencing the cut's shape. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Austin Chronicle — Fajita History](https://www.austinchronicle.com/food/fajita-history-11721578/)] Sonny Falcon, an Austin meat-market manager, is credited with operating the first commercial fajita-taco concession stand at a Diez y Seis (Mexican Independence Day) celebration in Kyle, Texas in September 1969, introducing the dish to a broad public at rodeos, fairs, and festivals. Independently, restaurateurs Otilia Garza and Ninfa Laurenzo ("Mama Ninfa," whose Houston restaurant opened in 1973) are credited with popularizing fajitas in a full sit-down restaurant format during the 1970s. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH — multiple named, dated, independently corroborated claimants, a genuinely multi-origin story rather than one contested single inventor] [SOURCE: [Austin American-Statesman — Austin's Fajita King](https://www.statesman.com/story/lifestyle/food/2019/09/25/why-you-can-thank-austin-man-for-your-sizzling-fajitas/2696961007/)]
- **The sizzling cast-iron skillet presentation**: credited to Otilia Garza, who encountered queso flameado served on a hot cast-iron plate in Acapulco and adapted the presentation for fajitas — turning a practical ranch dish into a deliberately theatrical restaurant centerpiece: the skillet visibly smokes/steams and audibly sizzles as it's carried from kitchen to table. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Food and the Story — The Sizzle That Changed Houston](https://foodandthestory.substack.com/p/the-sizzle-that-changed-houston)]
- Serving format: A two-part table presentation — a sizzling skillet of the grilled meat and vegetables delivered separately from a stack of warm tortillas and a tray of garnishes, assembled by the diner at the table rather than pre-assembled by the kitchen. This is a genuine, checkable serving-format distinction from a taco or enchilada, where the kitchen does the assembly.
- Primary protein: Skirt steak (the historical and still-classic default; chicken and shrimp fajitas are common modern menu variants but the beef skirt-steak version is the dish's authentic anchor), grilled and then sliced into strips against the grain.
- Side dishes/condiments: Grilled onion and bell pepper strips cooked alongside the meat in the same skillet; warm flour tortillas, guacamole, pico de gallo, sour cream, and shredded cheese served separately for diner self-assembly.
- Serving vessel: An oval or round cast-iron fajita skillet, roughly 9–10 inches at its longest dimension — smaller and shallower than a communal serving platter, sized for one skillet per 1–2 diners rather than a large shared family-style vessel.
- Real-world size (§4.5): Individual meat strips measure roughly 4–5 inches long and ½ inch wide (comparable to a AAA battery in length); the skillet itself, at roughly 9–10 inches, is close to but slightly smaller than a standard 26–28cm (~10–11 inch) entree plate per `tableware-composition-reference.md` §2, distinguishing it visually from a full-sized dinner plate at a glance. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — reasonable synthesis from standard cast-iron-skillet product sizing and typical meat-slicing convention, not independently sourced as an exact dish measurement]
- Visual/plating characteristics: The skillet's meat and vegetables should show visible char/grill marks and a glossy sheen from oil, with the onions and peppers translucent-to-lightly-browned (not raw-crisp or blackened); **visible rising steam/light smoke** is the single most important, checkable staging detail for this dish specifically — an un-smoking, static-looking skillet undersells the dish's defining visual convention. Tortillas should be warm, soft, and slightly puffed/blistered in places (griddle-warmed, not cold or stiff), stacked in a small cloth-lined basket or wrapped in foil beside the skillet.
- Common confusion: Not carne asada (a plated, already-sliced grilled steak served without the tableside self-assembly ritual or the sizzling skillet); not stir-fry (different seasoning profile and garnish set, and no self-assembly-with-tortillas convention).
- Confidence: HIGH for the multi-claimant origin history and the sizzling-skillet presentation's specific origin story; HIGH for composition and serving format.
- Sources: [Austin Chronicle — Fajita History](https://www.austinchronicle.com/food/fajita-history-11721578/); [Austin American-Statesman — Austin's Fajita King](https://www.statesman.com/story/lifestyle/food/2019/09/25/why-you-can-thank-austin-man-for-your-sizzling-fajitas/2696961007/); [Food and the Story — The Sizzle That Changed Houston](https://foodandthestory.substack.com/p/the-sizzle-that-changed-houston); [Wikipedia: Fajita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fajita)

### Dish: Chile con queso ("queso")

- Category: Everyday snack/appetizer
- Cuisine lineage: Tex-Mex, with a documented cross-border/cross-cultural construction — not a straightforward Mexican import.
- History: The earliest published recipe traces to an 1896 US magazine ("The Land of Sunshine"), under the name "chiles verdes con queso," in a chile-forward (not cheese-forward) form. Texas restaurant service is documented from at least 1910 (San Antonio's Gunter Hotel); one restaurant claims 1900 service without surviving documentation. Sources describe a later evolution merging the Mexican chile-and-cheese concept with the rising popularity of Welsh rarebit and European-style fondue, arriving at the molten, dippable form known today — a genuine Tex-Mex/European-American hybrid, not a straightforward transplant from Mexico. The dish stayed a regional Texas/Southwest specialty for decades; it reached wider national attention only after First Lady Lady Bird Johnson published her own recipe in the Washington Post in 1964, though broader popularization took additional decades. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — a specific, multi-source-corroborated timeline, though several individual claims (exact 1900 date, rarebit connection) rest on a single food-history article each] [SOURCE: [Food Republic — The Homesick Texan's Brief History Of Chile Con Queso](https://www.foodrepublic.com/2017/10/03/history-chile-con-queso/)]
- Serving format: Snack/appetizer, a shared communal dip — never a plated individual course.
- Primary composition: Melted processed or American-style cheese (yellow, not a Mexican white cheese) combined with diced green chiles and/or tomato, kept warm and semi-liquid rather than fully firm.
- Side dishes/condiments: Tortilla chips are the near-universal dipping vehicle; some restaurant versions ("Bob Armstrong" style) top the queso with taco meat, guacamole, and pico de gallo as a more elaborate loaded-queso variant — a real, coexisting, more special-occasion-coded register alongside the plain version.
- Serving vessel: A small-to-medium round bowl or crock — per `tableware-composition-reference.md` §2, roughly a "condiment bowl/dish" (10–12cm) for an individual portion or a "side bowl — medium" (15–18cm) for a shared appetizer portion — accompanied by a larger basket of tortilla chips.
- Real-world size (§4.5): Per the vessel fallback above, a shared queso order should fill a 15–18cm bowl to within roughly 1–2cm of the rim, with a smooth, level, glossy surface (not mounded or peaked, since it's a pourable, semi-liquid dip rather than a solid dish).
- Visual/plating characteristics: A smooth, glossy, uniformly melted orange-to-yellow surface (a slight orange tint from diced tomato/chile is common but the base color reads distinctly yellow-orange, not white), with small visible flecks of diced green chile and/or red tomato suspended throughout — never a grainy, separated, or oily-surfaced appearance, which would suggest an improperly melted/broken batch. **Texture/finish**: the surface should show a slight sheen and a slow-moving, thick-liquid consistency (visible slow dripping when a chip is lifted out), not a stiff, solid, fully-set block.
- Common confusion: Not guacamole (green, chunky, unmelted) or a tomato-based salsa (thin, translucent, no melted-cheese component). Not queso flameado (a distinct, drier, skillet-melted-cheese dish without queso's diced-chile liquid-dip consistency).
- Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH for the history; HIGH for composition and visual description.
- Sources: [Food Republic — The Homesick Texan's Brief History Of Chile Con Queso](https://www.foodrepublic.com/2017/10/03/history-chile-con-queso/); [Wikipedia: Chile con queso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chile_con_queso)

### Dish: Chicken-fried steak

- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (a classic diner/home-cooking comfort dish, but also a "signature," oversized-portion showpiece dish at some Texas restaurants)
- Cuisine lineage: **Contested — two genuinely competing origin accounts, disclosed honestly rather than resolved to one.** (1) The majority food-history account credits German and Austrian immigrants to the Texas Hill Country (see ZONE CHARACTERIZATION above), who adapted their Wiener schnitzel tradition (a thin, breaded, pan-fried veal or pork cutlet) to Texas's cheap, abundant beef once German settlement took hold from the 1840s. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — the majority, more frequently cited account] (2) A specific competing legend credits a short-order cook, Jimmy Don Perkins, in Lamesa, Texas, with accidentally inventing the dish in 1911 by garbling two separate orders (fried chicken and chicken-fried steak) into one. [CONFIDENCE: LOW-MEDIUM — a single, specific, folkloric attribution, the kind of origin legend this project treats cautiously elsewhere] [SOURCE: [TSHA — The History and Preparation of Chicken-Fried Steak](https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/chicken-fried-steak); [Chowhound — What Texans Get Wrong About The Origin Story Of Chicken Fried Steak](https://www.chowhound.com/2144420/texas-chicken-fried-steak-origin-story/)]
- Regional form variation: Not documented as form-changing within Texas; prevalence-only variation exists nationally (the dish is also strongly associated with Oklahoma and the broader South), but Texas is the most consistently cited origin/identity claimant.
- Serving format: Plate.
- Primary protein: A cubed or tenderized beef round steak, pounded thin, breaded (flour-dredged, sometimes egg-washed and re-dredged for a thicker crust), and pan- or deep-fried — the "chicken-fried" name refers to the fried-chicken-style breading/frying technique applied to beef, not to any chicken content.
- Side dishes/condiments: **Cream (white) gravy, made from the pan drippings, is a non-negotiable, near-universal accompaniment** — ladled generously over the steak or served in its own side vessel when the main plate has no room left. Mashed potatoes and green beans or other simple vegetables are the standard plate-mates.
- Serving vessel: A large dinner plate; at well-known signature-dish restaurants, the steak is deliberately cut/pounded oversized enough to visibly overhang the plate's edge — a real, sourced, checkable presentation convention at some establishments, though not the universal everyday-home-cooking portion size.
- Real-world size (§4.5): An everyday home/diner portion is roughly 6–8 inches across (comparable to a large slice of bread laid flat) and about ¼–½ inch thick before breading; a "showpiece" restaurant portion can span 10+ inches, genuinely exceeding a standard 26–28cm (~10–11 inch) entree plate's diameter and visibly overhanging its rim on one or more sides — this is a real, sourced, documented restaurant convention, not an invented exaggeration, but should be treated as the special/showpiece register rather than the default unless a brief specifically calls for it. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH for the showpiece-portion overhang convention, sourced to specific named restaurants' own documented reputation; MEDIUM for the standard/everyday portion size, this file's own reasonable inference] [SOURCE: [We3Travel — 10 Texas Chicken-Fried Steak Plates Bigger Than the State Fair](https://we3travel.com/texas-10-chicken-fried-steak-plates-bigger-than-the-state-fair/)]
- Visual/plating characteristics: A flat, wide, golden-to-deep-brown fried cutlet with a craggy, uneven, shatteringly crisp breaded crust (not a smooth, thin coating) — the crust should show visible texture and small raised bumps/ridges from the flour dredge, similar in spirit to the fried-chicken breading already documented in `us.md`, applied here to a flat steak rather than bone-in chicken pieces. The cream gravy is opaque, pale ivory-to-beige (not brown, which would suggest a different gravy style), speckled with visible cracked black pepper, and should look thick enough to cling to the meat rather than pooling thin and watery. **Texture/finish**: the crust should read dry and matte where not covered in gravy, and the interior meat, when cut, should show a uniform gray-brown well-done color throughout (not pink), consistent with a thin, fully-cooked cutlet.
- Common confusion: Country-fried steak (a near-synonym in some regions, sometimes distinguished by using brown gravy instead of cream gravy — this file did not find a fully settled, universally agreed distinction and flags this as a genuine, unresolved naming ambiguity). Schnitzel itself (the direct ancestor) is typically served without a thick cream gravy and often with a lemon wedge instead — the gravy is the Americanized, Texan-specific departure point.
- Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH overall; the origin story is explicitly contested (see above) and flagged rather than resolved.
- Sources: [TSHA — The History and Preparation of Chicken-Fried Steak](https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/chicken-fried-steak); [Chowhound — What Texans Get Wrong About The Origin Story Of Chicken Fried Steak](https://www.chowhound.com/2144420/texas-chicken-fried-steak-origin-story/); [We3Travel — 10 Texas Chicken-Fried Steak Plates Bigger Than the State Fair](https://we3travel.com/texas-10-chicken-fried-steak-plates-bigger-than-the-state-fair/)

### Dish: Kolaches / klobasnek (Czech-Texan heritage)

- Category: Everyday snack — explicitly checked against this file's inherited breakfast exclusion; kolaches and klobasnek are sold and eaten all day at Texas bakeries and gas-station bakery counters, not confined to a morning meal, so this is staged as a snack/on-the-go item. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — inferred from the format's ubiquity at all-day-operating bakeries and gas stations rather than an explicit meal-timing study]
- Cuisine lineage: Czech-Texan heritage, brought by Czech immigrants who settled Central Texas's Blackland Prairie towns (West, Hallettsville, Schulenburg, La Grange, Caldwell) between the 1840s and 1910s. See ZONE CHARACTERIZATION above for the settlement history.
- Regional form variation: **Two genuinely distinct, coexisting forms under one popular Texas name, per §4.6** — a real naming-collapse worth surfacing explicitly, not silently defaulting to one: (1) **Kolache (sweet, the traditional/original form)**: a round or oval yeast-dough pastry with a shallow well pressed into the center, filled with fruit (poppy seed, apricot, prune, or cream cheese are classic) — the historically correct, original Czech form. (2) **Klobasnek/klobasnik (savory, sausage-filled)**: dough wrapped fully around a sausage link (and often cheese and/or jalapeño), fully enclosing the filling rather than leaving a well — technically a different, distinct pastry, but **widely and popularly called "kolache" throughout Texas** despite the technical distinction, to the point that most Texans use "kolache" for both. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the technical distinction and the popular-naming collapse] [SOURCE: [The Anthony Kitchen — Texas Kolaches (Klobasneks) with Sausage](https://www.theanthonykitchen.com/texas-kolaches/); [Tasting Table — How Kolaches Went From A Czech Secret To A Texas Staple](https://www.tastingtable.com/1938089/how-kolaches-went-czech-to-texas-staple-local/)]
- **Default when unspecified**: given the popular-naming collapse above, a Texas bakery counter scene should plausibly show **both forms side by side** (sweet fruit kolaches and savory sausage klobasnek in the same case) rather than picking one exclusively — this is a genuinely common real-world display convention, not an artificial hedge. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- Serving format: Handheld snack/pastry, sold individually or by the half-dozen/dozen.
- Serving vessel: Typically displayed loose in a bakery case or sold in a simple paper bag/box — no formal plated presentation.
- Real-world size (§4.5): A sweet kolache's dough disc is typically rolled to roughly 2-inch rounds before proofing/filling, rising to a finished diameter of roughly 3–4 inches (comparable to the diameter of a hockey puck stood up, or a bit larger than the Coca-Cola can's 2.6-inch diameter). A klobasnek is typically formed from an oval or rectangular dough piece roughly 4–5 inches long by 2.5 inches wide, wrapped around a sausage link cut to roughly 3 inches — the finished klobasnek is a roughly 4–5-inch oblong log, comparable in length to the Coca-Cola can's 4.83-inch height, though visibly plumper/more irregular in cross-section than the can's uniform cylinder. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — multiple broadly consistent recipe-tier sources on dough-piece dimensions, though not an institutional/industry-standard measurement]
- Visual/plating characteristics: **Sweet kolache**: a soft, matte, pale-golden, slightly domed round of yeast dough with a distinct shallow central depression filled with a visibly different-colored fruit or cheese filling (deep purple-black for poppy seed, orange for apricot, pale cream-white for cream cheese) — the dough itself should look soft and slightly matte, not shiny/glazed. **Klobasnek**: a fully enclosed, smooth, matte, oblong golden-brown dough log with no visible filling from the outside (unlike the sweet kolache's exposed center) — cut or bitten open, it reveals a plump sausage link, sometimes with a strip of melted cheese, running the log's full length.
- Common confusion: **Each other** — the exposed-filling well vs. fully-enclosed distinction is the single clearest, most checkable marker between the two forms, and a scene depicting one while the associated text calls it the other's name is a real, checkable authenticity risk given how often the names are casually swapped in everyday Texas usage. Not a plain Danish pastry (a Danish is typically flakier/laminated dough, not a soft yeast-roll texture) or a corn dog (a klobasnek's casing is baked bread dough, not a fried cornmeal batter).
- Confidence: HIGH for the settlement history and the two-form distinction; MEDIUM-HIGH for the specific size figures.
- Sources: [Southern Foodways Alliance — The Keepers of Kolaches: The Evolutions of Texas-Czech Baking](https://www.southernfoodways.org/oral-history/the-keepers-of-kolaches-the-evolutions-of-texas-czech-baking/); [Colorado River Land Trust — Kolache Road Trip](https://www.coloradoriverlandtrust.org/kolache-road-trip-tracing-the-sweet-buttery-roots-of-texas/); [The Anthony Kitchen — Texas Kolaches (Klobasneks) with Sausage](https://www.theanthonykitchen.com/texas-kolaches/)

### Dish: Frito pie (a real, dated, staging-relevant Texas invention)

- Category: Everyday snack, strongly tied to a specific staging-relevant venue (Friday-night-football and county-fair concession stands — see ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES above)
- Cuisine lineage: Native-Texan-tradition, built on the Fritos corn chip (itself invented in San Antonio, c. 1932, by Charles Elmer Doolin, who purchased the recipe from a Mexican restaurant and adapted it).
- Regional form variation: **A genuinely contested origin claim between Texas and New Mexico**, disclosed honestly rather than resolved: Texans credit Daisy Dean Doolin (Charles Elmer Doolin's mother) with the original chili-and-cheese-over-Fritos combination in San Antonio in the 1930s, with a documented 1949 Dallas Dietetic Association conference serving as the earliest confirmed public record; New Mexicans credit Teresa Hernandez with a 1960s Santa Fe Woolworth's version, serving chili ladled directly into an opened Fritos bag. The earlier documented date favors the Texas claim, but this file treats the claim as contested rather than settled. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — the 1949 date is a specific documented event, but the earlier 1930s home-kitchen claim itself rests on family oral history] [SOURCE: [Texas Highways — All That and a Bag of Chips: The History of Frito Pie](https://texashighways.com/food-drink/all-that-and-a-bag-of-chips-the-history-of-frito-pie/)]
- Serving format: **Two coexisting, non-hierarchical serving formats per §4.6**: (1) a casserole-dish version (Fritos layered in a baking dish, covered in chili, cheese, and onion, baked and served with a fork) — the original 1949 form; (2) the "walking Frito pie" — chili and cheese ladled directly into a single-serving Fritos bag, split open along one side, eaten with a plastic spoon while standing/walking — the dominant form at football games, fairs, and concession stands, and the more staging-relevant of the two for an on-the-go scene.
- Primary composition: Fritos corn chips, Texas chili (see that entry above — no beans), shredded cheddar cheese, and diced raw onion.
- Serving vessel: For the walking version, the opened single-serving Fritos bag itself is the vessel — a small, brightly printed foil/plastic bag, split open lengthwise to form a makeshift bowl.
- Real-world size (§4.5): A standard single-serving Fritos bag measures roughly 3.5 inches wide by 7–8 inches tall unopened (comparable in height to a smartphone, and taller than the Coca-Cola can's 4.83-inch height) — opened and laid flat/split for eating, it forms a shallow trough roughly 3.5×6 inches. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — inferred from standard snack-bag packaging conventions, not independently sourced as an exact dish measurement]
- Visual/plating characteristics: A jumble of golden-yellow, ridged, triangular Fritos chips, some still visibly crisp at the bag's edges and others visibly softened/soaked where the chili has been ladled directly on top, with the same deep brick-red beef-chunk chili described in the Texas Chili entry pooling into the chip crevices, topped with a visible scattering of shredded yellow cheese (some melted from the chili's residual heat, some still dry/shredded) and raw diced onion. A plastic spoon typically protrudes from the bag/dish.
- Common confusion: Not nachos (nachos use tortilla chips, not Fritos' distinctive corrugated corn-chip shape, and are typically baked/broiled with the cheese fully melted over the chips rather than chili ladled on top of a mostly-unheated chip base).
- Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH for composition and the two serving formats; MEDIUM for the exact origin-city claim, honestly disclosed as contested.
- Sources: [Texas Highways — All That and a Bag of Chips: The History of Frito Pie](https://texashighways.com/food-drink/all-that-and-a-bag-of-chips-the-history-of-frito-pie/); [Texas Standard — Frito pie, a mainstay under Friday night lights, is a Texas original](https://texasstandard.org/stories/frito-pie-history-texas/); [The Takeout — The Origins Of The Quirky Yet Delicious Frito Pie](https://www.thetakeout.com/1728998/who-invented-frito-pie/)
