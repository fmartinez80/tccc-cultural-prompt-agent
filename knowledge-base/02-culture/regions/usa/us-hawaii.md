## FILE ROLE & METHOD

This is the thirteenth and, per `us.md`'s FILE ROLE & METHOD table, final planned regional file under the 13-file US structure. `us.md`'s national index carries a single, unresolved line for this file ("13 | `us-hawaii.md` | unchanged") and, unlike every other regional file built so far, **`us.md` currently holds no dish-level pointers into Hawaii content at all** — no national-index entry for loco moco, plate lunch, Spam musubi, poke, or any other Hawaii dish exists to build on or supersede. This file is therefore built almost entirely from scratch rather than expanding an existing national-index depth, the same structural position `us-appalachia.md` and `us-florida.md` were in when they were added as wholly new files rather than splits of existing content. (Whether `us.md` should later gain a thin pointer into any of this file's content is flagged as a candidate for a future project-wide cross-reference audit — see CANDIDATE QUEUE.)

**Scope inherited from `us.md`**: lunch, dinner, and snacks only (breakfast excluded); inclusion by staging relevance, not raw consumption frequency. **Spam musubi and malasadas are both genuinely all-day/anytime foods in real life** (musubi is convenience-store and lunch-counter fare eaten from morning through late night; malasadas are a bakery product sold and eaten all day, not confined to breakfast) — both are staged here as all-day/snack items, the same all-day-availability treatment `us-arizona.md` gave its machaca breakfast burrito and `us-new-mexico.md` gave its biscochito and sopapilla, not as morning-plate breakfast scenes. **No beverage other than Coca-Cola is cataloged as a dish subject, per `us.md`'s inherited scope** — POG (passionfruit-orange-guava) juice and other Hawaii-specific beverages are explicitly out of scope as dish subjects. **Shave ice is treated as in-scope**: it is a solid, plated/cupped food/dessert item (shaved ice plus syrup, with solid mix-ins), not a beverage, and clears scope the same way any other snack dish does.

**Critical framing requirement, carried through every section of this file: three distinct, non-conflated layers.**

1. **"Local" Hawaii food** — the real, everyday, dated, causally-explained plantation-era immigrant-fusion cuisine (loco moco, plate lunch, Spam musubi, saimin, chicken katsu plate, manapua, andagi, malasadas, li hing mui) that emerged from Japanese, Filipino, Chinese, Portuguese, and Korean immigrant labor communities on Hawaii's sugar and pineapple plantations. This file treats "local food" the same way `us-west-coast.md` treats the California roll and this project treats spaghetti and meatballs: **a genuine, independently-evolved fusion cuisine in its own right, not a lower-fidelity version of any single parent cuisine.**
2. **Native Hawaiian (Kanaka Maoli) food traditions** — poi, kalua pig, imu earth-oven cooking, laulau, and traditional poke — which predate Western contact and plantation-era immigration entirely, and which this file treats with the Indigenous-heritage sourcing discipline established by `us-appalachia.md`, `us-new-mexico.md`, and `us-arizona.md`: every claim attributing a food tradition to Kanaka Maoli origin is tagged with its actual evidentiary tier (tribally/Native-Hawaiian-authored and academic sourcing vs. journalism/enthusiast/luau-industry-tier sourcing), not smoothed over. **This file found meaningfully better sourcing for several Native Hawaiian claims than `us-arizona.md` or `us-new-mexico.md` found for their own Indigenous content** — see TRUSTED CONTENT and the individual dish entries below for exactly where that higher tier was and wasn't reached.
3. **Tourist/luau-industry food and imagery** — real and commercially significant, but explicitly **not** the default or only register for a generic Hawaii scene, per this project's established caricature-avoidance principle. This file documents the commercial luau as one real, legitimate, clearly-labeled-as-commercial register among several — never as equivalent to, or a stand-in for, everyday local life or living Native Hawaiian tradition. **A generic, region-unspecified "Hawaii" scene should default to the everyday local-food register** (a plate-lunch counter, a home kitchen, an okazuya-style Japanese deli counter) — **not a luau.**

**Poke gets the sharpest version of layer 1-vs-2 treatment in this file**: traditional Native Hawaiian poke (raw reef fish, sea salt, limu, kukui-nut inamona) and the modern mainland "poke bowl" fast-casual phenomenon (ahi-tuna-and-soy-sauce, customizable-bowl-format, dated to a specific 2012–2016 mainland restaurant boom) are two real, related, but genuinely distinct things — disclosed as separate dish entries per `country-file-schema.md` §4.6, not flattened into one.

**Swap test run on internal Hawaii geography, tested rather than assumed.** This file tested whether a real, staging-relevant Oʻahu-vs-neighbor-island or urban-Honolulu-vs-rural distinction exists that would make swapping one sub-region's norms into another look visibly wrong. **Finding: real, but it resolves as a zone/callout-level distinction within this one file, not a further split** — see ZONE CHARACTERIZATION below for the evidence and reasoning. Hawaii's food-culture and dish-grammar core (the plate-lunch format, the same plantation-immigration causal story, the same core dish list) is shared statewide; what varies between Oʻahu and the neighbor islands is density, cosmopolitanism, and commercial-vs-family-run register — the same kind of gradient `us-arizona.md` documented for Tucson vs. Phoenix, not a different cuisine or a different dish grammar the way New Mexico's split from Arizona was. This structural call is logged in `DECISIONS.md` for review; per `country-file-schema.md` §7, structural/naming decisions of this kind are logged transparently so a human reviewer can revisit them.

**Typical use note** (carried forward from prior regional files' own calibration): expect the realistic use case to skew toward general, archetypal scenes — a plate-lunch counter, a home kitchen making Spam musubi, a shave ice stand, a family-style Native Hawaiian meal — more than hyper-local block-level staging.

## ZONE CHARACTERIZATION

Hawaii clears the "would swapping this region's norms into another US region look visibly wrong" test on grounds distinct from every other regional file in this project: it is the only US state built on a Polynesian Indigenous foundation subsequently layered with a plantation-era Asian/Pacific immigration wave, rather than a Spanish-colonial, Anglo-settler, or Atlantic-immigration history.

**A genuinely different, and even more heavily layered, version of the Pacific-Rim-immigration-wave phenomenon `us-west-coast.md` documents for California — not a copy of that state's story.** `us-west-coast.md` documents Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese immigration to California as several historically distinct waves arriving over more than a century, each settling into an existing, already-established American urban economy. Hawaii's version is a different mechanism entirely: a compressed, plantation-labor-contract-driven immigration into a then-independent, then-annexed territory, with each group arriving on a specific, documented schedule to work the same sugar (and later pineapple) plantations side by side:

- **Chinese** laborers first arrived in 1852 (some sourcing cites the first recruits arriving that year, with roughly 26,000 working the plantations by 1887); Chinese workers were the first to complete their contracts and leave plantation labor, going on to found some of Hawaii's earliest Asian-immigrant-owned businesses and banks. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [HAWAIʻI Magazine — Hawaiʻi's Rainbow of Cultures and How They Got to the Islands](https://www.hawaiimagazine.com/hawaiis-rainbow-of-cultures-and-how-they-got-to-the-islands/); [University of Hawaiʻi–West Oʻahu — History of Labor in Hawaiʻi](https://www.hawaii.edu/uhwo/clear/home/HawaiiLaborHistory.html)]
- **Portuguese** contract workers, recruited specifically from the Azores and Madeira, arrived predominantly between 1878 and 1887 (roughly 17,500 total) — later moving disproportionately into skilled/overseer plantation roles and into Hawaii's ranching (paniolo) economy, a distinct occupational trajectory from the other groups. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [HAWAIʻI Magazine, as above](https://www.hawaiimagazine.com/hawaiis-rainbow-of-cultures-and-how-they-got-to-the-islands/)]
- **Japanese** laborers arrived in the largest single-group numbers: roughly 180,000 between 1886 and 1925, reaching 73% of the entire plantation workforce by 1902 — the demographic anchor of the plantation-era labor force and, not coincidentally, the single largest lineage-contributor to today's local-food dish list (chicken katsu, saimin's dashi/noodle base, Spam musubi's onigiri ancestry, okazuya delis themselves). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [HAWAIʻI Magazine, as above](https://www.hawaiimagazine.com/hawaiis-rainbow-of-cultures-and-how-they-got-to-the-islands/)]
- **Korean** laborers arrived starting in 1903 (the first large group aboard the RMS Gaelic), with roughly 65 ship arrivals and about 7,843 total passengers landing in Honolulu over the following two and a half years — a comparatively short, concentrated immigration window relative to the multi-decade Japanese and Filipino waves. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [HAWAIʻI Magazine, as above](https://www.hawaiimagazine.com/hawaiis-rainbow-of-cultures-and-how-they-got-to-the-islands/)]
- **Filipino** laborers arrived starting in 1906 and became, by the plantation industry's early-20th-century peak, more than half of the entire sugar-plantation workforce — the last major wave and, by sheer numbers at peak, the largest. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [HAWAIʻI Magazine, as above](https://www.hawaiimagazine.com/hawaiis-rainbow-of-cultures-and-how-they-got-to-the-islands/)]
- Smaller but real contributing waves came from Norway (1880), Germany (1881), Puerto Rico (1900), and Spain (1907). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — a single detailed source, internally consistent with the more heavily corroborated Chinese/Japanese/Filipino/Portuguese/Korean dates above] [SOURCE: [Images of Old Hawaiʻi — Plantation Camps](https://imagesofoldhawaii.com/plantation-camps/)]

**The direct causal mechanism from this immigration timeline to today's food is documented, not asserted.** Workers from these different ethnic groups were housed in separate ethnicity-segregated plantation camps but worked the same fields and, critically, shared lunches — the historically and academically well-attested origin of both the "mixed plate" concept and, per one University of Hawaiʻi professor's account, the plate lunch's format tracing partly to the Japanese bento box tradition specifically. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Wheretraveler — Plate Lunches: A Scoop of Hawaiian History](https://www.wheretraveler.com/oahu/eat/plate-lunches-scoop-hawaiian-history); [Honolulu Civil Beat — Friend or Foam: Hawaii's Plate Lunch History](https://www.civilbeat.org/2011/10/13267-friend-or-foam-hawaiis-plate-lunch-history/)] This is the single clearest, most checkable causal chain in this file: named immigration waves, on a documented timeline, sharing physical lunch space, producing a genuinely new, dated, fusion cuisine — the same "authentic fusion, not imperfect imitation" status this project already extends to the California roll and to spaghetti and meatballs, extended here to the entire local-food dish list as a class, not just one dish.

**Native Hawaiian (Kanaka Maoli) foodways predate every wave above by centuries and are not a product of, or subordinate to, the plantation-immigration story.** Kalo (taro) — the basis of poi — occupies a specific, sourced place in Hawaiian cosmology: per the Kumulipo creation narrative, kalo grew from the body of Hāloa, the stillborn first son of Wākea (sky father) and Papa (earth mother), making it (in the words of multiple sources describing this tradition) the "elder sibling" of the Hawaiian people. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the existence and content of this creation narrative, cited consistently across sources including a peer-reviewed academic journal article — see TRUSTED CONTENT below for the full sourcing-tier breakdown] [SOURCE: [Slow Food Foundation — Ark of Taste: Kalo Poi](https://www.fondazioneslowfood.com/en/ark-of-taste-slow-food/poi-kalo/); [Canoe Plants of Ancient Hawaiʻi — Kalo](https://www.canoeplants.com/kalo.html)] Kalua (earth-oven) cooking, laulau, and traditional poke likewise predate Western contact (1778) and the plantation era (from 1852 onward) by a wide margin. **This file treats Native Hawaiian foodways as a foundational, still-living tradition layered underneath the plantation-immigration story, not a historical prelude to it or a subset of "local food."**

**Internal variation, tested rather than assumed: a real Oʻahu/neighbor-island gradient, but not a structural cuisine split.** Oʻahu (Honolulu specifically) is documented as a genuine culinary center with a dense, cosmopolitan, trend-forward restaurant scene spanning everything from global fine dining to food trucks, concentrated in neighborhoods like Kakaʻako, Kaimukī, and Chinatown. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — consistent across several tourism/lifestyle-journalism sources, not an academic culinary-geography study] [SOURCE: [Hawaiʻi Accommodations Blog — Guide to Local Hawaii Food](https://www.mauiaccommodations.com/blog/guide-to-local-hawaii-food/)] The neighbor islands (Kauaʻi, Molokaʻi, Lānaʻi, and Hawaiʻi Island specifically) are documented as leaning more toward home/family-prepared food and smaller, longer-running family businesses, with a comparatively less dense restaurant-trend scene. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — same source tier as above] **This is a difference of density, commercial sophistication, and family-vs.-restaurant register — not a different dish grammar.** The same core local-food dish list (plate lunch, loco moco, Spam musubi, saimin) and the same plantation-immigration causal story apply statewide; no source located in this pass documented a neighbor-island-specific dish, ingredient, or construction method absent from Oʻahu, or vice versa, at anything like the depth of Arizona/New Mexico's chile-vs-tortilla or Tucson/Phoenix distinctions. **Decision: one file, no split, with this gradient carried as an explicit callout** — the same "emphasis, not structural split" treatment `us-south.md` gives Lowcountry and `us-arizona.md` gives its own Tucson/Phoenix gradient. Revisit only if a future, more deeply sourced pass finds a genuine neighbor-island-specific dish-grammar difference this pass didn't locate.

**A second, separate register — the commercial luau industry — layers on top of both the local-food and Native Hawaiian strata, and this file treats it as a distinct third layer rather than folding it into either.** Commercial luau accelerated sharply with Hawaii's mid-20th-century tourism boom, transforming what had been the traditional ʻahaʻāina (feast) into standardized buffet dining paired with a staged entertainment show; the Big Band and Elvis-era decades specifically introduced now-familiar tourist-luau signifiers — emcees, "hapa haole" music, cellophane skirts, and coconut bras — designed for visitor entertainment rather than to preserve the underlying ceremonial practice. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — consistent across multiple journalism and academic-adjacent sources, though this file did not locate a single primary academic source covering the luau industry's full commercial history end to end] [SOURCE: [Anton Post — The Fake Lūʻau Industry: How Hawaiʻi Sells Fraud, Not Ritual](https://antonpost.town.news/g/honolulu-hi/n/362147/fake-luau-industry-how-hawaii-sells-fraud-not-ritual); [Hawaiʻi Public Radio — This authentic Hawaiian lūʻau wants to reclaim space in the tourism industry](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/local-news/2024-02-26/this-authentic-hawaiian-luau-wants-to-reclaim-space-in-the-tourism-industry)] Scholarly and cultural criticism of the commercial luau specifically flags authenticity, commodification, and — in the case of hula performed at commercial luaus — a sanitized, de-sacralized presentation stripped of the religious/ceremonial content present in hula kahiko (traditional hula), alongside concern about the hypersexualized packaging of Native Hawaiian performers for a visitor audience. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — consistent across multiple sources, at journalism/cultural-commentary tier rather than a single authoritative academic study] A real, current, Native-Hawaiian-led counter-movement exists specifically to reclaim luau as a vehicle for genuine cultural education and pride rather than pure tourist spectacle — this file does not treat the commercial-luau critique as the final or only word on the practice, only as a real, sourced tension worth disclosing. [SOURCE: [Hawaiʻi Public Radio, as above](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/local-news/2024-02-26/this-authentic-hawaiian-luau-wants-to-reclaim-space-in-the-tourism-industry)]

**What this zone characterization is not claiming.** Hawaii is not internally uniform, and its food culture is not reducible to any single one of the three layers above. **Caricature-avoidance note (editorial judgment, not a sourced claim):** the single most important caricature risk this file identifies — more acute here than in any prior regional file, given how globally recognizable the luau/tiki visual vocabulary is — is defaulting every generic "Hawaii" scene to a luau, tiki torch, or coconut-bra register. That register is real and should be available when a brief specifically calls for it (clearly labeled as the commercial-tourism register it is), but the correct default for an unspecified everyday Hawaii scene is the plate-lunch counter, the okazuya deli, or a home kitchen — registers this file's own research found are actually how most residents eat most days. A second, related risk: treating Native Hawaiian foodways (poi, kalua pig, laulau, traditional poke) as interchangeable with either the local-food plantation-fusion cuisine or the commercial luau register — they are a third, distinct, older tradition, and this file's dish entries and sourcing-tier disclosures below are built specifically to keep that distinction visible rather than letting it collapse.

## TRUSTED CONTENT

- **The plate-lunch format itself — two scoops of rice, one scoop of macaroni salad, plus a hot protein — is a genuinely form-defining, non-negotiable structural convention, not an optional stylistic detail.** This is documented consistently and specifically across food-history sources as *the* defining format, not one option among several: "two scoops of rice and mac salad" is treated as shorthand for the entire local-food eating tradition. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Da Pidgin — Two Scoop Rice: The Language of the Hawaiian Plate Lunch](https://dapidgin.com/learn/two-scoop-rice); [Tasting Table — The Origin Of Hawaii's Most Popular Meal](https://www.tastingtable.com/943945/the-origin-of-hawaiis-most-popular-meal/)] See the dedicated Plate lunch entry below for full construction detail — this format underlies several other entries in this catalog (chicken katsu plate, garlic shrimp plate) rather than being restated in each.
- **"Local food" and "Hawaiian food" are two distinct, non-interchangeable terms in actual Hawaii usage, and this file follows that distinction rather than using "Hawaiian food" loosely to mean anything eaten in Hawaii.** "Local food" specifically denotes the plantation-era immigrant-fusion cuisine (layer 1 above); "Hawaiian food" specifically denotes Native Hawaiian/Kanaka Maoli traditional dishes (layer 2 above) — sources explicitly warn against conflating the two. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — consistent across multiple Hawaii-food-culture-focused sources, at enthusiast/specialist-blog tier rather than academic] [SOURCE: [Ono Hawaiian Recipes — Hawaiian Food vs Local Food: The Real Difference](https://onohawaiianrecipes.com/hawai%CA%BBi/culture-stories/hawaiian-food-vs-local-food/); [Onolicious Hawaiʻi — Hawaiian Food and Local Food](https://onolicioushawaii.com/hawaiian-food/)] This file uses "local food" and "Native Hawaiian food" as its own two labels for exactly this reason, deliberately avoiding "Hawaiian food" as a loose catch-all.
- **Indigenous-heritage sourcing discipline, following this project's own established precedent** (`us-appalachia.md`'s ramps/leather-britches entries; `us-new-mexico.md`'s Pueblo-bread entry; `us-arizona.md`'s Navajo/Hopi/Tohono O'odham entries): where a claim attributes a food tradition to Kanaka Maoli origin specifically, this file states the claim's evidentiary tier honestly rather than smoothing it over. **This file reached a genuinely mixed but, in places, meaningfully stronger tier than the prior two regional files' Indigenous content**: a Slow Food International Ark of Taste catalog entry (an NGO-institutional heritage-food register, not a random blog) and a peer-reviewed academic journal article (*Food, Culture & Society*, a Taylor & Francis-published journal, on Kānaka Maoli poi production) both directly corroborate poi's cultural centrality claim — a stronger source pairing than any single Navajo/Hopi claim in `us-arizona.md` reached. Kamehameha Schools (a Native-Hawaiian-serving educational trust) and the Office of Hawaiian Affairs (Hawaii's state agency for Native Hawaiian affairs) both publish direct, current-day programming material on kalo cultivation and poi-pounding — an institutional-Native-Hawaiian tier distinct from, and stronger than, an outside journalist's or blogger's retelling. By contrast, this file's kalua/imu and laulau entries rest on a **weaker** tier: a Bishop Museum blog post (a legitimate Hawaiian cultural-heritage institution, though this specific piece is a blog-format post rather than a peer-reviewed or curatorial primary source) plus otherwise enthusiast/recipe-blog-tier sourcing — flagged individually in those entries and in the GAP LOG, not inflated to match poi's stronger tier.
- **Confidence tags for Native Hawaiian content in this file therefore vary entry-by-entry and should not be read as uniform** — poi's cultural/cosmological claims are tagged HIGH on the strength of the academic and Native-Hawaiian-institutional sourcing above; kalua/imu's and laulau's general construction/technique claims are tagged MEDIUM-HIGH on journalism/enthusiast-tier sourcing, honestly disclosed as such in each entry.

## VISUAL & PLATING NORMS

- **Local-food plating reads as informal, mounded, and component-separated rather than sauced-together or individually plattered** — a plate lunch's rice, macaroni salad, and protein sit as three visually distinct mounds/portions on one plate or clamshell tray, not blended or layered; this "separate mounds on one plate" convention is the single most checkable visual signature of the local-food register and should govern loco moco, chicken katsu plate, and garlic shrimp plate staging alike.
- **The rice scoop's dome shape is a real, checkable, specific detail** — plate-lunch rice is portioned with an ice-cream scoop, producing a visible hemispherical dome with a slightly compressed, faintly ridged surface (from the scoop's release mechanism) rather than a flat-spread mound or a molded rice-cooker block. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Two Red Bowls — Hawaii Plate Lunch](https://tworedbowls.com/2015/05/27/hawaii-plate-lunch/)]
- **Native Hawaiian dishes read as earthier, more muted, and less individually plated than the local-food register** — poi's uniform lavender-gray, laulau's dark, moist, leaf-wrapped bundle, and kalua pig's shredded, smoky, unsauced pile share a visual palette of muted purples, browns, and greens rather than the local-food palette's bright macaroni-salad white, glossy brown gravy, and vivid katsu-sauce browns.
- **Shave ice's single most important, checkable visual/textural distinction from a mainland snow cone**: shave ice is made from a solid ice block shaved into extremely fine, snow-like flakes, producing a light, fluffy, almost powder-soft texture that visibly absorbs syrup into the ice itself rather than the syrup pooling on top; a snow cone is made from coarser crushed/chipped ice with a granular, crunchy texture that syrup does not soak into, so syrup instead pools at the bottom of the cup and stains only the ice's outer surface. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Food Republic — The Texture Difference Between Shaved Ice And Snow Cones](https://www.foodrepublic.com/1564319/difference-between-shaved-ice-snow-cone/); [KitchenAid — Shaved ice vs. snow cone: what's the difference?](https://www.kitchenaid.com/countertop-appliances/pinch-of-help/shaved-ice-vs-snow-cone)] A correctly-staged Hawaiian shave ice should show a smooth, rounded, almost cloud-like mounded surface with saturated, evenly-distributed syrup color throughout the visible ice — not the icy, semi-translucent, individually-visible ice chips of a snow cone.
- **Texture/finish specifically**: Spam musubi's Spam slice should show a glossy, caramelized, deep reddish-brown seared surface (from pan-frying, often with a shoyu-sugar glaze) rather than the pale pink, matte surface of unfried canned Spam — this sear is a checkable, non-optional detail. A malasada's fried surface should be deep golden-brown and slightly craggy/uneven (not a smooth, machine-uniform donut surface), generously and unevenly coated in granulated sugar that clings in visible clumps rather than an even dusting. A chicken katsu cutlet's panko breading should show a distinctly coarse, craggy, almost shattered-glass texture (large irregular panko crumb fragments visible) — a checkably different, coarser breading texture than a smooth-breaded American fried-chicken cutlet.
- **Doneness convention**: kalua pig should never show any pink or a firm, sliceable texture — it is long, slow-cooked until fully fall-apart and shreddable, uniformly moist gray-brown throughout with no distinct muscle-fiber structure remaining intact. Loco moco's hamburger patty is typically cooked to a fully-done, no-pink-visible finish consistent with `us.md`'s general US burger-doneness norm, topped with brown gravy that should look glossy and thick enough to cling rather than pool thin and watery, and a fried egg whose yolk should be runny/glossy (not hard-cooked) per near-universal loco moco convention.

## ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES — zone deltas from `us.md`'s national baseline

- **Plantation-camp-era architecture and layout is the direct causal explanation for the local-food demographic/immigration story documented in ZONE CHARACTERIZATION above, and is a real, checkable, still-partially-surviving built environment.** Plantation labor camps housed workers in ethnicity-segregated sections (a real, if uncomfortable, historical fact worth stating plainly rather than glossing over), with documented specific housing dimensions — for example, camp houses for Korean workers measuring roughly 12 by 70 feet, and double cottages roughly 20 by 24 feet for Japanese workers — simple, single-story wood-frame worker housing rather than any grander vernacular. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — a single detailed academic-adjacent source with internally consistent detail] [SOURCE: [Hakalau Plantation — Camps](https://www.hakalauhome.com/camps.html)] Some plantation communities retain rows of simple plantation-era wood-frame worker houses, along with mill buildings, camp churches, and schools, as a genuine, checkable built-environment register distinct from anything in a mainland US regional file. ### Dish: Plate lunch (structural/format entry — cross-cutting, referenced by multiple entries below, parallel treatment to `us-arizona.md`'s Sonoran-flour-tortilla entry and `us-new-mexico.md`'s Hatch-chile entry)

- Category: Everyday — the single most foundational, identity-defining format in this file's entire catalog.
- Cuisine lineage: Local/plantation-fusion — not attributable to any one immigrant group; the *format itself* (protein plus two scoops rice plus macaroni salad) is the fusion, independent of which specific protein fills it.
- History: Traces to 1880s plantation-era lunch culture, when workers from Japan, China, Korea, the Philippines, and Portugal shared meals in the fields; the format is documented as evolving partly from the Japanese bento box tradition specifically, per University of Hawaiʻi ethnic-studies scholarship. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Wheretraveler — Plate Lunches: A Scoop of Hawaiian History](https://www.wheretraveler.com/oahu/eat/plate-lunches-scoop-hawaiian-history)] The metal plantation lunch tin ("kaukau tin") was the original vessel; the disposable foam clamshell container became the modern standard as the format moved from the fields into roadside stands and lunch wagons through the 1930s and the postwar diner/drive-in boom. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Honolulu Civil Beat — Friend or Foam: Hawaii's Plate Lunch History](https://www.civilbeat.org/2011/10/13267-friend-or-foam-hawaiis-plate-lunch-history/)]
- Regional form variation: None within Hawaii — the two-scoop-rice/one-scoop-macaroni-salad/hot-protein grammar is statewide and shared across Oʻahu and the neighbor islands alike (see ZONE CHARACTERIZATION).
- Serving format: Plate/tray — never handheld; the entire point of the format is a composed, multi-component tray.
- Primary composition: Two scoops of steamed white rice, one scoop of macaroni salad (mayo-dressed, see below), plus one or more hot proteins (chicken katsu, kalua pig, teriyaki beef/chicken, garlic shrimp, and other entries in this catalog all commonly appear in this exact format) — a "mixed plate" specifically denotes two or more proteins on the same tray. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Hawaii Life — How the Mixed Plate Started](https://www.hawaiilife.com/blog/how-the-mixed-plate-started/)]
- Side dishes/condiments: Macaroni salad itself functions as the plate's defining, non-optional side — described consistently as mayo-heavy, its creaminess balancing the salty/smoky proteins; it is served pre-plated as its own distinct mound, never mixed into the rice or protein. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Tasting Table — The Origin Of Hawaii's Most Popular Meal](https://www.tastingtable.com/943945/the-origin-of-hawaiis-most-popular-meal/)]
- Serving vessel: A disposable foam hinged-lid clamshell container (a real, checkable industry-standard packaging item — a common commercial size is roughly 9 × 6 × 3in, though larger multi-compartment plate-lunch-specific containers are also common and this file did not confirm one single universal size) for takeout/counter service, or a plain plate for sit-down service. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM for the specific container-size figure, which is a general foodservice-packaging fact rather than a plate-lunch-specific measurement — flagged for review] [SOURCE: [WebstaurantStore — Dart 205HT1 foam hinged container listing](https://www.webstaurantstore.com/dart-205ht1-9-x-6-x-3-white-foam-take-out-container-with-perforated-hinged-lid-case/301205HT1.html)]
- Real-world size (§4.5): Each rice scoop is portioned with a standard ice-cream scoop, roughly ½ cup per scoop (comparable to a large snowball, distinctly smaller across than the Coca-Cola can's 2.6in diameter but taller/domed rather than flat) — two scoops together occupy roughly a third to a half of the tray's footprint. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Curtis J Cooks — Mastering Hawaiian Plate Lunch](https://curtisjcooks.com/guide-plate-lunch/)] Per the vessel fallback (`tableware-composition-reference.md` §2), the finished tray as a whole should read as generously, almost fully filled — plate lunches are documented and depicted as large, hearty portions, not a sparse, minimalist plating — with the two rice domes, the macaroni salad mound, and the protein each occupying a clearly separated, individually visible third of the tray rather than blending together.
- Visual/plating characteristics: See VISUAL & PLATING NORMS above for the "three separate mounds, never blended" convention and the rice-scoop dome shape. Macaroni salad should look pale, creamy-white, and glossy from the mayo dressing, with visible elbow-macaroni pieces rather than a smooth purée; it should never look sauced with vinaigrette or a non-mayo dressing, which would misrepresent the specifically mayo-forward Hawaii style.
- Common confusion: A mainland American "meat and two sides" diner plate (e.g., a Southern plate lunch) — ruled out specifically by the mayo-macaroni-salad-plus-two-scoops-rice combination, which has no equivalent convention in any other US regional file in this project.
- Confidence: HIGH for the format's history, structure, and statewide non-negotiable status; MEDIUM for the exact container-dimension figure.
- Sources: [Wheretraveler — Plate Lunches: A Scoop of Hawaiian History](https://www.wheretraveler.com/oahu/eat/plate-lunches-scoop-hawaiian-history); [Tasting Table — The Origin Of Hawaii's Most Popular Meal](https://www.tastingtable.com/943945/the-origin-of-hawaiis-most-popular-meal/); [Honolulu Civil Beat — Friend or Foam: Hawaii's Plate Lunch History](https://www.civilbeat.org/2011/10/13267-friend-or-foam-hawaiis-plate-lunch-history/); [Da Pidgin — Two Scoop Rice](https://dapidgin.com/learn/two-scoop-rice); [Hawaii Life — How the Mixed Plate Started](https://www.hawaiilife.com/blog/how-the-mixed-plate-started/); [Curtis J Cooks — Mastering Hawaiian Plate Lunch](https://curtisjcooks.com/guide-plate-lunch/)

[CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — a specific, detailed, consistently-repeated account across multiple sources, though resting on local oral history/newspaper retelling rather than a contemporaneous primary record, the same evidentiary tier this project applies to similarly-dated dish-origin stories elsewhere (the chimichanga, the Reuben)] [SOURCE: [National Geographic — The Surprising History of Hawai'i's Loco Moco](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/culture/article/loco-moco-hawaii-food-history); [Whats Cooking America — Loco Moco Recipe and History](https://whatscookingamerica.net/history/locomocohistory.htm)] **The name's origin is itself a specific, sourced anecdote**: "Loco" was the nickname of the first teenager to eat the dish (in this file's sourcing, glossed as meaning "crazy" in Portuguese/Hawaiian-pidgin usage), and "moco" was added simply because it rhymed. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — a specific, repeated account, single-tier sourcing] Hilo's Cafe 100 (opened by a World War II 100th Infantry Battalion veteran, cited here for historical background only, per the trademark-avoidance rule) helped popularize the dish further and is documented as having trademarked "Loco Moco" and "Home of the Loco Moco" as business marks. [CONFIDENCE: LOW-MEDIUM — this file's own synthesis from component-level sourcing, not an independently measured finished-dish figure]

The gravy should coat but not fully submerge the rice below the patty — enough rice should remain visible at the plate's edges that the dish doesn't read as a uniform brown mass; the patty's edges should be visible and distinct from the rice beneath it (not pressed flat into the rice), and the egg yolk, when unbroken, should sit glossy and slightly domed on top of the patty.

### Dish: Spam musubi

- Category: Everyday, genuine all-day/anytime snack — staged here as an all-day item per this file's inherited scope, not a breakfast plate.
- Cuisine lineage: Local/plantation-fusion, with a specific, dated, WWII-era-origin story built directly on the Japanese onigiri tradition.
- History: Spam arrived in Hawaii during World War II as a shelf-stable, protein-rich ration good for a Pacific theater with limited fresh-meat supply; locals adopted it into everyday cooking during and after the war. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Time — The WWII Origins of Spam in Asian American Cuisine](https://time.com/5593886/asian-american-spam-cuisine/)] Spam musubi itself — a slice of grilled/pan-fried Spam atop a block of rice, wrapped together with nori — is most often credited to Barbara Funamura, a Japanese-American woman in Hawaii, though the exact origin is genuinely disputed: survivors of mainland Japanese-American internment camps have separately claimed to have created a precursor (seasoned Spam slices over rice in a baking pan) during the war. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — a specific named-inventor claim that this file treats as contested rather than settled, following this project's standard practice for similarly disputed dish origins] [SOURCE: [Tasting Table — The WWII Roots Of Spam Musubi](https://www.tastingtable.com/1106582/the-wwii-roots-of-spam-musubi/)] The dish is widely understood to have developed directly from onigiri (compacted rice and nori, often with furikake or other toppings), most likely emerging in Hawaii sometime in the 1940s–50s. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Regional form variation: None identified as form-changing within Hawaii.
- Serving format: Handheld — a rectangular block construction, eaten out of hand, no plate required.
- Primary composition: A block of rice (often lightly vinegared or plain, sometimes seasoned with furikake), topped with a pan-fried slice of Spam (frequently glazed with a shoyu-sugar or teriyaki-style sauce), the whole assembly wrapped in a strip of nori.
- Side dishes/condiments: None required — a self-contained snack; sometimes sold alongside other onigiri-style items at a convenience store or okazuya counter.
- Serving vessel: None (handheld) or a simple wax-paper/plastic sleeve for convenience-store sale.
- Real-world size (§4.5): The rice block is commonly built to match the Spam slice's own footprint — roughly 3.5 × 2 inches, about ½ to ¾ inch thick — with a standard Spam slice (about ½ inch thick) placed on top, and a 4 × 8-inch nori sheet wrapped around the full assembly. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — consistent across multiple recipe-tier sources for the mold-based construction, though not an independently measured commercial-product study] [SOURCE: [Hungry Huy — How To Make Spam Musubi](https://www.hungryhuy.com/spam-musubi/)] The finished block's overall footprint (roughly 3.5 × 2in) is distinctly smaller and flatter than the Coca-Cola can's 2.6in diameter footprint when viewed from above, but the rice-plus-Spam stack height (roughly 1.5–2in once the Spam slice sits on the rice block) approaches or exceeds the can's diameter — a useful comparative anchor for a scene with the can present.
- Visual/plating characteristics: See VISUAL & PLATING NORMS above for the Spam's required caramelized sear. The nori wrap should sit smooth and taut around the rice block with a clean, straight-edged rectangular silhouette (not loosely wrapped or bunched) — the rice itself should be barely visible at the block's top and bottom edges where the nori doesn't fully cover it, with the glazed Spam slice's glossy, deep-brown top surface as the dish's single most visually dominant element.
- Common confusion: A plain onigiri (Japanese rice ball) — ruled out by the rectangular, layered-block silhouette (never a triangular or round onigiri shape) and the visible Spam slice on top, which has no onigiri equivalent.
- Confidence: HIGH for the WWII-era Spam-adoption context and general construction; MEDIUM for the specific named-inventor claim, honestly disclosed as contested.
- Sources: [Time — The WWII Origins of Spam in Asian American Cuisine](https://time.com/5593886/asian-american-spam-cuisine/); [Tasting Table — The WWII Roots Of Spam Musubi](https://www.tastingtable.com/1106582/the-wwii-roots-of-spam-musubi/); [Wikipedia: Spam musubi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spam_musubi); [Hungry Huy — How To Make Spam Musubi](https://www.hungryhuy.com/spam-musubi/)

### Dish: Saimin

- Category: Everyday — a standing menu item at dedicated saimin shops and plate-lunch counters alike.
- Cuisine lineage: Local/plantation-fusion, and genuinely Hawaii-specific, distinct from ramen, pho, or any other noodle soup already documented elsewhere in this project.
- History: Invented during Hawaii's plantation era in the late 1800s, emerging from the same shared-lunch "mixed plate" culture documented in ZONE CHARACTERIZATION, drawing specifically on Chinese, Japanese, Okinawan, Portuguese, and Filipino culinary influence. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Onolicious Hawaiʻi — Saimin, Hawaii's Noodle Soup](https://onolicioushawaii.com/saimin/); [SFGate — How a humble bowl of noodle soup became Hawaii's favorite comfort food](https://www.sfgate.com/hawaii/article/saimin-hawaii-noodle-soup-history-18693937.php)] The name derives from Cantonese Chinese ("thin wheat noodles"). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]

[CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: Saimin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saimin)] Saimin is also documented as its own distinct multi-immigrant-cuisine fusion (not a Japanese import alone the way ramen is), and is eaten with both chopsticks and a spoon, the broth drunk directly from the bowl rather than left behind. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Regional form variation: None identified as form-changing within Hawaii.
- Serving format: Bowl.
- Primary composition: Thin wheat noodles in a clear dashi-based broth, topped with sliced kamaboko (fish cake), green onion, and often a hard-boiled or soft egg.
- Side dishes/condiments: None required — a standalone bowl dish; sometimes ordered alongside a plate-lunch item at a shared counter.
- Serving vessel: A medium-to-large individual soup bowl — per `tableware-composition-reference.md` §2's fallback, filled to within 1–2cm of the rim, generous enough to be a full meal on its own rather than a starter-sized portion.
- Real-world size (§4.5): No independently sourced, dish-specific bowl-diameter figure was found this pass; per the vessel fallback, a main-course saimin bowl should read as noticeably larger and deeper than a Western side-soup bowl, closer to or exceeding the Coca-Cola can's 4.83in height when measured bowl-rim-to-base for a deep, generous serving bowl. [CONFIDENCE: LOW-MEDIUM — this file's own inference, flagged for review]
- Visual/plating characteristics: The broth should read pale, golden-clear, and notably thinner/lighter in both color and viscosity than a ramen broth (which is typically cloudier, richer, and more opaque, whether shoyu, miso, or tonkotsu style) — a genuinely checkable, side-by-side distinguishing detail if both dishes ever needed to be compared in the same scene. Kamaboko slices should show their characteristic pink-and-white or pink-rimmed swirl pattern, laid flat and visible rather than submerged.
- Common confusion: Ramen (already documented in this project's national/other regional content) — ruled out by saimin's clearer, thinner broth and chewier, higher-ash-content noodle, per the sourced distinction above. Not pho (a Vietnamese beef-and-star-anise-forward broth with garnishes served on the side) — saimin's garnishes are pre-added to the bowl, not served separately.
- Confidence: HIGH for the historical/plantation-fusion origin and the ramen-distinction claim; LOW-MEDIUM for the specific bowl-size synthesis.

- Category: Everyday to sacred/ceremonial — a genuine both/and: poi is eaten as an everyday staple starch and simultaneously carries deep cosmological significance, not a special-occasion-only food.
- Cuisine lineage: Native Hawaiian (Kanaka Maoli) — predates Western contact (1778) and the plantation-immigration era entirely; not a product of, or descended from, the local/plantation-fusion cuisine documented elsewhere in this catalog.
- **Sourcing-tier disclosure (per this file's Indigenous-heritage sourcing discipline)**: this entry's cultural/cosmological claims are corroborated by a genuinely stronger source pairing than this project's prior Indigenous-heritage entries typically reached — a peer-reviewed academic journal article (*Food, Culture & Society*, Taylor & Francis) specifically on Kānaka Maoli poi production, and a Slow Food International Ark of Taste catalog entry (an NGO-institutional heritage-food register), corroborated further by direct programming materials from Kamehameha Schools (a Native-Hawaiian-serving educational trust) and the Office of Hawaiian Affairs (Hawaii's state agency for Native Hawaiian affairs) on present-day kalo cultivation and poi-pounding instruction. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the cultural/cosmological claims below, on the strength of this source combination] [SOURCE: [Tandfonline/Food, Culture & Society — Cultural Traditions and Food: Kānaka Maoli and the Production of Poi in the Heʻeʻia Wetland](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15528014.2016.1208340); [Slow Food Foundation — Ark of Taste: Kalo Poi](https://www.fondazioneslowfood.com/en/ark-of-taste-slow-food/poi-kalo/); [Kamehameha Schools — Ka Wai Ola: Papahana Kālai Papa Me Pōhaku Kuʻi ʻAi](https://kawaiola.news/mauliola/papahana-kalai-papa-me-pohaku-kui-ai/)]
- History/cultural significance: Per the Kumulipo (the Hawaiian creation chant), kalo (taro) grew from the body of Hāloa, the stillborn first son of Wākea (sky father) and Papa (earth mother) — making kalo, in the words of multiple sources describing this tradition, the literal ancestor and "elder sibling" of the Hawaiian people, not merely a crop. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH, per the sourcing-tier disclosure above] Poi is made by cooking and mashing the whole kalo corm (tuber) with water; it is frequently described, including in academic literature, as the "soul food" of Hawaii. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] Contemporary academic scholarship frames kalo cultivation and poi production specifically as an active vehicle of Indigenous resilience and cultural resurgence for Kānaka Maoli today, not a static historical practice — the Office of Hawaiian Affairs' own grant-funded programming (teaching participants to make and use a papa kuʻi ʻai/kalo board and pōhaku kuʻi ʻai/pounder) has served nearly 1,000 people over roughly a decade, a real, checkable, present-day figure. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Regional form variation: None identified as form-changing — poi's core preparation is consistent statewide; consistency/thickness ("one-finger," "two-finger," "three-finger" poi, referring to how many fingers are needed to scoop a given thickness) is a real, sourced textural spectrum rather than a geographic split. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — commonly referenced across sources, though this file did not find the "finger" terminology in an academic source specifically, only enthusiast-tier retellings; flagged for corroboration]
- Serving format: Bowl, eaten with fingers (traditionally two or three fingers used to scoop, per the consistency-naming convention above) or a spoon in a more casual/modern context.
- Primary composition: Cooked, peeled, and pounded kalo corm, mixed with water to the desired consistency; no other ingredients in its traditional form (no salt, sugar, or flavoring added).
- Side dishes/condiments: Traditionally eaten alongside kalua pig, laulau, and other savory dishes as the starch component of a full Native Hawaiian meal — its intentionally bland, only-faintly-sweet flavor is described as a deliberate counterpoint to salty/savory proteins, not a bland-by-accident food.
- Serving vessel: A simple bowl — historically a hand-carved wooden calabash bowl for a traditional/ceremonial presentation; a plain modern bowl for an everyday meal.
- Real-world size (§4.5): No independently sourced, dish-specific dimension applies to a paste/porridge-consistency food; per the vessel fallback (`tableware-composition-reference.md` §2), a standard individual serving should fill a small-to-medium bowl (12–18cm) to within 1–2cm of the rim, with a thick, batter-like consistency that clings to a dipped finger or spoon and holds a soft peak rather than pooling flat like a thin liquid. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — this file's own inference from the "finger consistency" convention, not an independently measured figure]
- Visual/plating characteristics: A uniform, smooth, matte lavender-gray-to-purple-gray color (from the kalo corm itself, distinct from any other starch documented in this project) — poi should never look white, tan, or beige, which would misrepresent the ingredient entirely; the surface should look thick and slightly glossy-wet, without visible lumps or fibrous corm pieces remaining (properly pounded poi is smooth), and should hold a soft, slumping mound shape rather than spreading completely flat.
- Common confusion: A generic gray or purple mashed-root-vegetable dish (e.g., mashed purple sweet potato or ube-based dessert paste, documented elsewhere in other cultures' food traditions) — the checkable differentiator is poi's specific matte lavender-gray (not the more saturated violet-purple of ube) and its context (served as a savory starch alongside kalua pig/laulau, never as a standalone sweet dessert).
- Confidence: HIGH for cultural/cosmological significance and basic composition, on the strength of the academic/institutional sourcing tier reached; MEDIUM for the "finger consistency" naming convention and the specific vessel-fill/texture synthesis.

- Category: Everyday-to-ceremonial — kalua pig itself is served in everyday local-food contexts (plate lunches, home meals) as well as at ceremonial/celebratory gatherings and commercial luaus; the imu cooking method itself is the more occasion-specific, labor-intensive element.

- **Sourcing-tier disclosure**: this entry's construction/technique detail rests on a Bishop Museum blog post (a legitimate, real Hawaiian cultural-heritage institution — the Bishop Museum holds the largest collection of Native Hawaiian artifacts and documentation in the world — though this specific cited piece is a blog-format post rather than a peer-reviewed or curatorial primary publication) plus otherwise enthusiast/practitioner-tier sourcing (a primitive-skills specialist site, luau-industry venue pages). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: as above] The entire process — building the imu, heating the stones, and cooking the food — takes the better part of a day, making it inherently a communal, social, occasion-scale cooking method rather than an everyday-meal technique; most everyday kalua pig served in plate lunches and home kitchens today is made in a conventional oven or slow cooker with liquid smoke, not an actual imu. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]

- Serving format: Two genuinely distinct presentations — (1) a whole or half pig, imu-cooked and often ceremonially presented (carried out, sometimes still wrapped in leaves, before being portioned) at a luau/large gathering; (2) shredded, already-portioned kalua pork served as a plate-lunch or home-meal protein, with no whole-animal presentation at all. **Default when unspecified**: the everyday shredded-pork plate-lunch format, since it is by far the more common real-world context a Coca-Cola staging brief would plausibly depict; the ceremonial whole-pig presentation should be used only when a brief specifically calls for a luau/ceremonial scene.
- Primary composition: Pork (traditionally a whole pig), rubbed with Hawaiian sea salt, cooked in the imu (or, for the everyday version, oven/slow-cooker-braised with liquid smoke to approximate the imu's smoky flavor) until fully fall-apart tender, then shredded.
- Side dishes/condiments: Served plain or with a light sprinkle of additional Hawaiian sea salt; no heavy sauce.
- Serving vessel: For the everyday version, standard plate-lunch tray/clamshell (see Plate lunch entry); for the ceremonial/whole-pig version, no single vessel — presented on leaves or a large platform/tray before portioning.
- Real-world size (§4.5): No independently sourced, dish-specific dimension applies to shredded meat; per the vessel fallback, an everyday plate-lunch portion should fill roughly a third of the tray, in loose, visibly shredded (not chunked or sliced) strands. [CONFIDENCE: LOW-MEDIUM — this file's own inference]
- Visual/plating characteristics: See VISUAL & PLATING NORMS and doneness-convention notes above — kalua pig should read uniformly moist, gray-brown, and finely shredded throughout, with no distinct muscle-fiber structure remaining, and no red/pink coloring or heavy sauce coating (a checkable difference from a mainland BBQ pulled pork, which is typically darker, glossier, and sauce-coated).
- Common confusion: Mainland pulled pork/BBQ pork (documented elsewhere in this project) — ruled out by the absence of any BBQ sauce or heavy glaze on kalua pig, which is seasoned with salt alone, and by its lighter, less caramelized color.
- Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH for the imu's construction/process and kalua pig's basic composition; sourcing tier is honestly disclosed as museum-blog/enthusiast-practitioner level, not academic or a Native-Hawaiian-authored primary source — see GAP LOG.

### Dish: Laulau (Native Hawaiian — apply Indigenous-heritage sourcing discipline)

- Category: Everyday-to-special-occasion — sold as a standing plate-lunch-counter item as well as prepared for family/community gatherings.
- Cuisine lineage: Native Hawaiian (Kanaka Maoli) — a pre-contact dish and cooking method, not plantation-era-derived.
- **Sourcing-tier disclosure**: this entry rests entirely on recipe-blog/enthusiast-tier sourcing (Whats Cooking America, Onolicious Hawaiʻi, and similar sites) — this file did not locate an academic or Native-Hawaiian-institutional-tier source specifically for laulau's history or construction, a genuinely weaker tier than the poi entry above and comparable to `us-arizona.md`'s and `us-new-mexico.md`'s weaker-tier Navajo/Hopi/Pueblo-bread entries. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — enthusiast-tier sourcing only, consistently described across multiple such sources]
- History/technique: The word laulau literally means "leaf leaf" (lau = leaf), referring to its double-leaf wrapping construction. Traditionally, pork, salted butterfish (salted cod, despite the "butterfish" name), or chicken is layered inside taro (lūʻau) leaves, which are then bundled and tied inside a tougher outer wrapping of ti leaves, and slow-cooked in an imu. Today, most everyday laulau is steamed on a stovetop rather than imu-cooked, reserving the imu method for special occasions. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM] [SOURCE: [Whats Cooking America — Hawaiian Lau Lau Pork History and Recipe](https://whatscookingamerica.net/pork/hawaiian-laulaupork.htm); [Onolicious Hawaiʻi — Hawaiian Lau Lau](https://onolicioushawaii.com/lau-lau/)] Raw or undercooked taro leaves contain calcium oxalate crystals that cause a scratchy-throat sensation if not fully cooked — a real, checkable reason the leaves must be cooked thoroughly, not an optional step. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] Ti leaves themselves carry ceremonial/protective cultural significance in Hawaiian tradition beyond their practical role as a heat-resistant wrapper. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- Regional form variation: None identified as form-changing within Hawaii.
- Serving format: Plate/bundle — served as a single wrapped bundle, unwrapped at the table or by the diner.
- Primary composition: Chunks of fatty pork and/or salted butterfish (and, in some versions, chicken), wrapped in taro leaves, then bundled in ti leaves, and steamed (or imu-cooked for a ceremonial version).
- Side dishes/condiments: Commonly served with rice and poi as the starch components of the meal.
- Serving vessel: A plate, the ti-leaf bundle typically left tied and presented whole, to be opened by the diner.
- Real-world size (§4.5): No independently sourced, precise dimension was found this pass; qualitatively described as a fist-sized to slightly-larger-than-fist-sized bundle, roughly comparable in overall bulk to two tamales side by side (see `us-new-mexico.md`'s tamale entry for a size reference point), though this file did not find a specific inch measurement. [CONFIDENCE: LOW — flagged for review]
- Visual/plating characteristics: The outer ti-leaf wrapper should appear dark green, glossy, and slightly wilted/softened from steaming, tied closed (traditionally with a strip of the leaf itself or twine) rather than left open; once unwrapped, the inner taro-leaf layer should show a darker, more olive-green, fully-cooked (never bright, raw-looking green) color, with the pork/fish filling visible as darker chunks embedded within the cooked, collapsed leaf matrix — a moist, dark, leaf-dominant visual very different from any other wrapped dish in this project's catalog (distinct from a corn-husk tamale's pale masa or a banana-leaf-wrapped dish's smoother, less fibrous wrapper).

### Dish: Poke — traditional Native Hawaiian (apply Indigenous-heritage sourcing discipline; see next entry for the distinct mainland commercial poke bowl)

- Category: Everyday — a pre-contact, everyday Native Hawaiian dish, not a special-occasion food.
- Cuisine lineage: Native Hawaiian (Kanaka Maoli) — Hawaiians have eaten raw fish since long before Western contact; "poke" itself means "to slice" or "to cut crosswise into pieces" in Hawaiian, describing the preparation technique rather than naming a specific recipe. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Bishop Museum Blog — The History of Poke in Hawaiʻi](https://blog.bishopmuseum.org/history/the-history-of-poke-in-hawai%CA%BBi/)]

[CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH, honestly distinguished from poi's HIGH-tier sourcing]

- Regional form variation: None identified as form-changing within Hawaii for the traditional form.
- Serving format: Bowl or small dish — a side/starter portion, not a full composed meal the way a modern poke bowl is (see next entry).
- Primary composition: Cured reef fish — commonly heʻe (octopus) or aku (skipjack tuna) — cut into pieces, mixed with limu (seaweed; Hawaiians have documented names for more than eighty distinct types, with limu kohu the type most associated with poke specifically, still gathered today by independent fishermen from specific, often undisclosed coastal locations), ʻinamona (a condiment made from roasted, ground kukui nut/candlenut mixed with sea salt), and paʻakai (Hawaiian sea salt). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Vital Choice — Poke Facts & History](https://www.vitalchoice.com/articles/cooking-tips/poke-facts); [Wikipedia: Inamona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inamona)]
- Side dishes/condiments: None beyond the ʻinamona/limu/salt mixed directly into the dish itself — no soy sauce, sesame oil, or scallion, all of which belong to the modern mainland version's East-Asian-influenced flavor profile documented in the next entry, not the traditional form.
- Serving vessel: A small bowl or dish, per `tableware-composition-reference.md` §2's side-bowl fallback (12–15cm).
- Real-world size (§4.5): No independently sourced, dish-specific dimension applies to a loose cut-fish preparation; per the vessel fallback, a traditional side portion should fill a small 12–15cm bowl roughly half to two-thirds full, in irregular, hand-cut pieces roughly ½–¾ inch across rather than the more uniform, larger cubes typical of the modern mainland version (see next entry). [CONFIDENCE: LOW-MEDIUM — this file's own inference]

The overall dish should look drier, less glossy, and less sauced than the modern mainland poke bowl documented below.
- Common confusion: **The modern mainland poke bowl (this file, next entry)** — the single most important, sourced distinction in this catalog's poke coverage; see that entry for the full disclosure. Not a Japanese sashimi presentation (individually sliced, arranged fish with no seasoning mixed in) — poke's defining "cut and dressed together" preparation, named for that exact action, is the checkable differentiator.
- Confidence: HIGH for the traditional composition and pre-contact origin; MEDIUM-HIGH overall sourcing tier, honestly distinguished from poi's stronger academic-tier sourcing.

### Dish: Shave ice

- Category: Everyday snack/dessert — a solid, plated/cupped food item, not a beverage, and explicitly in scope per this file's assignment.
- Cuisine lineage: Local/plantation-fusion-adjacent — brought to Hawaii by the same Chinese plantation-era immigration wave credited with li hing mui and saimin's spread, though shave ice as a dessert format has since become one of the most broadly recognized "Hawaii food" items outside the state, occasionally over-genericized as interchangeable with a mainland snow cone (which this entry's checkable distinctions specifically rule out).
- Regional form variation: None identified as form-changing within Hawaii.
- Serving format: A cupped/bowled dessert, eaten with a spoon (or, for smaller cups, drunk/slurped at the melted-syrup stage) — not handheld the way most other snacks in this catalog are.
- Primary composition: A solid block of ice, shaved (not crushed) into extremely fine, snow-like flakes, mounded into a cup or bowl and doused with one or more brightly-colored flavored syrups.
- Side dishes/condiments: **A genuine, sourced, non-negotiable construction convention — the "unders"**: a small scoop of ice cream and/or roughly 2 tablespoons of sweetened azuki (adzuki) beans placed at the *bottom* of the cup before the ice is added, so the topping is discovered partway through eating rather than visible at the top. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: general Hawaii shave-ice-culture sourcing, consistent across multiple sources] Mochi balls, li hing mui powder (see that entry above), fresh fruit, and condensed milk drizzled over the top are all common additional toppings, applied at the top rather than buried underneath.
- Serving vessel: A conical paper or plastic cup, or a wider shallow bowl for a larger, more elaborate presentation — no single universal size was confirmed this pass.
- Real-world size (§4.5): No independently sourced, precise cup-volume figure was found this pass; per the vessel fallback, a standard single-serving cup should read as comparable in overall height to roughly half to two-thirds of the Coca-Cola can's 4.83in height when the ice is mounded above the cup's rim (a generously overfilled, domed presentation is the norm, not a flush-to-the-rim fill), with the cup's own diameter comparable to or slightly larger than the can's 2.6in diameter for a standard single-serving size. [CONFIDENCE: LOW-MEDIUM — this file's own qualitative inference, flagged for review]
- Visual/plating characteristics: See VISUAL & PLATING NORMS above for the full checkable ice-texture distinction from a mainland snow cone (fine, fluffy, syrup-absorbing shave ice vs. coarse, crunchy, syrup-resistant crushed ice). The finished mound should show smooth, rounded, almost snowdrift-like contours (not sharp, angular, individually-visible ice chip edges), with syrup colors that saturate evenly through the visible ice surface rather than pooling in isolated streaks or only at the very top. When a spoon cuts into the mound, the buried "unders" (azuki beans and/or ice cream) should appear as a visually distinct pocket of color/texture partway down, not visible at all from the surface.
- Common confusion: A mainland snow cone — ruled out specifically and checkably by the ice texture (fine/fluffy/syrup-absorbing vs. coarse/crunchy/syrup-resistant, per the sourced distinction above) and by the buried "unders" convention, which has no snow-cone equivalent. Not Italian/mainland "shaved ice"/Italian ice sold in some other US regions (a smoother, more sorbet-like frozen dessert, not a snow-textured ice pile) — ruled out by shave ice's distinctly granular-but-fine (not smooth/creamy) texture.
- Confidence: HIGH for the texture distinction from a snow cone; MEDIUM-HIGH for the "unders" convention; LOW-MEDIUM for exact serving-size figures.
- Sources: [Food Republic — The Texture Difference Between Shaved Ice And Snow Cones](https://www.foodrepublic.com/1564319/difference-between-shaved-ice-snow-cone/); [KitchenAid — Shaved ice vs. snow cone: what's the difference?](https://www.kitchenaid.com/countertop-appliances/pinch-of-help/shaved-ice-vs-snow-cone); [Wikipedia: Hawaiian shave ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_shave_ice); [HAWAIʻI Magazine — Hawaii Shave Ice: Our Guide to Favorite Toppings](https://www.hawaiimagazine.com/hawaii-shave-ice-our-guide-to-favorite-toppings/)

### Dish: Lomi lomi salmon (a luau-plate staple with a specific, non-precontact, trade-good origin — honestly disclosed rather than presented as ancient)

- Category: Special-occasion/luau-plate staple, also served at family gatherings.
- Cuisine lineage: **A genuinely important honest-disclosure case, parallel to this project's treatment of dishes whose popular framing implies an origin they don't actually have.** Despite its frequent presentation as a traditional/ancient Hawaiian dish, lomi lomi salmon is *not* pre-contact — salmon do not swim in Hawaii's warm waters at all. The dish dates specifically to the early-to-mid 1800s, when Russian traders and, following them, North American whalers brought barrels of salted salmon into Honolulu and Lahaina as a trade good; Hawaiians incorporated the imported salted fish into their own food traditions by rinsing off most of the salt, dicing it with ripe tomatoes and sweet onion, and "lomi-lomi-ing" (massaging/kneading) the mixture together — the same adaptive, ingredient-incorporating pattern this file documents for the rest of Hawaii's food history, applied here to a specific 19th-century trade good rather than a plantation-immigration-era ingredient. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: journalism-tier food-history sourcing, consistent across multiple sources describing the same salmon-as-trade-good origin] By the early 20th century it was a fixture at luaus and family gatherings, a status it retains today.
- Regional form variation: None identified as form-changing.
- Serving format: A chilled salad/relish, served as a cold side dish — never a hot entree.
- Primary composition: Diced salted salmon (rinsed of most of its curing salt), diced ripe tomato, diced sweet onion (Maui onion where available), combined and gently massaged together by hand.
- Side dishes/condiments: None — a standalone cold side.
- Serving vessel: A small side bowl or dish, per `tableware-composition-reference.md` §2 (12–15cm).
- Real-world size (§4.5): No independently sourced, dish-specific dimension applies to a diced salad/relish; per the vessel fallback, a standard side portion should fill a small 12–15cm bowl roughly half full. [CONFIDENCE: LOW-MEDIUM]
- Visual/plating characteristics: A chunky, colorful, glistening mixture of deep pink-orange salmon dice, bright red tomato dice, and pale translucent onion dice, visibly distinct pieces rather than a blended purée — served well-chilled, so the mixture should look glossy/wet rather than dry.
- Common confusion: A mainland tomato-and-onion salsa or pico de gallo — ruled out by the diced salted-salmon component, which has no equivalent in a Mexican-American salsa.
- Confidence: HIGH for the non-precontact/trade-good origin and general composition; LOW-MEDIUM for the specific size synthesis.
- Sources: [Taste of Home — Lomi Lomi Salmon Recipe](https://www.tasteofhome.com/recipes/lomi-lomi-salmon/); [Wikipedia: Lomi-lomi salmon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lomi-lomi_salmon); [196 Flavors — Lomi-Lomi Salmon](https://www.196flavors.com/lomi-lomi-salmon/)

### Dish: Haupia (coconut pudding)

- Category: Special-occasion/luau-plate dessert staple, also made for home celebrations.
- Cuisine lineage: Native Hawaiian in name and base technique (a coconut-milk pudding tradition found throughout Polynesia broadly, not exclusive to Hawaii), historically thickened with pia (Polynesian arrowroot starch) — which is the direct source of the dish's name — with modern versions substituting cornstarch. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: Haupia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haupia)]
- Regional form variation: None identified as form-changing; modern fusion applications (haupia layered into pie, cake, or ice cream) are ordinary contemporary variation, not a geographic split.
- Serving format: A firm, chilled, cut-square dessert — the standard luau-buffet-dessert presentation.
- Primary composition: Coconut milk, sugar, and a starch thickener (traditionally pia/arrowroot, commonly cornstarch today), set until firm and sliceable.
- Side dishes/condiments: None — served plain, sometimes alongside other desserts on a buffet dessert table.
- Serving vessel: A small dessert plate or directly from a cut sheet pan on a buffet table.
- Real-world size (§4.5): No independently sourced, precise dimension was found this pass; qualitatively, haupia is set in a pan and cut into small squares comparable in size to a standard fudge or brownie square (roughly 1.5–2 inches per side). [CONFIDENCE: LOW-MEDIUM — this file's own qualitative inference]
- Visual/plating characteristics: A uniform, opaque, bright white color (from the coconut milk), with a firm, jiggly-but-sliceable, custard-like texture and clean-cut square edges — should never look glossy/wet the way a loose pudding would, since haupia is specifically set firm enough to hold a cut square shape.
- Common confusion: A mainland vanilla or coconut-cream pudding served in a cup (a soft-set, spoonable dessert) — ruled out by haupia's firm, sliceable, cut-square presentation, which a spoonable cup pudding cannot replicate.
- Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH for composition and luau-dessert-staple status; LOW-MEDIUM for the specific size figure.
- Sources: [Wikipedia: Haupia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haupia); [Hawaii Travel Guide — Haupia Recipe](https://www.hawaii-guide.com/hawaii-eats/recipes/haupia); [This Hawaii Life — Haupia](https://thishawaiilife.com/haupia/)
