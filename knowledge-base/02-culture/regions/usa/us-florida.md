## ZONE CHARACTERIZATIONS

### North Florida

North Florida is genuinely distinct from South Florida on settlement history and climate, but genuinely continuous with the Deep South on both — which is itself the finding that justifies giving it its own zone inside a Florida file rather than either merging it into `us-south.md` or treating all of Florida as one uniform "tropical/Latin" identity. Its formative immigrant/settlement history predates and is unrelated to the 20th-century Cuban-Caribbean migration that defines South Florida's food identity: St. Augustine's Minorcan community arrived as indentured laborers in the 1760s–70s, and Tampa's Cuban/Spanish/Italian cigar-worker immigration (from 1886) is a 19th-century, cigar-industry-driven wave distinct in cause and community from Miami's post-1959 Cuban exile wave. North Florida is documented, repeatedly and explicitly, as culturally part of the Deep South, not just "Southern-adjacent" — Jacksonville, Tallahassee, and Pensacola are described as having "more in common with Savannah and Mobile than they do Miami or Ft. Lauderdale." [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: North Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Florida)] Yet North Florida is still Florida: it has a genuine Gulf/Atlantic coastal-seafood identity (oysters, grouper) and its own 19th-century Cuban-immigrant urban food tradition in Tampa with no equivalent in inland Georgia, Alabama, or Mississippi. Swapping an Alabama Gulf Coast BBQ default into a Tampa or Apalachicola scene, or swapping Miami's tropical-modernist register into a rural North Florida farmhouse scene, would both be real, checkable errors.

**Caricature-avoidance note**: don't default every North Florida scene to a plantation-South cliché — the sourced Deep South continuity here is about settlement pattern, cuisine, and vernacular small-town/rural culture, not a specific antebellum aesthetic; Gulf Coast beach-tourism culture and Tampa's urban immigrant-neighborhood register are equally authentic, non-plantation alternatives.

### South Florida / Floribbean

South Florida clears the same distinctness test on independently corroborated causal grounds, not just a different name for similar food. First, a distinct, datable immigration/settlement pattern: the 1959 Cuban Revolution forced an exodus of more than 250,000 Cubans to South Florida between 1959 and 1962, concentrating in what became Miami's Little Havana — a sudden, large-scale, politically-driven refugee migration distinct in cause and timing from North Florida's older, cigar-industry-driven Cuban immigration. South Florida additionally holds the largest Haitian-American population in the country — Florida is home to an estimated 500,000 Haitian Americans, nearly half the US total, concentrated largely in South Florida's Little Haiti — a second, independent immigrant-community thread with no equivalent in North Florida's documented food history. Second, a named, dated, chef-driven culinary movement with no North Florida equivalent: "Floribbean" cuisine emerged specifically in South Florida/the Keys in the 1980s, associated with the "Mango Gang" (Norman Van Aken, Allen Susser, Mark Militello, Douglas Rodriguez), who built restaurants and reputations in Miami/Key West, not Tampa or the Panhandle. Third, a genuinely different, sourced architectural/climate register: Miami Beach's Art Deco Historic District (800+ buildings, built 1923–1943) has no equivalent built environment in North Florida, and South Florida sits in a genuinely tropical (not humid subtropical) climate register.

**What this zone characterization is not claiming**: South Florida is not one thing either — Miami's Cuban/Caribbean urban identity, the Keys' fishing-village/tourist identity, and Miami Beach's Art Deco resort identity are three genuinely different registers within this same zone. **Caricature-avoidance note**: South Florida should not default every scene to one of two extremes — a South Beach nightlife/pastel-neon cliché, or a tiki-bar-tourist cliché. Little Havana's everyday register (a family-run cafeteria-style restaurant, a modest ventanita counter, a plain sidewalk) is the more common, less staged-looking real texture of daily South Florida eating, and should be the default for a generic "casual Miami meal" brief.

### Florida-wide (applies to both zones)

- **The screened porch / "lanai" is a genuine, climate-driven Florida outdoor-living feature, statewide — not exclusively a North or South Florida signature.** A screened structure attached to a house, used to eat and socialize outdoors without mosquito exposure, real and postwar-dated; the best-documented architectural history is specifically Miami/South Florida's 1950s modernist housing boom, but the underlying climate driver (heat, humidity, mosquitoes, a pronounced wet season) is not unique to South Florida. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH for the practice being real and climate-driven statewide; the detailed architectural history is South-Florida-specific] [SOURCE: [Click Americana — Screened-in porch, patio and lanai spaces](https://clickamericana.com/topics/home-garden/screened-in-porch-patio-lanai-pool-cage); [Museum of Miami — The Florida Home: Modern Living 1945–1965](https://museumofmiami.org/exhibition/the-florida-home-modern-living-1945-1965/)]

### North Florida

- **North Florida inherits `us.md`'s national norms** except where noted below. Climate is genuinely different from South Florida and worth stating as a concrete staging fact: North Florida has a humid subtropical climate with real winter cool spells and occasional frost, versus South Florida's tropical climate — a North Florida scene can authentically include cooler-weather cues (a light jacket, a fall/football-season framing) that would look wrong for a Miami or Keys scene. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- **The "Florida Cracker" cattle-herding/rural-homestead culture is a real, named, sourced regional identity marker for inland North Florida**, distinct from both coastal seafood culture and Tampa's urban immigrant culture — cattle herders and their families, largely eating off the land (swamp cabbage/hearts of palm, venison, wild turkey, quail, cane syrup on biscuits), gave the term its food-culture content. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — sourced to tourism/heritage-tier sources] [SOURCE: [Florida Division of Historical Resources — Cattle in Florida](https://dos.fl.gov/historical/museums/historical-museums/united-connections/foodways/food-cultivation-and-economies/cattle-in-florida/)] **Not written up as a full dish entry**: swamp cabbage/cane syrup are real but the sourcing found is tourism/heritage-tier rather than deep enough for a full HIGH-confidence visual/plating entry — flagged in the Candidate Queue rather than padded into a thin entry.

### South Florida / Floribbean

- **Black beans and rice, and fried plantains, are the standard, near-universal starch/side pairing for a Cuban-Miami plated main** (ropa vieja, roast pork, etc.) — stated once here rather than repeated on every dish entry below. Two real, coexisting forms exist: **moros y cristianos** (black beans and white rice cooked/served together, the more Havana-associated form) and **congrí** (a red-bean, Oriente-Cuba-associated variant); Miami's dominant register in available sourcing is moros y cristianos — black beans and white rice plated separately as two visually distinct components (black beans matte and thick, white rice fluffy and separate-grained). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM for the Miami-prevalence claim specifically]
  - **Plantains**: **tostones** (green, unripe, sliced, fried twice for a crisp, craggy, pale golden-tan exterior with a dense, starchy interior) and **maduros** (ripe, yellow-skinned-with-black-spots, sliced and pan-fried once — soft, caramelized, deep amber-brown, sweet) are both real, common, non-hierarchical accompaniments, not substitutes for each other. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- **Cuban coffee culture and the *ventanita* — informational venue context, not a beverage-catalog entry.** The **cortadito** (Cuban espresso "cut" with steamed milk) is a hallmark of Miami's Cuban-American coffee culture, and the **ventanita** — a tiny walk-up coffee window built directly into the exterior wall of a bakery, café, laundromat, or grocery store — is a genuine, large-scale (Miami is reported to have over 1,000 of them, a single-source estimate), sourced piece of Miami's physical streetscape, useful as staging background even though the coffee itself stays outside this file's dish catalog. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the ventanita's existence and role]
- **A genuine, Florida-native (Seminole-originated) architectural form exists for outdoor dining and should be used instead of a generic "tiki bar" caricature.** The **chickee** — a raised wooden-platform structure with an open-sided frame of bald-cypress poles and a thatched palmetto-frond roof, originally developed by the Seminole people in the early 1800s for shelter in the Everglades — is now widely used commercially across South Florida for open-air bars and restaurant seating, especially waterfront. A real, sourced regional vernacular, not an invented tropical-bar prop. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: Chickee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chickee)]

## VISUAL & PLATING NORMS

### North Florida

- **Cold, cracked-shell seafood service (stone crab) and cold, half-shell raw seafood service (oysters) are both genuinely matte-and-glossy-mixed presentations, not a single uniform "seafood" look.**
- **Fried, breaded exteriors recur across this zone's dishes (deviled crab, fried grouper) and should each get their own checked finish, not one generic "fried food" description** — deviled crab's breadcrumb coating (traditionally stale Cuban bread) fries to a substantial, craggy, deep golden-brown crust around a football/torpedo-shaped croquette, structurally different from a thin, flat, evenly-battered fried grouper fillet.
- **The pressed Cuban sandwich's flattened, smooth-toasted (not grill-striped) crust remains this zone's single most load-bearing texture/finish distinction** — a grill-marked/striped Cuban sandwich is a specific, checkable, commonly-made authenticity error (a panini-press artifact, not the traditional plancha result).

### South Florida / Floribbean

- **Three genuinely different plating registers coexist in this zone and should not be flattened into one "tropical Florida" look.** (1) **Casual Cuban-Miami cafeteria/diner register**: steam-table or counter service, everyday plates, a main plus a mounded scoop of rice and a separate mound of black beans and a few plantain slices — practical, not composed. (2) **Fine-dining "Floribbean" register**: chef-composed plating with negative space, a protein as the visual centerpiece with a small, brightly-colored fruit salsa spooned to one side. (3) **Haitian/Caribbean carryout register**: a styrofoam or foil takeout container with the main and its starch/sides mounded together.
- **Color palette runs vivid and tropical, not muted**: matte near-black beans, bright white separate-grained rice, red/orange tomato-based sauces, deep pink-orange guava paste, golden-fried plantains and pastry — a saturated, high-contrast palette distinct from North Florida's paler, more Spanish/Mediterranean-coded browns.
- **Texture/finish is the primary checkable differentiator between this zone's fried starches, and should not default to one generic "golden and crispy."** Tostones: pale golden-tan, craggy, flattened-and-refried surface with visible ridges from smashing. Maduros: deep amber-to-near-black caramelized surface, soft and glossy inside. Conch fritters: a rough, craggy, deep-fried batter exterior closer to a hush puppy than a smooth tempura.

### North Florida

#### Dish: Cuban sandwich (Tampa) — full authoritative entry, carried forward and expanded from `us.md`'s index entry
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (a genuine civic point of pride, not just a deli item)
- Cuisine lineage: Cuban/Spanish/Italian immigrant-community invention, **developed in Tampa's Ybor City, not Havana**, despite the name — a direct parallel to this KB's spaghetti-and-meatballs/mozzarella-sticks-style naming caution.
- **Causal origin**: Ybor City was founded in 1885–86 as a planned cigar-manufacturing town by Vicente Martínez Ybor, who moved Cuban cigar-rolling labor and Cuban tobacco to US soil to avoid import tariffs. Cuban, Spanish, and Italian immigrant cigar workers arrived en masse — by 1910 Ybor City's cigar factories employed over 10,000 workers among a local population that included roughly 14,000 Cuban, 7,500 Spanish, and 1,500 Italian residents. The Cuban sandwich is a direct product of this specific multi-immigrant-community mixing (Cuban roast pork and bread, Spanish ham, Italian salami, Swiss cheese), a cheap, portable, filling meal for factory workers. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Library of Congress — Birth of Ybor City, the Cigar Capital of the World](https://guides.loc.gov/this-month-in-business-history/october/ybor-city)]
- Regional form variation: **Form-changing, and a live, still-contested civic identity question.** Tampa's version includes **Genoa salami** layered between the roast pork and ham; Miami's version does not, and Miami residents/officials have publicly rejected salami as inauthentic ("Salami is for pizza" — Miami Mayor Tomás Regalado, 2012). In 2012 the Tampa City Council passed a resolution officially declaring the "Historic Tampa Cuban Sandwich" the city's signature sandwich; an NPR reader poll found Tampa's version favored 57% to 43%. **This is the single most checkable, name-the-city authenticity detail**: a Cuban sandwich depicting Tampa should show a visible layer of salami; one depicting Miami should not. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [WUSF — The Cuban Is Officially Tampa's Signature Sandwich](https://www.wusf.org/culture/2012-04-19/the-cuban-is-officially-tampas-signature-sandwich); [NPR — The Cuban Sandwich Crisis: Tampa v. Miami, For the Win](https://www.npr.org/sections/thesalt/2012/04/21/151050107/the-cuban-sandwich-crisis-tampa-v-miami-for-the-win)]
- Serving format: Sandwich/handheld — no plated sibling documented.
- Primary protein: Ham, roasted (mojo-marinated) pork, and — in the Tampa version specifically — Genoa salami.
- Primary starch/accompaniment: Cuban bread — a distinct bread, not a generic sub roll.
- Side dishes/condiments: Swiss cheese, dill pickle slices, yellow mustard — spread inside before pressing, not served on the side.
- Venue variation: Tampa's classic Cuban cafeteria/lunch-counter setting (steam-table Cuban comfort food alongside the sandwich) is the most authentic North Florida register; a modern fast-casual Cuban sandwich shop is an equally real, more contemporary alternative.
- Visual/plating characteristics: Pressed flat and compressed — roughly half its unpressed height — on a plancha or sandwich press, giving the bread a **golden-brown, evenly toasted, smooth, slightly flattened surface**; the traditional plancha method does **not** produce ridged grill marks (a panini-press artifact, a checkable inauthenticity if present). Melted white Swiss cheese and a thin line of yellow mustard are visible squeezed out at the pressed edges. The bread itself, before pressing, is pale, soft-crumbed, and light — traditional Cuban bread is baked with a fresh palmetto-palm leaf laid across the top before baking, which cracks the crust in a single line down the loaf's length. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for composition, Tampa origin, and press/plancha finish] [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: Cuban bread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_bread)] **Prompt-writing note**: La Segunda Central Bakery (est. 1915, Ybor City, the largest Tampa Cuban-bread producer) is a real, specific, still-operating business cited here for authenticity/history — describe a generic traditional Cuban bakery in an actual image prompt, not this specific storefront.
- **Miami's Cuban sandwich venue register — a documented, genuinely distinct variant, kept here as a single addendum rather than a competing entry per the merge decision in FILE ROLE & METHOD above.** Miami versions are typically pork-and-ham only (no salami), on a softer-crumbed Miami-style Cuban bread, pressed to a smooth, crisp, golden exterior — contrasted in food journalism against Tampa's craggier, more uneven crust. A "double press" technique (an initial open-faced press to crisp the ham before final assembly and pressing) is documented at a well-known, long-operating Little Havana restaurant. **Per the landmark-avoidance rule**, that restaurant is cited for historical/authenticity evidence only — describe a generic, brightly-lit, cafeteria-style Little Havana Cuban restaurant instead. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Miami New Times — Miami vs. Tampa: Which City Makes the Best Cuban Sandwich?](https://www.miaminewtimes.com/food-drink/miami-vs-tampa-which-city-makes-the-best-cuban-sandwich-21965593/)]
- Common confusion: Should not be confused with a generic Italian sub/hoagie — Cuban bread is lighter, softer-crumbed, and the sandwich is always pressed hot, never served cold/unpressed. Should not be confused with a Miami-style Cuban sandwich if a brief specifically names Tampa (no salami in Miami's version) or vice versa.
- Confidence: HIGH overall.
- Sources: [Wikipedia: Cuban sandwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_sandwich); [La Segunda Bakery — How We Bake History](https://www.lasegundabakery.com/our-story); [Tasting Table — You Don't Need A Panini Press To Make Warm Cuban Sandwiches](https://www.tastingtable.com/1415224/no-panini-press-warm-cuban-sandwiches/)

#### Dish: Minorcan clam chowder (St. Augustine, datil pepper)
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (a signature, civic-identity dish for St. Augustine specifically, comparable in local pride to the Cuban sandwich's Tampa status)
- Cuisine lineage: Minorcan-immigrant tradition — genuinely distinct from both mainstream Southern and Cuban-Floridian food lineages elsewhere in this file.
- **Origin/settlement history**: Minorca (an island off Spain, then under British colonial control) supplied indentured laborers — alongside Greek and Italian recruits — for Andrew Turnbull's 1768 New Smyrna, Florida colonization project, the largest British colonization attempt in the region. After roughly seven years, the Minorcan community relocated about 70 miles north to St. Augustine, where their descendants remain a substantial, still-identifiable community today (St. Johns County is cited at roughly 25,000–26,000 Minorcan descendants). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Florida's Historic Coast — The Datil Pepper: A History of Heat and Flavor](https://www.floridashistoriccoast.com/blog/the-datil-pepper-a-history-of-heat-and-flavor-in-st-augustine/); [Southern Cultures — Bottling Hell: Marketing St. Augustine, Florida's Datil Pepper](https://www.southerncultures.org/article/bottling-hell-marketing-st-augustine-floridas-datil-pepper/)]
- **The datil pepper**: a small chile in the habanero family, ripening green to yellow to orange, with a sweet-tangy, fruity heat comparable to a Scotch bonnet — genuinely, specifically associated with St. Augustine/northeast Florida (it once appeared on the city's own seal). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Regional form variation: Genuinely confined to St. Augustine/northeast Florida — not a statewide or national dish.
- Serving format: Plate/bowl — a soup course, not a handheld item.
- Primary composition: A tomato-based (never cream-based) chowder — salt pork, onion, green bell pepper, carrots, diced potato, canned tomato and tomato paste, garlic, herbs, clam juice/fish stock, clams, and the datil pepper as the defining heat/flavor element.
- Visual/plating characteristics: **Deep, opaque red-orange color** from the tomato base — the single most checkable differentiator from every other chowder already documented in this KB (`us-northeast.md`'s New England chowder is pale ivory/cream-based; Manhattan-style is a thinner tomato-red; this is its own distinct, denser, more vegetable-forward red). Visible chunky diced vegetables and clam pieces suspended in a thick, not-thin, tomato-based liquid — hearty/stew-like rather than a thin broth soup. No cream, no dairy sheen. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Common confusion: Should not be rendered as a pale, cream-based New England-style chowder or a thin, brothy Manhattan-style chowder — more tomato-saturated in color and thicker/chunkier than either.
- Confidence: HIGH for origin, geography, and composition.
- Sources: [Authentic Florida — Authentic Minorcan Clam Chowder Recipe](https://authenticflorida.com/authentic-minorcan-clam-chowder-recipe/); [Wikipedia: Datil pepper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Datil_pepper)

#### Dish: Tampa deviled crab (Ybor City)
- Category: Everyday — a working-class street-food/lunch-counter item historically, still a genuine local specialty today
- Cuisine lineage: A genuine fusion dish credited to Ybor City's mixed Cuban/Spanish/Italian cigar-worker immigrant community, most likely adapted from a Spanish croquette tradition — the same causal community as the Cuban sandwich above, a second, independent data point for Ybor City's distinct food identity.
- **Origin, honestly contested**: one popular account ties the dish's rise to the 1920 Ybor City cigar workers' strike (a cheap way for striking workers to feed their families); other sources describe it more simply as a Spanish croquette technique transformed by Tampa's immigrant communities. Both accounts agree on the causal community and era; the exact triggering event is not resolved. A once-common Cuban-immigrant street vendor (riding a motorized bike with a crab-shaped logo, selling devil crab through the 1920s–1953) is documented as a real historical distribution channel. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH for the community/era; LOW for the specific 1920-strike origin claim] [SOURCE: [Tampa Magazine — Take a Bite of a True Tampa Original: The Devil(ed) Crab](https://tampamagazines.com/deviled-crabs/); [Southern Foodways Alliance — Tampa Devil Crabs (oral history)](https://www.southernfoodways.org/oral-history/tampa-devil-crabs/)]
- Serving format: Snack/handheld or a plated appetizer — a self-contained, breaded, fried croquette.
- Primary composition: Blue crab meat mixed with a **sofrito** (diced onion, green pepper, garlic, tomato, oregano, sautéed in olive oil) — a marker of the dish's Cuban/Spanish lineage, distinct from an Anglo-American "deviled" preparation (typically a mustard/mayonnaise dressing).
- Primary starch/coating: Breadcrumbs traditionally made from **slightly stale Cuban bread** — tying this dish directly back to the same bread tradition as the Cuban sandwich entry above.
- Visual/plating characteristics: **A distinctive palm-sized, elongated football/torpedo shape** — this silhouette should distinguish it at a glance from a round hush puppy, a cylindrical mozzarella stick, or a flat crab cake patty. Exterior: deep, craggy, substantial golden-brown breadcrumb crust (not a thin, smooth tempura-style coating) from deep-frying. When broken or bitten open, the interior shows a moist, chunky, orange-red-tinted crab-and-sofrito filling (the tomato in the sofrito tints the filling, distinct from a plain white crab-cake interior) with visible small flecks of green pepper and onion — a genuinely different cross-section from a Maryland-style crab cake (`us-mid-atlantic.md`), which is a broader, flatter, less-breaded patty with more visible lump crab.
- Common confusion: Should not be rendered as a round, flat Maryland-style crab cake or as a plain fried hush puppy.
- Confidence: HIGH for composition, shape, and Ybor City community origin; LOW-MEDIUM for the specific 1920-strike founding story.
- Sources: [La Segunda Bakery — Exploring Food Traditions in Ybor City](https://www.lasegundabakery.com/blog/exploring-food-traditions-in-ybor-city)

#### Dish: Apalachicola oysters (raw bar)
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion — a genuine regional-pride dish for the Florida Panhandle's "Forgotten Coast"
- Cuisine lineage: Gulf Coast working-waterfront tradition — an ecological/economic story (a specific bay's oyster-producing capacity) rather than a settlement-immigration story.
- **Causal/geographic basis**: Apalachicola Bay, on the Florida Panhandle's Gulf coast, was for well over a century one of the most productive oyster estuaries in the country — at its documented peak supplying roughly 90% of Florida's wild-caught oysters and about 10% of the entire nation's, earning Apalachicola the nickname "Oyster Capital of the World." Commercial harvesting dates to the 1800s. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [WJHG — The former "Oyster Capital of the World" could soon make a comeback](https://www.wjhg.com/2025/08/13/former-oyster-capital-world-could-soon-make-comeback/)]

[CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the collapse/closure/reopening timeline]
- Serving format: Raw on the half shell (the classic, most iconic presentation) or, as a secondary, equally real preparation, chargrilled/baked with butter and garlic.

- Visual/plating characteristics: **Raw oysters on the half shell** show a matte, craggy, irregular grey-white exterior shell cradling a glossy, translucent, grey-tan-to-beige oyster body sitting in a small pool of its own clear-to-slightly-cloudy liquor — a genuinely different finish from stone crab claws' hard, glossy, brightly colored shell (below), worth distinguishing since both are "cold seafood served on ice" dishes in this file. Multiple oysters typically arranged in a ring or cluster on crushed ice.
- Common confusion: Should not be confused with a Gulf/Chesapeake blue crab or a Maine/Northeast oyster presentation already documented elsewhere in this KB.
- Confidence: HIGH for the Apalachicola Bay history/identity and the fishery-collapse timeline; MEDIUM for the specific raw-bar visual/serving-vessel description.

#### Dish: Grouper sandwich (Florida Gulf Coast)
- Category: Everyday — a genuine casual/beach-dining staple, not a special-occasion dish
- Cuisine lineage: Gulf Coast fishing-economy origin — the origin story here is economic (a formerly low-status "bait fish" becoming a prized menu item), not immigrant-community-driven.
- **Origin, honestly contested**: grouper was historically considered a lower-status fish, even used as bait for redfish in the early 1900s; it rose to Gulf Coast menu prominence in the 1970s as other, previously-preferred fish (kingfish, pompano) became more expensive. A Tampa-area fishing-family member is one named claimant to having first put a fried grouper sandwich on a restaurant menu in the late 1960s/early 1970s, but this is a single-source, unverifiable oral claim. The earliest *documented* restaurant advertisement, per Tampa Bay History Center research, is a 1974 ad for a restaurant in Panama City — meaning this dish's documented history spans two North Florida Gulf Coast cities, not one single point of origin. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH for the 1970s rise and 1974 Panama City documented-first-ad claim; LOW for the specific named-first-claimant claim] [SOURCE: [Tampa Magazine — The History of the Iconic Grouper Sandwich](https://tampamagazines.com/the-history-of-the-iconic-grouper-sandwich/)]
- Regional form variation: Prevalence-coded to Florida's Gulf Coast specifically (Tampa Bay through the Panhandle) rather than the Atlantic side or the rest of the US — genuinely regionally distinctive, not a generic "fish sandwich" found equally everywhere. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- Serving format: Sandwich/handheld — no plated, knife-and-fork sibling documented as equally iconic.
- **Two genuine, coexisting preparation variants**: **fried** (the more traditional, casual-beach-shack default) and **blackened** (a Cajun/Creole-spice-crusted, pan-or-grill-seared alternative, now equally common).
- Side dishes/condiments: Lettuce and tomato slices, tartar sauce as the default condiment; banana pepper rings appear in some sourcing as a common regional addition.
- Visual/plating characteristics: **Fried version** — a thick, substantial white fish fillet (grouper is a large, dense, flaky white-fleshed fish, notably thicker than a thin generic "fish sandwich" patty) coated in a batter or breading that fries to an evenly golden-brown, moderately crisp exterior; cut or bitten, the interior shows large, moist, opaque-white flakes, cooked through — this large-flake, dense-but-tender interior is the dish's key differentiator from a thinner, more uniform whitefish like tilapia or cod. **Blackened version** — the fillet's exterior turns a very dark brown-to-near-black from a Cajun/Creole dry-spice crust seared at high heat, a matte, deeply charred-looking (though not actually burnt) finish, with the same moist, large-flake white interior beneath.
- Common confusion: Should not be rendered with a thin, uniform, finely-flaked fillet (more characteristic of a generic fast-food fish sandwich). A blackened grouper sandwich should not be confused with a plain grilled fish sandwich (no dark spice crust) or an actually-burnt fillet.
- Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH overall — the dish's regional identity and two-preparation-variant structure are well-corroborated; the exact origin story is contested/unverifiable as noted above.
- Sources: [Chowhound — This Fish Sandwich Is One Of Florida's Signature Seafood Dishes](https://www.chowhound.com/2108967/grouper-sandwich-florida-seafood-staple/); [TasteAtlas — Grouper Sandwich](https://www.tasteatlas.com/grouper-sandwich)

#### Entry: Floribbean cuisine (a named culinary movement/category, not a single fixed dish)
- Category: Special-occasion-leaning (fine-dining register), though the ingredients/techniques it popularized (tropical fruit salsas, mild white fish preparations) have since diffused into more everyday South Florida cooking
- Cuisine lineage: A genuine, dated, US (specifically South Florida) culinary invention — a fusion built from Caribbean, Latin American, and tropical ingredients/techniques layered onto local Florida seafood and produce, not an imported cuisine. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- **Origin and naming — an honest, sourced ambiguity, not resolved into one tidy story**: the term is documented as originating in a 1988 letter by chef Norman Van Aken describing his plan to fuse "the golden treasures and vibrant calypso flavors of old Key West" with a contemporary sensibility — but Van Aken himself is on record saying he never actually adopted or used "Floribbean" to describe his own cooking, preferring "New World Cuisine." The cuisine/movement itself is real and well-documented; the specific term functioning as its most common public label is a separate, slightly awkward fact from its own coiner's account. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the movement's reality and chefs involved; MEDIUM-HIGH, with this caveat, for the term itself] [SOURCE: [Norman Van Aken — On the term "Floribbean Cuisine"](https://normanvanaken.com/food-culture/floribbean-cuisine-term/); [Wikipedia: Floribbean cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floribbean_cuisine)]
- Causal driver: the influx of Cuban immigrants to South Florida following the 1959 Cuban Revolution is documented as a specific, important factor — the Caribbean strains already embedded in Cuban food culture became a direct inspiration for the "Mango Gang" (Van Aken, Allen Susser, Mark Militello, Douglas Rodriguez), who built the movement's reputation from the 1980s onward, primarily through restaurants in Miami and the Keys. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Defining characteristics: emphasis on fresh, local ingredients and complex spice medleys — strong flavors deliberately offset by milder ones; extensive use of several kinds of peppers, but generally **less raw chili heat than the Caribbean dishes that inspire it**. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- Illustrative dish, flagged as representative rather than "the" one canonical Floribbean dish: **grilled mahi-mahi with mango salsa**. Visual/plating characteristics: a mild white fish fillet, grilled with light char marks, as the plate's visual centerpiece, paired with a chunky, brightly-colored raw salsa (diced mango, sometimes pineapple, red bell pepper or red onion, cilantro) spooned to one side rather than blanketing it. **Texture/finish**: the fish should show light grill char striping on an otherwise pale, opaque, flaky (not glossy or sauce-coated) surface — well-done throughout; the salsa should read as fresh and raw (bright, saturated, slightly wet-looking) rather than cooked-down or glossy like a reduction. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — recipe-blog-tier sourcing]
- Common confusion: should not be conflated with a generic "Caribbean restaurant" or a Cuban-Miami cafeteria register — Floribbean is specifically a chef-driven, composed fine-dining register with fruit-forward brightness.
- Confidence: HIGH for the movement's existence, causal drivers, and named chefs; MEDIUM for the specific illustrative-dish visual detail.
- Sources: [Tasting Table — What Is 'Floribbean' Cuisine And How Did It Come About?](https://www.tastingtable.com/1226720/what-is-floribbean-cuisine-and-how-did-it-come-about/)

#### Dish: Ropa vieja
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (widely described as Cuba's national dish, and a Cuban-Miami restaurant/home staple)
- Cuisine lineage: Cuban, with roots traced by multiple sources to Spain (Sephardic Jewish and/or Canary Islands settler cooking traditions) before evolving with Caribbean/Cuban ingredients. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- Regional form variation: None identified as form-changing within the US — present wherever Cuban-American communities are, but Miami's Little Havana is the single most concentrated, most-cited US venue. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- Serving format: Plate only — no handheld/sandwich sibling documented.
- Primary protein: Beef, traditionally flank steak, slow-cooked until tender enough to shred into long strands.
- Primary starch/accompaniment: White rice and black beans (moros y cristianos), often with fried plantains (tostones or maduros).
- Side dishes/condiments: Green olives and sometimes raisins or capers, documented as common additions to the sauce itself, not a separate table condiment.
- Visual/plating characteristics: The dish's name ("old clothes" in Spanish) is a direct visual metaphor — the shredded beef should look like loose, torn strands/fibers, not a solid cut of meat, evoking pulled fabric. The beef sits in a thick, reddish-orange tomato-based sauce visibly studded with strips of red and green bell pepper, sliced onion, and small distinct dark-green olives. **Texture/finish**: the beef should read as well-done, fibrous, and matte (not glossy or seared); the sauce should be thick and clinging rather than thin/soupy, with a glossy sheen distinct from the beef's own matte fibers. Bell peppers should stay firm and bright, not cooked to a dull gray-green.
- Common confusion: Should not be confused with **Chicago Italian beef** (`us-northeast.md`) or **Baltimore pit beef** (`us-mid-atlantic.md`) — both are sliced or shaved whole cuts, not long-braised-and-shredded strands, and neither carries ropa vieja's tomato-pepper-olive sauce.
- Confidence: HIGH for composition, visual appearance, and Cuban-national-dish status; MEDIUM for the precise Spain-to-Cuba origin chain.

A genuinely two-way cross-cultural lineage worth stating explicitly. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]

- Confidence: HIGH overall for composition and Miami-specificity; MEDIUM for the exact 1920s Havana origin story.

#### Dish: Key lime pie (full authoritative entry, per the merge decision in FILE ROLE & METHOD above)
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion — Florida's official state pie, but also an everyday dessert item at casual restaurants throughout South Florida and the Keys
- Cuisine lineage: Genuinely South Florida/Keys-originated — sources broadly agree the pie developed in Key West in the late 19th century (one detailed account dates it to at least the 1890s), though the exact originator is contested between at least two named accounts (a folk story crediting "Aunt Sally," a cook for Key West's first millionaire, William Curry, versus a competing claim crediting a 1931 Borden Company product-development effort in New York) — this file flags the origin-story conflict explicitly rather than picking one silently. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH for a general Key West/1890s-or-later origin window; LOW-MEDIUM for either specific originator story]
- Regional form variation: Originated specifically in the Keys/South Florida; has since become Florida's official state pie (a statewide symbolic designation).
- Serving format: Plate only (sliced from a whole pie) — no handheld sibling.

The reliance on sweetened condensed milk rather than fresh milk/cream is itself historically caused: fresh milk and reliable refrigeration were uncommon in the isolated Florida Keys until the 1930s, making shelf-stable condensed milk the practical choice. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for composition; MEDIUM-HIGH for the refrigeration-scarcity causal explanation]
- Side dishes/condiments: Topped with either a torched meringue or a dollop of whipped cream — both real, coexisting, non-hierarchical toppings per §4.6.
- Visual/plating characteristics: **The single most important, most commonly-gotten-wrong checkable detail: an authentic key lime pie's filling is a pale, custardy yellow — not bright green.** The widespread bright-green version is a well-documented inauthenticity marker, traced by food journalism to early-20th-century tourists conflating the green color of the key lime fruit itself with the color of the pie, with some sellers subsequently adding artificial green food coloring to match that expectation. A correctly-depicted pie's cross-section shows a matte, tan-to-golden-brown graham cracker crust (grainy, crumbly texture) holding a smooth, dense, set custard filling in a pale creamy yellow (closer to a lemon curd's color than any green), topped with either **toasted meringue** (glossy-white body with browned, scorched peaks from a broiler or torch) or **whipped cream** (softer, matte, less structured peaks, pure white with no browning). **Texture/finish**: the custard should read as smooth and dense/set — a firm, sliceable custard, not a mousse; the crust should look matte and slightly rough/granular, never glossy or smooth like a pâte sucrée.
- Common confusion: Should not be depicted with a bright, saturated green filling — this is the primary, well-documented, checkable authenticity error for this specific dish.
- Confidence: HIGH for composition, the pale-yellow-not-green color fact, and the condensed-milk causal story; MEDIUM for the specific competing origin-story details.

#### Dish: Stone crab claws (full authoritative entry, per the merge decision in FILE ROLE & METHOD above — see the North Florida zone's own cross-reference note for its real but secondary Big Bend/Cedar Key presence)
- Category: Special-occasion/seasonal delicacy — priced and marketed as a premium, seasonal item, not an everyday dish
- Cuisine lineage: A specifically Miami Beach-anchored popularization story.
- **Origin**: Joe's Stone Crab, still operating in Miami Beach, was founded in 1913 by Joe Weiss; in the early 1920s, encouraged by a marine biologist, Weiss began boiling and chilling local stone crab claws (at the time considered inedible/undesirable) and serving them with a homemade mustard sauce — the dish and presentation that made the restaurant, and by extension Miami Beach, a culinary landmark. **Per the landmark-avoidance rule**, Joe's is cited here for real historical/authenticity evidence, not as something to name or reproduce in an image prompt — describe a generic Miami-area seafood restaurant or seasonal seafood market instead. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Miami Palm Beach Today — Stone Crabs & Stardom](https://miamipalmbeachtoday.com/2026/03/25/stone-crabs-stardom-the-untold-magic-of-joes-in-miami-beach/)]
- Regional form variation: **More strongly South Florida/Miami-identified than a North Florida dish, on the strength of Joe's founding role and modern harvest volume** — Monroe County/the Keys and Collier County/Everglades City together accounted for roughly 52% of the documented 2010 statewide harvest, and Everglades City brands itself "Stone Crab Capital of the World." The dish's physical form and presentation are consistent statewide (the North Florida/Big Bend presence, documented since the 1920s, is real but secondary — see that zone's cross-reference note).
- Serving format: The claws only, served cold — this is the entire dish; there is no separate "whole crab" plated sibling, because Florida's harvesting regulation is specifically claw-only.
- **Seasonality, a genuinely hard, checkable fact**: Florida's stone crab harvest season runs October 15 through May 1 — an authentic stone crab scene reads as a fall/winter/early-spring occasion, not a midsummer one. Florida regulation permits removing only one claw per crab (even if both meet the legal minimum size of 2⅞ inches), after which the live crab is returned to the water to regenerate the lost claw over 12–18 months; declawing egg-bearing female crabs is prohibited. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [FWC — Stone crab season starts Oct. 15](https://myfwc.com/news/all-news/stone-crab-1024/)]
- Side dishes/condiments: A cold, creamy **mustard sauce** (mayonnaise, dry mustard, Worcestershire sauce, steak sauce, and cream) is the standard, near-universal accompaniment — served in a small ramekin for dipping, not poured over the claws; a lemon wedge is a common secondary accompaniment. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Visual/plating characteristics: **A genuine, checkable, easy-to-get-wrong color transformation between raw and cooked claws.** Raw/live stone crab claws are a dull, dark brownish-red to greenish-gray with dusky gray mottling and shiny black claw tips. Once cooked (boiled), the shell transforms to a vibrant, glossy orange-to-red color, sometimes with deeper maroon/purple highlights near the joints, while the black claw tips remain black — the correctly-depicted serving color is this bright cooked orange-red, not the duller raw color. Claws are served **already cracked** (a shallow crack or split in the shell, prepared before serving so the meat can be lifted out with fingers) — not whole, unbroken shells, and not fully shelled/picked meat either; a partially-exposed white, firm, opaque meat visible at the crack is the correct presentation. The claws should be chilled and glossy-wet-looking from the ice bed. **Texture/finish**: the exposed meat should look firm, opaque, and slightly glossy/moist, in a single dense, curved muscle shape rather than the flaked/shredded texture of picked crab meat (a crab cake, `us-mid-atlantic.md`). The mustard sauce should read as a smooth, pale off-white to light tan, opaque, creamy sauce, distinctly different from a clear vinaigrette or a red cocktail sauce.
- Common confusion: Should not be confused with a Maryland crab cake or steamed blue crab (`us-mid-atlantic.md`) — a different species, different cooking/seasoning conventions, and (for the crab cake specifically) processed/formed meat rather than a whole intact claw. Should not be depicted with the claw's raw dull-brown color, or served hot — this is always a cold dish.
- Confidence: HIGH overall for harvesting regulation, presentation, sauce composition, and the raw-to-cooked color transformation.

#### Dish: Conch fritters
- Category: Everyday snack/appetizer — a Key West/Keys-specific staple, described by multiple sources as one of the two most recognizable dishes of Key West cuisine (alongside key lime pie)
- Cuisine lineage: **Bahamian in origin, not native-Florida** — brought to Key West by Bahamian settlers ("wreckers" and others who shaped the city's early history), for whom conch was an easily accessible, reliable protein source. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Regional form variation: Genuinely Keys-specific within South Florida — not documented as a broader Miami-mainland dish the way the Cuban-Miami dishes above are.
- Serving format: Snack/appetizer, plated as a shareable portion, not a full meal.
- Primary composition: Finely minced conch meat mixed with diced vegetables (onion, bell pepper) and a thick batter, deep-fried into small, irregular rounds — similar in spirit and form to a hush puppy, but with more visible ingredient texture.
- Side dishes/condiments: Served with a citrus-forward remoulade or dipping sauce as a common, though not confirmed-universal, pairing. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- Visual/plating characteristics: Irregular, roughly golf-ball-to-egg-sized rounds, not uniformly spherical — a deliberately rustic, hand-formed shape. **Texture/finish**: the exterior is a deep golden-brown, rough and craggy (visible batter texture and small ingredient bits breaking the surface) from deep-frying; cut or broken open, the interior shows a moist, slightly dense, chunky texture with visible small flecks of minced conch and diced vegetable distributed through the batter — not a smooth, uniform, hush-puppy-style cornmeal interior.
- Common confusion: Should not be depicted as a plain hush puppy (uniform interior, no visible seafood or vegetable flecks) — the visible minced-conch/vegetable texture inside is the confirming detail.
- Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH for composition, visual description, and Bahamian origin; MEDIUM for the specific dipping-sauce convention.
- Sources: [Food Republic — The Iconic Florida Dish That Has Bahamian Roots](https://www.foodrepublic.com/2010099/iconic-florida-dish-bahamian-roots-conch-fritters/)

#### Dish: Griot (fried pork) with pikliz (Little Haiti)
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion — a Haitian culinary staple with strong presence in Miami's Little Haiti specifically, per multiple sources describing the neighborhood as "Miami's undisputed capital for Caribbean food"
- Cuisine lineage: Haitian, brought to Miami by the Haitian immigrant community that began arriving in significant numbers in the 1950s–60s. Florida holds an estimated 500,000 Haitian Americans — nearly half the US total — concentrated largely in South Florida, making Little Haiti a genuine, large-scale, well-populated cultural anchor rather than a small enclave. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Regional form variation: Not identified as form-changing — griot is described consistently without a documented Miami-specific twist on the preparation itself; its significance here is Little Haiti's concentration and cultural weight as a Miami neighborhood.
- Serving format: Plate — served as a main, typically with rice and pikliz alongside.
- Primary protein: Pork, first simmered (often in a citrus-and-seasoning marinade) and then fried — a two-step cooking method, not simply fried raw pork.
- Side dishes/condiments: **Pikliz** — a spicy, pickled cabbage-and-vegetable slaw (cabbage, carrots, bell peppers, scotch bonnet or similar chile, in a vinegar brine) — served alongside or on top as a near-mandatory pairing, both a condiment and a textural/acidic counterpoint to the rich fried pork.
- Visual/plating characteristics: Pork pieces (typically bone-in or larger chunks rather than uniform cutlets) with a deeply browned, matte, crisp fried exterior — the simmer-then-fry method should read visually as a substantial, craggy, well-browned crust (closer to a fried-chicken-style crust than a thin pan-sear). Pikliz provides a sharp visual and textural contrast: pale cabbage and orange-carrot shreds, flecked with red pepper, sitting in a clear-to-pale vinegar brine — crisp, raw-looking, and wet/glossy, directly against the pork's matte, dry-looking fried crust. **Texture/finish**: the pork's exterior should be matte, deeply golden-to-dark-brown, and visibly craggy/textured; the interior, given the simmer-first method, should look tender and moist rather than dry. Pikliz should stay crisp and raw-looking, its vegetables holding distinct shape and color.
- Common confusion: Should not be confused with a generic fried pork chop or Southern-fried pork (no equivalent to pikliz as an accompaniment) or with the frita/Cuban-Miami dishes above (a genuinely separate immigrant-community food tradition).
- Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH for composition and the simmer-then-fry method; MEDIUM for the specific serving-vessel/plating-arrangement detail, which was not independently sourced to the same depth as this file's Cuban-Miami entries.
- Sources: [Norman Van Aken — A Word On Food: Griot](https://normanvanaken1.substack.com/p/a-word-on-food-griot); [The Sunshine Republic — The Haitian Community in Florida](https://thesunshinerepublic.com/2025/03/31/the-haitian-community-in-florida-culture-and-contributions/)
