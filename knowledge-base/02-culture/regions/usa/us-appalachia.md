## FILE ROLE & METHOD

This is the fourth regional file, added to `us.md`'s 13-file list because Appalachia was found to have a distinct pantry (climate/geography-driven) and settlement pattern not captured by `us-south.md` (which explicitly excludes Appalachia, Florida, and Louisiana Creole/Cajun) or by `us-mid-atlantic.md`. Nothing in `us.md` pointed here before this pass — every dish and environment claim below is genuinely new content, not a promoted index entry.

**Single-zone file, unlike `us-northeast.md`, `us-mid-atlantic.md`, and `us-south.md`.** Those files split into two-to-three internal zones because real, causally-distinct sub-clusters existed within the region. This pass did not find evidence of a comparably sharp internal split within Appalachia's own food culture — the corn/beans/pork/foraging pantry, the coal-mining settlement history, and the mountain-holler settlement pattern recur consistently from West Virginia south through the mountain counties of Kentucky, Tennessee, Virginia, and North Carolina, without a documented "would swapping norms between two Appalachian sub-areas look visibly wrong" case as clear as, say, Maine lobster shacks vs. Boston brownstones. **This is a working judgment from one research pass, not an exhaustively validated triage** — flagged in the Gap Log below as a real possible under-count (a Central Appalachia/West Virginia-Kentucky vs. Southern Appalachia/North Carolina-Georgia split is plausible and was not ruled out, only not found with strong-enough evidence this pass to build).

**Scope**: West Virginia (entirely Appalachian), eastern Kentucky, the mountains of eastern Tennessee (explicitly **not** Nashville or Memphis, which `us-south.md` covers as its own zone), western North Carolina, western Virginia's mountain counties, and the mountain (not Piedmont/Deep-South-coded) parts of northern Georgia and Alabama where genuinely Appalachian-coded. **This is narrower than the Appalachian Regional Commission's (ARC) own official 423-county, 13-state economic-development definition** (which also includes parts of New York, Ohio, Maryland, Mississippi, and South Carolina) — that ARC boundary is a federal economic-development designation, not itself a food-culture claim, cited here only as background confirming that Appalachia is a real, officially-recognized, multi-state region, not a citation for this file's own (narrower, food-culture-driven) scope. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the ARC boundary itself] [SOURCE: [Appalachian Regional Commission — About the Appalachian Region](https://www.arc.gov/about-the-appalachian-region/)]

**Overlap discipline with `us-south.md` and `us-mid-atlantic.md`**: this file deliberately does not build full entries for dishes/registers that are genuinely shared, non-distinct Southern institutions rather than Appalachian-specific ones — the "meat-and-three" diner format and general church-potluck/"dinner on the grounds" culture are both flagged explicitly below as **shared with the broader South, not Appalachia-exclusive** (consistent with `us-south.md`'s own Tennessee-zone and Deep South-zone treatment of meat-and-three), with only their Appalachian-specific menu content (soup beans, leather britches, ramps) treated as this file's own material. This file also does not claim BBQ, fried chicken, or hot chicken as Appalachian-defining, since none of those cleared a "distinctly Appalachian, not just present in Appalachia" bar in this pass's research — Appalachia's own defining food identity runs through preserved/foraged pantry staples (beans, corn, cured pork, wild greens) rather than a barbecue or fried-chicken tradition, which is where the sharpest, most checkable contrast with `us-south.md`'s content lies.

**Typical use note** (following `us-northeast.md`'s, `us-mid-atlantic.md`'s, and `us-south.md`'s own calibration): expect the realistic use case to skew toward general, archetypal scenes — a mountain-home kitchen, a front porch, a small-town diner counter, a community supper — rather than hyper-local, county-specific staging. This file does not have a quick-reference table split by sub-zone (since there is only one zone), but the table below still separates the broadly-reusable registers from the more granular dish-specific detail further down.

## ZONE CHARACTERIZATION

Appalachia clears the "would swapping this region's norms into a neighboring region look visibly wrong to someone who knows the place" test on causal grounds distinct from both neighboring files. First, **climate and geography directly shaped the pantry**: higher elevations and a shorter growing season favor cold-hardy crops (potatoes, cabbage, apples) over the rice, seafood, or long-season row-crop agriculture that anchor Deep South and Chesapeake foodways; poorer mountain soils further constrained large-scale farming, pushing mountain households toward small subsistence plots, foraging (ramps, wild greens, berries), hunting, and food preservation (drying, canning, salt-curing) as core survival strategies rather than optional traditions. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Fiveable — Appalachian Foodways](https://fiveable.me/hs-appalachian-studies/unit-4/appalachian-foodways/study-guide/411BmXXOewTSOUDh)] Second, a **distinct settlement pattern**: predominantly Scots-Irish, English, and German immigration into an isolated mountain frontier, overlapping with existing Cherokee foodways, produced a corn/beans/cured-pork pantry genuinely different from the Chesapeake's blue-crab/oyster water-body identity or the Deep South/Lowcountry/Carolinas/Nashville-Memphis clusters `us-south.md` covers. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM] [SOURCE: [Appalachian Genealogy — Three Peoples, One Mountain](https://appalachiangenealogy.substack.com/p/early-settlers-appalachia)] Third, and most concretely dateable: **industrial coal mining from the late 19th century onward** overlaid a second, genuinely distinct settlement institution — the company-owned coal camp/coal town, complete with a company-store commissary and scrip currency — onto the existing rural mountain culture, and directly produced at least one iconic, well-documented dish (the pepperoni roll, born of Italian immigrant coal-mining communities in north-central West Virginia) with no equivalent origin story in either neighboring file. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the coal-camp/commissary institution and the pepperoni roll's origin] [SOURCE: [Appalachian Genealogy — Company Towns and Coal Camps](https://appalachiangenealogy.substack.com/p/company-towns-and-coal-camps-life)]

**What this zone characterization is not claiming**: Appalachia's food culture is not monolithic across its full north-south span, and West Virginia's coal-camp-and-pepperoni-roll identity is not the same lived experience as a Blue Ridge/western-North-Carolina mountain community's — this file groups them because the underlying pantry (beans, corn, cured pork, foraged greens) and the mountain-geography/subsistence-farming causal story are shared across the whole span, on the same "grouped because they clear the distinctness bar together against outside neighbors, not because they're internally identical" logic `us-mid-atlantic.md` uses for Maryland/DC/Virginia. **Caricature-avoidance note**: Appalachia should not default every scene to poverty-coded imagery — modern Appalachian towns, suburbs, and cities (Asheville, Boone, Morgantown, Chattanooga's mountain-facing neighborhoods) are real, unremarkable, contemporary American settings, and the region has an active contemporary fine-dining and craft-food scene reinterpreting these same staple ingredients — this file documents the traditional/staple-food register because that is what has the strongest, most checkable sourcing and the clearest "would look wrong elsewhere" distinctness, not because it is the only authentic register.

## TRUSTED CONTENT

- **The core pantry is corn, dried beans (pinto specifically), cured pork, potatoes, and foraged/preserved greens** — not a Deep-South rice/seafood pantry or a Chesapeake shellfish pantry. Traditional Appalachian cuisine emphasizes locally sourced ingredients, preservation techniques (drying, canning, salt-curing), and resourcefulness, built on cornmeal, pork, beans, and foraged foods like ramps and berries. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Fiveable — Appalachian Foodways](https://fiveable.me/hs-appalachian-studies/unit-4/appalachian-foodways/study-guide/411BmXXOewTSOUDh)]
- **Settlement lineage is Scots-Irish, English, and German, overlapping with pre-existing Cherokee foodways** — a genuinely different immigration story from the Northeast's 19th/20th-century urban immigrant waves or the Chesapeake's own settlement pattern. The Scots-Irish specifically had to transition from a diet based on dairy, potatoes, barley, and rye to one based on corn, wheat, and pork once in Appalachia. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM] [SOURCE: [Appalachian Genealogy — Three Peoples, One Mountain](https://appalachiangenealogy.substack.com/p/early-settlers-appalachia)]
- **Ronni Lundy's *Victuals: An Appalachian Journey* (2016, James Beard Foundation Book of the Year) is the strongest single specialist/practitioner-tier source available on this region's food identity** — cited here as authoritative background establishing that the region's food-writing tradition treats it as its own distinct subject, not for any single specific factual claim. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the book's existence/credentials]
- **Historical game-meat foraging (squirrel, rabbit, groundhog, possum, venison) is real and sourced, but deliberately not built into a full dish entry** — sourcing describes general household practice rather than one specific, visually-checkable, staging-relevant dish. Flagged as a real cultural fact, not a dish-catalog candidate. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- **The coal camp/company town is this region's other formative institution, distinct from its rural-farm identity.** Coal companies built and tightly controlled entire towns (a company store/"commissary," post office, school, worker housing); miners were commonly paid in scrip usable only at the company store, while most families still kept gardens and raised poultry/pigs as a self-sufficiency counterweight. Housing took the form of small, uniform shotgun houses (roughly 12 feet wide, rooms arranged one behind another) built in rows near the mine. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the commissary/scrip system and shotgun-house form; MEDIUM for exact row/uniformity visual detail]
- **Front-porch culture is a real, distinct, still-referenced Appalachian social-eating institution.** In holler (narrow mountain-valley) communities specifically, front porches are a genuine, actively-used gathering space — for shelling peas, stringing beans, peeling apples, and visiting with neighbors — not a decorative architectural leftover. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- **Church dinners and community suppers are a genuine, still-active Appalachian institution, but the institution itself is shared with the broader rural South rather than Appalachia-exclusive** — "dinner on the grounds" is documented as a North Carolina/Southern ritual broadly, not sourced as Appalachia-specific in its basic form. What is more specifically Appalachian is the menu content — leather britches and pickled ramps are named specifically among traditional "dinner on the grounds" dishes — and the standalone ramp-supper/ramp-festival format below, which has no equivalent institution documented for `us-south.md`'s scope. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM for the general institution's Southern framing; MEDIUM-HIGH for the Appalachian-specific menu items]
- **Cornbread's defining "no sugar" tradition is itself a broader Southern (not Appalachia-exclusive) divide, stated honestly rather than claimed as a distinguishing Appalachian trait.** The commonly cited line runs along the Ohio River, not the Appalachian boundary specifically: "if you grew up north of the Ohio River you will put sugar in your cornbread; if you grew up south of the river, you don't." Appalachian cornbread sits squarely within the no-sugar, cast-iron-skillet Southern tradition rather than constituting a separately-evolved regional form; this file could not confirm an Appalachia-specific cornbread form genuinely distinct from `us-south.md`'s own cornbread content, beyond a couple of Appalachian-specific *serving customs*. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM for the Ohio-River framing (single-source-tier); HIGH that no-sugar/skillet cornbread is broadly Southern/Appalachian, not exclusively either]
  - **What is more specifically Appalachian**: crumbling cornbread into a glass of cold buttermilk as a traditional evening snack is specifically associated with Appalachian tradition, alongside the folk saying "if God had meant cornbread to have sugar in it, he'd have called it cake." [CONFIDENCE: LOW-MEDIUM — a single search-aggregated, folk-saying-tier source]

## VISUAL & PLATING NORMS

- **The region's overall palette is muted, earthy, and rustic — cast iron, unglazed pottery, wood tables — rather than bright/colorful or polished/upscale.** [Editorial synthesis from the ingredient and setting facts throughout this file, not an independently sourced styling claim — flag for review.]
- **Cast iron is the region's defining cookware, used specifically because it produces a genuinely different crust/finish than other bakeware.** Cornbread batter poured into a screaming-hot, fat-preheated cast-iron skillet sears on contact, producing a dark-gold, craggy, cracking crust distinct from a pale, soft-crusted cornbread baked in a standard pan. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- **Soup beans' broth is thin and cloudy, not thick or puréed** — a genuine, checkable texture distinction from a creamy bean soup or a thick chili. The beans stay whole and intact, suspended in a thin, pale tan-to-light-brown, slightly cloudy broth colored by the beans' own starch and any pork seasoning — never a thick, opaque, gravy-like stew.
- **Pinto beans show a specific color transformation worth getting right**: raw, they are speckled pinkish-tan and oval; once cooked, the mottled speckling fades and the bean darkens to a more uniform light-to-medium brown, with skins that stay intact but soften — not the deep near-black of a black bean, and not the pale cream of a navy or great northern bean.
- **Leather britches beans have a distinctive rehydrated texture and color that should not be confused with fresh or canned green beans.** The skins take on a silky, near-dissolving texture while the beans turn a darker, more olive-brown-green than a fresh green bean's bright green — a matte, wrinkled, "leathery" surface even after full rehydration is the point-of-difference detail. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- **Ramps have a specific, checkable two-tone appearance that distinguishes them from a scallion or standard leek**: a small white bulb often flushed with a maroon-to-purple blush at the base, attached to broad, flat, deep-green leaves (closer to a lily-of-the-valley leaf in shape than a scallion's thin, tubular green top) — the leaf shape is the most reliable visual differentiator from a scallion. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- **Pepperoni rolls, in their traditional/original form, show no visible melted cheese or sauce** — a genuine, checkable authenticity marker versus a modern deli/bakery variant. A traditional pepperoni roll's cut cross-section shows dense, plain bread dough (lightly stained orange-red in the pockets immediately surrounding the pepperoni, from rendered pepperoni grease) around distinct rounds or a thin log of pepperoni — not a stretchy, melted-cheese pull. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- **Fried apple pies have a specific fry-crisped, blistered crust texture distinct from a baked, flaky pie crust.** The crust surface shows small raised blisters and a firmer, crisper texture than a baked hand pie — crisp at the crimped edge, slightly softer toward the center. The filling cooks down into a darker, more concentrated amber-brown mass — not the pale, chunky, wet-looking filling of a fresh-apple pie.
- **Apple stack cake's finish is the opposite of what most "layer cake" imagery implies — no icing, no frosting, and the top layer left bare.** The thin cake layers are individually pale golden-brown and dry/dense (closer to a firm cookie than a soft sponge) when freshly baked, but after resting overnight or longer with a thick, dark amber-brown spiced apple filling between every layer, the moisture soaks into the cake, visually darkening it at the edges and turning the assembly moist, dense, and cohesive rather than dry and separate-looking. The cut cross-section should show clearly countable, thin, distinct layers with dark filling between each one.
- **Old Bay-style dry-seasoning dusting (`us-mid-atlantic.md`) and New England's butter/chilled-shellfish register (`us-northeast.md`) both have no equivalent in this file** — Appalachia is not a seafood region, and this file's own signature seasonings (black pepper, salt, rendered pork fat, the pungent raw bite of a ramp) should not be confused with either neighboring region's coastal seasoning registers.

## DISH CATALOG

### Dish: Soup beans (pinto beans)
- Category: Everyday — described directly as "the iconic, dollar-stretching, belly-filling, soul-enriching meal of Southern Appalachia"
- Cuisine lineage: Native-American-tradition in the sense of being a genuine Appalachian regional invention/adaptation — heirloom bean-and-corn cultivation traces to Eastern Cherokee varieties traded with early settlers, but the specific pinto-bean version most associated with the dish today likely arrived only around the turn of the 20th century, once rail transportation made shipping bulk dried pinto beans from the Midwest cheaper than growing local heirloom varieties. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM] [SOURCE: [What's Cooking America — Appalachian Soup Beans Recipe and History](https://whatscookingamerica.net/soup/appalachian-soupbeans.htm)]
- Regional form variation: Prevalence-only relative to the broader South — beans and cornbread is a staple dish in Appalachia and throughout the South, but Appalachia is where the dish carries its most concentrated, identity-defining cultural weight (see coal-mining/lunch-pail history above, which is Appalachia-specific).
- Serving format: Bowl, as a main or side at suppertime — not a handheld dish.
- Primary protein: The beans themselves provide the protein; a ham hock, salt pork, or fatback is commonly added during cooking purely as seasoning, not as a separate protein component of the plate.
- Primary starch/accompaniment: Cornbread (see paired-meal note below) — soup beans and cornbread together are the region's most iconic meal pairing, not two independent dishes that happen to be served together.
- Side dishes/condiments: Raw chopped onion or, seasonally, chopped raw or pickled ramps, added at the table as a topping; boiled greens (cabbage or sauerkraut) and stewed or fried potatoes are common accompanying sides; a glass of cold buttermilk is a traditional accompaniment.
- Serving vessel: A plain bowl for the beans; cornbread served alongside on a small plate or directly on the table.
- Venue variation: Home-cooked as an everyday staple; also a small-town diner/lunch-counter daily-special item (see Environment section).
- Visual/plating characteristics: A thin, pale tan-to-light-brown, slightly cloudy broth (from bean starch, not a thickener) with whole, intact pinto beans suspended in it — not mashed or puréed, and not a thick, opaque stew consistency. Cooked pinto beans lose their raw speckled pattern and turn a more uniform light-to-medium brown, with soft-but-intact skins. A few strands of rendered pork fat or a piece of ham hock may be visible in the pot/bowl. Raw white onion or fresh green ramps, chopped and scattered on top, provide the dish's only bright color contrast against the otherwise all-brown/tan palette.
- Common confusion: Should not be rendered as a thick chili (no tomato, no ground meat, no thick roux-based consistency) or as refried beans (beans stay whole, not mashed into a paste).
- Confidence: HIGH overall for the dish's identity and cultural significance; MEDIUM for the specific pinto-vs-heirloom-bean origin timeline.
- Sources: [What's Cooking America — Appalachian Soup Beans Recipe and History](https://whatscookingamerica.net/soup/appalachian-soupbeans.htm); [West Virginia Public Broadcasting — A Taste Of Home](https://wvpublic.org/story/arts-culture/a-taste-of-home-how-pinto-beans-and-cornbread-became-an-appalachian-tradition/)

### Dish: Appalachian skillet cornbread
- Category: Everyday
- Cuisine lineage: Native-American-tradition (corn itself is a New World/Indigenous crop; the specific savory, no-sugar, cast-iron-skillet form is a broader Southern-and-Appalachian tradition — see the explicit honesty note in TRUSTED CONTENT above).
- Regional form variation: **Prevalence/shared-tradition, not form-changing.** This file could not confirm a cornbread form genuinely distinct from `us-south.md`'s own cornbread content — the defining "no sugar, cast-iron skillet, cornmeal-only" tradition runs along a broader North-vs-South (roughly Ohio River) line, not an Appalachia-specific one. What is Appalachia-specific is a serving custom (crumbling into buttermilk as a snack) rather than the bread's own form.
- Serving format: Wedge or square, plated alongside soup beans or other mains — not handheld.
- Primary starch: Cornmeal, traditionally with no added flour and no added sugar. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM for the exact Ohio-River-line framing]
- Serving vessel: Baked and often still served from the cast-iron skillet itself, cut into wedges, or turned out and cut into squares.
- Visual/plating characteristics: A dense, crumbly, coarse interior crumb — not the fine, light, cake-like crumb of a sweetened Northern-style cornbread — pale golden-yellow inside. The crust, from being poured into a screaming-hot, fat-preheated cast-iron skillet, sears immediately into a **dark golden-brown to almost mahogany, craggy, cracking crust** on the bottom and edges, distinctly darker and more textured than the pale interior crumb. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Common confusion: Should not be rendered as a sweet, cake-like cornbread or as a corn muffin.
- Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH for the visual/texture description; MEDIUM for how cleanly this is distinguishable from `us-south.md`'s own cornbread content, since the underlying tradition is explicitly shared rather than Appalachia-exclusive.
- Sources: [Will Wander For Food — Southern Skillet Cornbread, No sugar, no flour](https://willwanderforfood.com/2016/03/05/southern-skillet-cornbread-no-sugar-no-flour/)

### Paired-meal note: Soup beans and cornbread together
Per this project's own practical rule against checklist-clutter (`country-file-schema.md` §7.5), this is documented as a plating/staging note rather than a third, redundant dish entry. When staged together — the region's single most representative everyday meal — the cornbread is typically served on a separate small plate/directly on the table (to be broken by hand and eaten alongside or crumbled directly into the bean broth) or, in the buttermilk-snack custom, crumbled into a separate glass of buttermilk as a different course entirely. A raw-onion or ramp topping, if used, goes on the beans, not the cornbread. The two items should be staged as a single, unified everyday meal rather than as two disconnected dishes that happen to share a table. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]

### Dish: Leather britches beans (shuck beans)
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (a genuine church-supper/community-meal staple)
- Cuisine lineage: Contested/layered — commonly attributed in mountain-South folklore to Cherokee and other Southeastern tribal food-preservation tradition (contemporary Cherokee communities still make shuck beans today), though the practice may also trace to German Palatinate settlers who brought a comparable dried-bean tradition to the mountains, later adopted region-wide. This file states the conflicting-origin evidence explicitly rather than picking one account. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- Regional form variation: None identified within Appalachia — a consistent, region-wide preservation method and dish.

### Dish: Ramps (wild leeks) — as a foraged ingredient and community-meal centerpiece
- Category: Everyday (as a seasonal condiment/side) to special-occasion (the dedicated ramp supper/festival meal)
- Cuisine lineage: Native-American-tradition — ramps are native to the Appalachian region specifically, foraged as one of the first plants to emerge each spring; foraging tradition is often attributed to Cherokee foodways, adopted broadly across the region. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH for the botanical/seasonal claim; MEDIUM for the specifically-Cherokee-origin framing]
- Regional form variation: None — ramps are Appalachia-specific by definition (a native mountain-forest plant); genuinely absent from `us-south.md`'s and `us-mid-atlantic.md`'s scope as a foraged regional ingredient in the way this file documents.
- Serving format: Two genuine, coexisting registers — (1) a raw or lightly pickled condiment, chopped and scattered atop soup beans or other dishes; (2) cooked, most commonly fried in bacon fat together with potatoes (and often eggs), served as a side/main at a community ramp supper alongside pinto beans, cornbread, and ham.
- Side dishes/condiments (ramp-supper format specifically): Potatoes, pinto/brown beans, cornbread, ham and bacon — this is the standard, sourced menu for an organized community ramp supper, not an invented pairing.
- Serving vessel: A simple bowl or plate; at a community ramp supper, served cafeteria/potluck-style on a plate at a long shared table.
- Visual/plating characteristics: A ramp's raw form shows a small white bulb, often blushed with a maroon-to-purple tint at its base, attached to broad, flat, deep-green leaves — broader and flatter than a scallion's thin tubular green top, closer in leaf shape to a lily-of-the-valley leaf. When fried with potatoes, the ramp leaves wilt down to a dark, glossy green and the white bulbs soften and turn translucent, distinct from a raw ramp's crisp, matte appearance. A heavy annual harvest has driven real population concerns; the Great Smoky Mountains National Park banned ramp harvesting outright in 2002 after studies showed a ramp population needs many years to recover from a single harvest. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the botanical description and the 2002 GSMNP ban]
- Common confusion: Should not be rendered as a scallion/green onion (thinner, uniformly tubular leaves) or a standard cultivated leek (much larger, paler, less pungent, cultivated rather than foraged).
- Confidence: HIGH for the botanical/visual description and the festival culture; MEDIUM for the specific Cherokee-origin framing.
- Sources: [Farmers' Almanac — What Are Ramps?](https://www.farmersalmanac.com/what-the-heck-are-ramps); [Practical Self Reliance — Ramps (Wild Leeks)](https://practicalselfreliance.com/ramps-wild-leeks/); [The Real WV — Ramp season returns](https://therealwv.com/2026/04/13/the-front-porch-its-ramp-season/); [Richwood Chamber of Commerce — Feast of the Ramson](https://www.richwoodchamberofcommerce.org/feast-of-the-ramson)

### Dish: Pepperoni roll
- Category: Everyday, strongly on-the-go/snack-coded
- Cuisine lineage: Italian-American, specifically born of Italian immigrant coal-mining communities in north-central West Virginia in the early 20th century — a genuine, dated, single-region American invention on the same order as the Philly cheesesteak (`us-mid-atlantic.md`) or the NYC bagel-and-lox (`us-northeast.md`), not an import from Italy itself.
- Regional form variation: None — this dish is West Virginia/Appalachia-specific by definition and origin; it has since spread in availability but the dish's identity remains anchored here. Widely promoted (though not formally, legislatively adopted) as West Virginia's state food — a 2013 state House resolution was tabled, and a second 2021 House resolution passed the House but stalled in the state Senate, so it remains an "unofficial" rather than legally official designation, even though the state's own tourism bureau calls it the state food. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Serving format: Handheld snack/on-the-go item — no plated sibling.
- Primary composition: Yeast bread dough rolled around whole or sliced pepperoni sticks and baked; **the traditional/original form contains no cheese and no sauce** — just dough and pepperoni. Cheese, hot peppers, and sauce were later additions credited to experimentation by a specific baker building on the original commercial recipe, and are common in modern versions but are not the historical baseline. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Origin/historical anchor: Commercially popularized around 1927 by Giuseppe "Joseph" Argiro, an Italian immigrant miner from Calabria, at his Country Club Bakery in Fairmont, West Virginia — though it's widely acknowledged coal miners' wives were likely making a home-kitchen version even earlier. Argiro recalled miners eating a stick of pepperoni in one hand and bread in the other, and combined the two into one portable, shelf-stable food that let a miner eat one-handed while still able to drink water or keep working with the other. Pepperoni rolls do not require refrigeration, making them ideal for packing into a mine or a school lunch. Fairmont, WV has branded itself "the pepperoni roll capital of the world." **Prompt-writing note**: Country Club Bakery is a specific, real, still-operating business — cite for historical context, describe a generic small-town bakery or convenience-store bakery case in an actual image prompt, not this specific storefront. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Serving vessel: Sold from a bakery case, convenience-store/gas-station food case, or grocery bakery section; eaten from a simple paper bag or napkin, not plated.
- Visual/plating characteristics: A **pinwheel-shaped cross-section** when cut, from the dough being rolled up around the pepperoni before baking — visible spiral rings of dough around a central pepperoni core, or, in a simpler log-shaped version, pepperoni pieces embedded directly through a plain bread log. The crust is a smooth, evenly **glossy golden-brown** (from an egg wash), notably shinier than a craggy, matte cornbread crust — a genuinely different bread finish from this file's other baked goods. Traditional versions show no melted cheese in the cross-section (dense, plain bread crumb around the pepperoni); the dough surrounding the pepperoni pieces takes on a light orange-red tint and small grease-pooled specks where rendered pepperoni fat has soaked in during baking — this orange staining is a genuine, checkable authenticity detail, not a flaw.
- Common confusion: Should not be rendered as a stromboli or a cheese-pull-heavy pizza roll (both cheese-forward with a visible melted-cheese pull) unless the brief specifically calls for a modern cheese-added variant — the traditional form is plain bread and pepperoni only.
- Confidence: HIGH overall.
- Sources: [e-WV: The West Virginia Encyclopedia — Pepperoni Rolls](https://www.wvencyclopedia.org/entries/1778); [WDTV — House passes bill declaring Pepperoni Roll official food of West Virginia](https://www.wdtv.com/2021/04/06/house-passes-bill-declaring-pepperoni-roll-official-food-of-west-virginia/)

### Dish: Fried apple pie (fried pie / half-moon pie)
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (a farmhouse/homestead tradition, also sold at roadside stands and church suppers)
- Cuisine lineage: A farm/homestead tradition across Appalachia, the broader South (where it may be called an "applejack"), and the Midwest — settlers of Scottish, Irish, and German descent brought pastry-pie traditions that adapted to locally available dried fruit; this file treats it as prevalence-strong in Appalachia specifically (tied to hearthside, pre-electric-oven cast-iron-skillet cooking) rather than exclusively Appalachian in origin. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- Regional form variation: Prevalence-only — fried pies exist elsewhere in the South and Midwest under other names; the dried-apple, cast-iron-skillet-fried, half-moon-shaped version is the form most consistently tied to Appalachian hearthside cooking specifically, from the era before ovens were common in mountain homes.
- Serving format: Handheld, half-moon-shaped hand pie — no plated-slice sibling documented.
- Primary composition: A simple, sturdy pastry dough (not a laminated, flaky pie-crust style) folded over a filling and crimped shut, then fried (traditionally in lard) in a cast-iron skillet rather than baked.
- Primary filling: Rehydrated dried apples (sometimes dried peaches), cooked down with sugar and spice before filling — using dried rather than fresh fruit is treated as central to the traditional version's texture, not an equivalent substitution.
- Visual/plating characteristics: A half-moon shape (a circle of dough folded in half over the filling), sealed at the curved edge with a **visible fork-crimped or hand-pinched pattern** — this crimped edge is a checkable, specific detail, not a smooth-sealed edge. The fried crust shows small raised **blisters** and a genuinely **crisp, firm texture** at the edges from frying in fat, distinct from a softer, flakier baked hand pie; color is an even, deep golden-brown all over. The dried-apple filling, visible if the pie is shown cut open, is a darker, more concentrated **amber-brown, jam-like, cooked-down mass** — not the pale, wet, visibly-chunky look of a fresh-apple pie filling.
- Common confusion: Should not be rendered as a baked (not fried) hand pie — the blistered, crisp-fried crust texture and the darker, jammier dried-apple filling are the two checkable differences.
- Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH for the visual/texture description; MEDIUM for how cleanly Appalachian-exclusive this dish is versus the broader South/Midwest tradition it shares.
- Sources: [Due South — Discovering The Delicious Roots Of Fried Pies In Appalachia](https://duesouth.media/history-of-fried-pies-in-the-south/); [Blind Pig and The Acorn — Fried Apple Pies](https://blindpigandtheacorn.com/fried-apple-pies/)

### Dish: Apple stack cake
- Category: Special-occasion (traditionally a wedding cake and holiday dessert, not an everyday item) — included per the same "staging relevance over raw frequency" logic `us.md` uses for Thanksgiving dinner
- Cuisine lineage: Native-American-tradition (a genuine Appalachian pioneer-era invention) — sources date it to at least the early 1800s, with several states claiming a role in its origin. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM for the specific dating/origin claim]
- Regional form variation: None identified — a single, consistent Appalachian tradition, distinct from a standard American frosted layer cake.
- Serving format: Plate, sliced from a large multi-layer stacked cake — a special-occasion dessert, not a snack.
- Primary composition: Traditionally, at least six and often eight or more very thin, dense, cookie-like or gingerbread-like spiced cake layers, stacked with a thick spiced apple filling (apple butter, applesauce, or rehydrated dried-apple preserves) spread between every layer.
- Historical/cultural anchor: At mountain weddings, tradition held that each guest or neighboring family would bake and bring one cake layer, and the bride's family supplied and spread the apple filling as layers arrived — the number of layers a bride received was said to reflect her popularity. One proposed origin account frames the stack cake as a local, low-cost substitute for an elaborately tiered, iced wedding cake that would have been prohibitively expensive for most mountain families. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — a specific, named, but singly-sourced folklore account]
- Visual/plating characteristics: **No icing or frosting at all** — the top layer is left bare, and the cake's rustic, visibly-layered structure is the intended presentation, not something to be hidden under a smooth iced exterior. Freshly baked, the individual layers are pale golden-brown, thin (closer to a thick cookie or biscuit than a fluffy cake layer), and notably dry/dense. After assembly, the cake is deliberately rested — often overnight or longer — so the moisture from the dark amber-brown spiced apple filling soaks into the dry cake layers; the finished, ready-to-serve cake should therefore look and read as moist, dense, and cohesive rather than dry, with the individual layers softened and darkened at their edges by the filling. A cut slice should show clearly countable, thin, distinct layers alternating with visible dark filling — a tall, many-layered cross-section is the dish's single most identifying visual trait, on the same "cross-section is the point" logic as this project's other tall layered/stacked dishes.
- Common confusion: Should not be rendered as a standard frosted American layer cake (icing, fewer/thicker layers, lighter sponge-like crumb) — the bare top, thin/dense layers, and dark visible filling between many distinct layers are the checkable differences.
- Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH for the visual/texture description and the wedding-tradition framing; MEDIUM for the exact historical origin dating.
- Sources: [Wikipedia: Stack cake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_cake); [Appalachian History — Dried Apple Stack Cake](https://www.appalachianhistory.net/2017/10/stack-cake.html); [The Kitchn — Apple Stack Cake Recipe](https://www.thekitchn.com/how-to-make-apple-stack-cake-240546)

### Dish: West Virginia hot dog (slaw dog)
- Category: Everyday
- Cuisine lineage: A genuine West Virginia-specific invention layered onto the national hot dog, credited to early-20th-century Greek and Italian immigrant vendors — a distinct, coexisting sibling to the other named regional hot dog styles `us.md`'s national index already documents (Chicago, Coney/Michigan, Sonoran, NYC cart dog), none of which this file duplicates.
- Regional form variation: **Form-changing relative to `us.md`'s national hot dog entry — a candidate to add there as an additional named regional style pointing here** (see Candidate Queue). West Virginia-specific; not documented as a form found in `us-south.md`'s or `us-mid-atlantic.md`'s scope.
- Serving format: Sandwich/handheld only.
- Primary protein: A standard grilled or steamed hot dog.
- Side dishes/condiments, and how served: Yellow mustard applied first (often to both cut sides of the bun), followed by finely diced onion, a smooth, finely ground (not chunky) beef-based chili sauce without beans, and finally a scoop of creamy coleslaw on top, in that order — all four toppings are standard, not optional add-ons, making this a fully "loaded," pre-dressed hot dog rather than a plain dog with condiments on the side.
- Historical/demographic context: Greek immigrants arrived in West Virginia in significant numbers around the turn of the 20th century (rising to over 3,000 by 1920, concentrated in Harrison, Hancock, and Ohio counties), and Greek- and Italian-owned hot dog stands proliferated through the 1910s–20s in the state's coalfield and Ohio River industrial towns, serving working-class miners and factory workers; one account also suggests the hot dog itself may have traveled south down the Ohio River from Cincinnati's meatpacking industry. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Visual/plating characteristics: The chili sauce is **smooth and finely ground**, closer in visual texture to a thin, spreadable meat sauce than to a chunky, bean-and-tomato-forward Texas- or Cincinnati-style chili — it should read as a thin, glossy, reddish-brown coating clinging to the dog, not a chunky topping with visible large pieces. The coleslaw sits as a distinct, separate, pale creamy-white mound on top of the chili layer, not mixed into it — the visual contrast between the chili's smooth reddish-brown and the slaw's pale, textured, shredded-cabbage look is the dish's defining plated signature. Diced onion appears as small, distinct white flecks, visible between the chili and slaw layers rather than on top.
- Common confusion: Should not be rendered as a Coney/Michigan-style dog (`us.md`'s national entry — beanless chili but no coleslaw, no onion-and-mustard-first layering) or a Chicago-style dog (celery salt, tomato, relish, sport peppers — no chili, no slaw at all). The coleslaw specifically is the single most reliable differentiator from every other regional hot dog style already documented in `us.md`.
- Confidence: HIGH for the composition and layering; MEDIUM-HIGH for the specific historical-origin narrative.
- Sources: [e-WV: The West Virginia Encyclopedia — Greeks](https://www.wvencyclopedia.org/entries/2097); [Southern Foodways Alliance — Slaw Abiding Citizens: A Quest for the West Virginia Hot Dog](https://www.southernfoodways.org/slaw-abiding-citizens-a-quest-for-the-west-virginia-hot-dog/)
