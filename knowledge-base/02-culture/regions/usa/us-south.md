## FILE ROLE & METHOD

This is the third regional file built under the 13-file US structure decided in `us.md`. It holds full, authoritative depth for the dishes `us.md`'s national index pointed here (Carolina barbecue, Nashville hot chicken, Memphis BBQ, classic Southern fried chicken), plus a large body of genuinely new dishes researched specifically for this file.

**Internal structure — three zones, one file**, decided by hand after all three research passes completed:

1. **Deep South** (Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi — inland core) — soul food, Alabama white-sauce BBQ, Georgia peach country, Mississippi Delta.
2. **Carolinas & Lowcountry** (North Carolina, South Carolina, and coastal Georgia) — inland Piedmont/Carolina barbecue, plus a labeled Lowcountry callout for the rice-and-seafood, Gullah Geechee-rooted coastal tradition.

This mirrors how Virginia was split between `us-mid-atlantic.md` (Tidewater) and this file (inland/Piedmont, not yet researched) — a real, evidenced geographic distinction, not a nominal state-border convenience.

Neither state is covered by this file. Logged in the Candidate Queue below.

## ZONE CHARACTERIZATIONS

### Deep South (Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi)

This zone clears the "would swapping norms into another Southern zone look visibly wrong" test on several independent grounds. Versus the Carolinas/Lowcountry: Lowcountry identity is rice- and seafood-forward and Gullah Geechee-influenced, while the inland Deep South core is pork-and-produce-forward, built on dry-land field crops (collards, field peas, corn) rather than a coastal rice/seafood economy — depicting an inland Alabama meat-and-three plate with Lowcountry rice-and-seafood norms, or vice versa, would be a visible error. Versus Tennessee: Nashville hot chicken is a heat-forward, cayenne-oil-crust variant already flagged in `us.md` as its own form-changing case, distinct from the plain, non-spicy buttermilk-fried chicken that is the Deep South's own default; Tennessee is also the documented origin point of the meat-and-three *term* itself, though the format is real and present across the Deep South too. Positive, GA/AL/MS-specific evidence anchors the zone further: Alabama white barbecue sauce is geographically anchored to northern Alabama (Decatur) and visually unlike any other BBQ sauce style already in this KB; Georgia's peach industry is tied to the state's specific climate and soil; the Mississippi Delta's hot-tamale tradition is a documented, geographically narrow food-culture pocket mapped by the Southern Foodways Alliance's own "Hot Tamale Trail."

**What this zone is not claiming**: Georgia, Alabama, and Mississippi are not food-culturally identical to each other — Georgia's peach industry and Atlanta's soul-food restaurant scene, Alabama's white-sauce BBQ belt (concentrated in the state's northern half), and Mississippi's Delta hot-tamale-and-blues culture are each their own sub-regional stories, grouped here because all three clear the distinctness bar against this file's other two zones and because soul food and the meat-and-three format apply consistently across all three at the depth researched. A future pass could reasonably split Georgia's peach country, the Alabama BBQ belt, and the Mississippi Delta into their own labeled sub-zone callouts within this zone — flagged as a real possibility, not attempted here.

**Caricature-avoidance note**: not every Deep South scene should default to a soul-food plate or a BBQ pit — ordinary suburban and urban Georgia/Alabama/Mississippi housing follows `us.md`'s national baseline just as often.

### Carolinas & Lowcountry (North Carolina, South Carolina, coastal Georgia)

The Carolinas' and Lowcountry's food culture is distinct from the Deep South on a real, named geographic/economic line, not a state-border convenience: the **Fall Line**, the geological boundary separating South Carolina's harder-rock Piedmont/interior from its softer coastal plain, is what let the Lowcountry become one of the great rice-growing regions of the 18th–19th-century world — an economic base the Deep South's cotton-and-inland-plantation economy did not share. That rice economy directly caused the region's distinct population history: enslaved West Africans were brought to the Lowcountry specifically for rice-farming expertise, and because Lowcountry plantations sat isolated on coastal/barrier islands rather than integrated into a denser interior population, the resulting Gullah Geechee community retained an unusually intact West African cultural and culinary throughline — the basis of a federally designated National Heritage Area (the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor, established by Congress in 2006, spanning coastal NC to northern Florida). Concretely: Lowcountry cuisine's defining triad — rice as the starch base, saltwater shellfish rather than the Deep South's greater emphasis on inland/freshwater catfish and flour-gravy dishes, and direct West African cooking techniques (one-pot rice dishes like red rice and perloo, paralleling jollof rice and thieboudienne) — pushes its identity in a genuinely different direction from Southern food generally. On barbecue specifically, the Carolinas are a sauce-driven wet-barbecue culture, in direct contrast to both neighboring styles this file documents elsewhere: Memphis serves ribs dry, with rub-built bark and no integral sauce; Alabama's defining sauce is a mayonnaise-based white sauce, a completely different base and color from the Carolinas' thin vinegar or mustard sauces.

**What this zone is not claiming**: North Carolina, South Carolina, and the Lowcountry are not food-culturally identical to each other — the Piedmont/inland Carolinas' barbecue-and-fried-chicken core and the coastal Lowcountry's rice-and-seafood, Gullah-influenced core are genuinely different registers within this zone, which is exactly why the Lowcountry is carried as a **labeled callout** rather than folded silently into one undifferentiated "Carolinas" description. What unifies them into one zone rather than three is that both halves clear the distinctness bar against the Deep South and Tennessee, and neither half is evidenced deeply enough on its own yet to need a fully separate regional file.

**Caricature-avoidance note**: ordinary suburban and urban housing (Charlotte, Columbia, Charleston's own denser in-town neighborhoods) following `us.md`'s national baseline remains equally real and common — the barbecue-joint and marsh-front registers below are differentiators to reach for when a brief calls for them, not a mandatory default.

### Tennessee (Nashville and Memphis)

This zone clears the distinctness test on food-identity grounds that are independently well-documented, not just a nominal state boundary: Nashville is synonymous with hot chicken, Memphis with dry-rub barbecue and rib culture — two of the most specific, non-interchangeable, nationally recognized single-dish identities in the Southern food landscape. Swapping them (staging a Memphis scene around hot chicken, or a Nashville scene around dry-rub ribs) would be a real, checkable error to anyone who knows either city. Beyond the two headline dishes, the cities trace to different causal roots: Nashville's hot chicken is layered onto Nashville's own **meat-and-three** cafeteria tradition, documented as originating specifically in the employee cafeteria of Nashville's May Hosiery Mill in the 1930s; Memphis barbecue traces to a Mississippi River port city's early-20th-century pitmaster culture (Beale Street's late-night rib stands from the 1920s onward, Leonard's Pit Barbecue from 1922, Rendezvous from 1948) layered onto Beale Street's blues-and-nightlife economy — a distinct settlement/economic history from Nashville's cafeteria-and-hosiery-mill origin.

**Internal structure decided here, per the Tennessee research pass's own flagged recommendation**: Nashville and Memphis are carried as **two labeled callouts within one Tennessee zone**, not split into separate files. The food-identity split between them is real and sharp — comparably sharp to the state's own West Tennessee/Middle Tennessee macro-regional distinction — but the relationship is "two anchor cities each carrying one dominant, non-overlapping signature dish" rather than a broad multi-county cultural region the way Lowcountry or Deep South are, so a same-zone, two-callout structure (mirroring the Lowcountry-within-Carolinas treatment above) is judged sufficient. This is flagged as a live question a future pass with more evidence could revisit, not treated as permanently closed.

**Caricature-avoidance note**: not every Nashville scene should default to a hot-chicken shack, and not every Memphis scene to a barbecue joint — Memphis has its own well-documented soul-food institutions and Nashville its broader meat-and-three tradition beyond hot chicken specifically, but for this file's staging purpose, the two headline dishes are the correct, best-supported anchors.

## TRUSTED CONTENT — ZONE-WIDE NORMS

### Deep South

- **Soul food and "Southern food" are related but genuinely distinct categories, and this distinction should not be flattened.** Soul food is specifically the culinary tradition of African Americans, rooted in West and Central African foodways carried across the slave trade and adapted under enslavement and later poverty/segregation, using ingredients that were local, plentiful, and affordable to African Americans (offal, greens, corn, field peas, pork scraps). "Southern food" is the broader regional cuisine, including white Southern cooking traditions — "all soul food is Southern food, not all Southern food is soul food" is a commonly cited formulation. The Southern Foodways Alliance frames a comparative distinction: soul cooking tends toward more capsicum-pepper heat, a heavier hand with salt and pepper, and greater use of offal than country/white Southern cooking. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Tasting Table — The Difference Between Southern Food And Soul Food](https://www.tastingtable.com/2089005/southern-vs-soul-food-difference/); [Afroculinaria — Southern or Soul?](https://afroculinaria.com/2016/08/11/southern-or-soul/)]
- **"Soul food" itself is a 1960s–70s civil-rights-era coinage**, not the tradition's origin point — the cuisine's home-cooking and church-dinner tradition long predates the name, which was applied retroactively as an expression of cultural pride. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [African American Registry — 'Soul Food' in America](https://aaregistry.org/story/soul-food-a-brief-history/)]
- **Staging implication**: a soul food scene's authenticity rests as much on occasion and context (Sunday dinner, a church potluck, a family gathering) as on the specific dish plated — do not treat it as interchangeable with generic "Southern comfort food."
- **Alabama white barbecue sauce is a genuinely distinct, geographically anchored condiment, not a novelty.** Mayonnaise-based, not tomato-based, invented in 1925 by Bob Gibson at his barbecue pit in Decatur, northern Alabama (now Big Bob Gibson Bar-B-Q); combines mayonnaise, apple cider vinegar, lemon juice, black pepper, and salt, developed for chicken specifically — the mayonnaise's fat keeps smoked white meat from drying out over hours of hickory smoke. Historically confined to northern Alabama, only becoming widely commercially available in 1994. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Encyclopedia of Alabama — Big Bob Gibson's White Sauce](https://encyclopediaofalabama.org/media/big-bob-gibsons-white-sauce/)]
  - **Visual identity**: opaque, creamy, pourable, matte-sheened — the visual opposite of every red/brown BBQ sauce style elsewhere in this KB. Used two real, coexisting ways: **dunked/dipped** (chicken pieces dunked directly into a bowl or bucket off the smoker, a thick, even, off-white coating pooling in skin creases — the default absent other direction, since it's Big Bob Gibson's own signature style) or **drizzled/basted** (a thinner application leaving streaks). The chicken underneath is smoked, not fried, so skin shows matte, smoke-darkened golden-tan where sauce is thin or absent, not a fried craggy crust. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
  - **Common confusion**: never a red, brown, or amber BBQ sauce — the complete absence of tomato and the creamy-white color/texture is the single most checkable marker.

### Carolinas & Lowcountry

- **Barbecue in the Carolinas is fundamentally a whole-hog and pork-shoulder culture, not a beef culture** — the opposite emphasis from Texas. South Carolina's Pee Dee region is "universally" whole-hog, cooked skin-side-up 12+ hours before being flipped, sauced, and hand-pulled with lean and fatty meat mixed together; SC's Midlands and NC's Piedmont/Lexington tradition instead favor pork shoulder specifically. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Tasting Table — North Vs South Carolina Barbecue](https://www.tastingtable.com/1672632/north-versus-south-carolina-barbecue-differences/)]
- **South Carolina's mustard-sauce tradition has a specific, documented immigrant-settlement cause.** German immigrants, recruited and passage-paid by the colony starting in the 1730s–1750s, settled as independent farmers and brought a preference for mustard-cooked smoked meats, fusing over time with local whole-hog pit-cooking. The Bessinger family commercialized the sauce starting in 1939; the region is still nicknamed "the Mustard Belt." [CONFIDENCE: HIGH — already locked in `us.md`; this file adds settlement-history depth] [SOURCE: [Southern Kitchen — Carolina 'cue](https://www.southernkitchen.com/story/lifestyle/2021/08/10/whole-hog-guide-history-legacy-carolina-barbecue-sauces/5559222001/)]
- **North Carolina's own East/West split is a second axis, distinct from the NC/SC sauce split.** Eastern NC sauce is thin, clear, vinegar-and-red-pepper-flake, no tomato at all. Western/Piedmont/"Lexington-style" keeps the vinegar base but adds ketchup for body, color, and sweetness — credited to the same German-immigrant influence, which also favored pork shoulder over whole hog. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Our State — Why Are There Two Styles of NC Barbecue?](https://www.ourstate.com/nc-barbecue-styles/)]
- **Chopped vs. pulled is a real, checkable sub-regional preference.** Eastern NC is chopped fine (~¼-inch) to absorb the thin sauce; Lexington/Piedmont is chopped coarser (~1-inch) with a cleaver, "outside brown" meaning extra crisp bark mixed in. South Carolina, despite outsiders' "chopped" association, actually leans pulled (one single-source figure: ~70% of South Carolinians who eat pork barbecue prefer it pulled — [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM, not independently cross-verified]). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the NC chop-size distinction] [SOURCE: [Taste of Home — What Is North Carolina Barbecue?](https://www.tasteofhome.com/article/north-carolina-barbecue/)]
- **Rice is the structural starch of Lowcountry cuisine specifically**, in a way it is not for the Deep South generally — see Zone Characterization above. Multiple genuinely distinct rice-forward dishes (Hoppin' John, red rice, perloo/pilau) exist side by side, not one generic "rice on the side."
- **Gullah Geechee foodways are the specific, named cultural throughline for the Lowcountry callout**, not a generic "Southern coastal" gloss — the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor (Act of Congress, 2006) is the first U.S. National Heritage Area dedicated specifically to the African American experience. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [NPS — Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor](https://www.nps.gov/places/gullah-geechee-cultural-heritage-corridor.htm)]
- **"Southern," "Lowcountry," "Gullah," and "soul food" are related but not interchangeable terms** — Lowcountry denotes the coastal SC/GA rice-and-seafood tradition specifically; Gullah denotes the Gullah Geechee community's own foodways within it; "soul food" is a broader African American tradition not confined to the coast. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM] [SOURCE: [Discover South Carolina — Southern, Lowcountry, Gullah or Soul](https://discoversouthcarolina.com/articles/southern-lowcountry-gullah-or-soul-whats-the-difference-between-these-sc-cooking-styles)]

### Tennessee

#### Nashville
- **Meat-and-three is a real, historically specific Nashville institution**, not a generic Southern "plate lunch" that merely also exists there. The term traces to the 1930s employee cafeteria of Nashville's May Hosiery Mill, serving a choice of meat and vegetables plus bread for 25 cents; it now names a specific, still-common Nashville restaurant format (one protein from a rotating daily list, three vegetable sides, cafeteria/counter service). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Nashville Scene — The Past, Present and Future of the Nashville Meat-and-Three](https://www.nashvillescene.com/food_drink/features/the-past-present-and-future-of-the-nashville-meat-and-three/article_f03d33f2-4348-5a87-ade7-c13b0b305997.html)]
- **Hot chicken has gone from one Nashville family's dish to a national fast-food trend** — menu mentions rose roughly 65.7% between 2018 and 2023, and the format is now offered by KFC, Popeyes, Hardee's, Raising Cane's, Chick-fil-A, and the standalone chain Dave's Hot Chicken (started with $900 in 2017, ~$1B valuation by 2025). A "Nashville hot chicken" scene is now equally plausible as a small local shack or a modern fast-casual chain counter — a brief naming Nashville specifically should default to the former. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [CNBC — How Nashville Hot Chicken Became So Big](https://www.cnbc.com/2023/11/29/how-nashville-hot-chicken-became-so-big.html)]

#### Memphis
- **The dry-rub/wet-sauce divide is a genuine, actively debated civic distinction**, not a marketing gimmick — Memphis is described as home to a real civic argument between the two camps, with over 100 BBQ restaurants citywide. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM for the "100+/civic argument" framing; HIGH for the underlying dry/wet distinction, corroborated independently below]
- **Beale Street has a documented, specific history as Memphis's barbecue-and-nightlife corridor**, not just a modern tourist strip — dating to 1841, by day historically the center of Memphis's Black commercial district, by night the birthplace of Memphis blues. Late-night barbecue stands were a fixture from the 1920s onward, predating Rendezvous by roughly two decades. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [AmazingRibs — A History of Memphis Barbecue](https://amazingribs.com/a-history-of-memphis-barbecue/)]
- **Prompt-writing note**: today's Beale Street is a real, operating district with specific, named, trademarked businesses — describe the generic category (a historic multi-story brick commercial strip, string lights, blues-club-style signage kept unreadable) rather than naming or replicating any specific current business.

### Carolinas & Lowcountry

- **Sauce color/consistency is the single most checkable Carolina-barbecue authenticity marker, internally distinguished by sub-style.** Eastern NC: thin, fully clear/translucent vinegar liquid with visible red-pepper flecks, no color body at all. Western NC/Lexington: still thin, but a light reddish-orange-brown tint from ketchup — more colored than Eastern NC's clear sauce but far thinner than Kansas City's thick sauce (`us.md`). SC Midlands "Carolina Gold": a distinct bright yellow-gold, translucent-to-semi-opaque, pourable sauce — never a thick, opaque, mayonnaise-white sauce (that is Alabama's, above) nor a deep amber/brown mustard. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- **Bark/char texture differentiates Carolina style from Texas/Kansas City, and has its own internal split.** Whole-hog Pee Dee bark appears as small, dark reddish-brown to near-black crisp fragments mixed back into paler pulled pork — not a uniform dark crust like Texas brisket's all-over bark. Lexington/Piedmont "outside brown" shows the same dark fragments chopped into more uniform ~1-inch pieces. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- **Pulled vs. chopped is a real textural fork, not a menu-wording difference.** Pulled shows long, distinct, hand-shredded strands with visible muscle-fiber striation; Lexington-style chopped is a coarser, non-stringy mass of small irregular pieces; Eastern NC's fine chop is more uniform, mixing almost completely into its thin sauce. Rendering Eastern NC with long pulled strands would be a checkable error.
- **Shrimp and grits' finish is a defining detail, not just "shrimp on grits."** Grits should read glossy, smooth, loosely pourable/spoonable (stone-ground, slow-cooked) — a soft mound that slumps when disturbed, distinct from a stiffer, moldable grit-cake texture (a different, valid serving format). Shrimp should show a light golden-to-reddish sear, firm and opaque, pale pink-to-white interior — never translucent/raw or uniformly gray-pink/rubbery.
- **She-crab soup's pale pink-tan color, from crab roe, is its single most checkable differentiator** from a generic cream-of-crab soup — see the dish entry below.
- **Frogmore stew/Lowcountry boil is served as a communal, unplated pour-out**, not individually plated — a genuinely distinct convention from every other dish in this zone.

### Tennessee

- **Nashville hot chicken's crust color is the single most checkable, distinguishing visual marker versus mainstream fried chicken** — a genuinely reddish/maroon, not just "spicy-looking" surface. A detailed description of Prince's "Hot" tier describes the crust as "a deep, peppery maroon color," cayenne "caked into little bunches" so heavily that the outer layer is simultaneously moist (from the oil-carried paste) and dusty/granular in texture (from caked spice) — not a smooth glaze and not a dry, flaky breading alone. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Oxford American — Some Like It Extra Hot](https://oxfordamerican.org/magazine/issue-49-spring-2005/some-like-it-extra-hot)]
- **Memphis dry rub vs. wet sauce is a real, checkable visual fork, the same logic `us.md` applies to Texas-vs-Kansas-City sauce.** Dry-rubbed ribs show a matte, dark reddish-brown to almost blackened crusted "bark" with visible spice flecks and **no glossy sheen anywhere**. Wet ribs are brushed with a tomato-based sauce, producing a glossy, wet-looking, deep reddish-brown coating that visibly clings and drips. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: Memphis-style barbecue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memphis-style_barbecue)]
- **Memphis barbecue sauce, when it appears, is thinner and less sweet than Kansas City's** — less tomato paste, more vinegar, pouring and drip more readily rather than clinging in a thick, glossy coat. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- **Ribs are presented as a whole or half slab on a tray** — a genuinely different visual unit from a pulled-pork sandwich's shredded, bunless-until-assembled meat.

### Deep South

- **The meat-and-three cafeteria-style restaurant is a real, specific, well-documented Southern dining institution, not a generic "diner."** One main protein plus a choice of three side dishes, served cafeteria-style — a customer moves down a steam-table line and carries a loaded tray to a table, rather than ordering from a printed menu with table service. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] **Historically and etymologically rooted in Nashville, Tennessee specifically — see the Tennessee zone above for the fuller origin account (May Hosiery Mill, 1930s) and its full sourcing; this zone's own research corroborates rather than re-derives it.** The format itself is documented as present and common across the Deep South too (Birmingham and Atlanta are both named in the same urbanization-driven-origin story, workers needing a fast, affordable lunch on a factory schedule), even though its most famous named exemplars sit in Nashville, out of this zone's own scope — this zone's staging should use the format generically (a concrete-block or plain storefront building, a steam table, a tray line) rather than pointing to a specific named Tennessee restaurant. **Concrete staging detail**: stainless-steel steam-table wells holding a rotating selection of sides, kept warm under heat lamps; plastic or heavy diner-style trays; staff serving from behind the line; simple Formica tables, paper napkin dispensers, a sweet-tea station. A filled plate shows one protein (fried chicken, meatloaf, a smothered/braised meat) alongside three distinct sides (collard greens, black-eyed peas, mac and cheese, cornbread, mashed potatoes, fried green tomatoes) in separate compartments, not intermingled. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM for the concrete staging synthesis]
- **Front porch dining/socializing is a genuine, historically documented Southern social space**, not a caricature. #### Dish: Southern fried chicken — plated (full zone entry — supersedes `us.md`'s compact index entry for the classic, non-Nashville-hot version)
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (a Sunday-dinner and celebration centerpiece as much as an everyday dish)
- Cuisine lineage: A genuinely layered lineage, not single-origin. Frying chicken in fat was brought to the American South by Scottish immigrants, who fried it plain and unseasoned; enslaved West African cooks, whose own traditions already included frying chicken (in palm oil, for special occasions) and seasoning it heavily, combined and transformed the technique — the heavily seasoned, well-crusted fried chicken now recognized as "Southern" is credited to this African American culinary synthesis, not the European technique alone. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — a consistent, recurring narrative, but journalism/enthusiast-tier sourcing, not academic]
- Regional form variation: This entry documents the base style Nashville hot chicken (Tennessee zone) is itself a derivative *of*; no further Deep-South-specific form-changing variant beyond the technique below was found — an absence-of-evidence finding, not confirmed uniformity.
- Serving format: Plate (bone-in pieces) — sibling entry to `us.md`'s Fried chicken sandwich, sharing a protein tradition but a different visual/serving context.
- **Primary technique — pan-fried (shallow-fried), not deep-fried, and worth stating explicitly for photorealism.** Buttermilk-brined, dredged in seasoned flour, fried in a cast-iron skillet in oil reaching only about halfway up the sides of the pieces — roughly three-quarters of each piece sits above the oil line. Fried skin-side-down first until pale golden, turned (sometimes more than once), finished over lower heat, often covered, until cooked through. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Southern Kitchen — Southern cast-iron fried chicken](https://www.southernkitchen.com/story/recipes/2021/07/22/southern-cast-iron-fried-chicken/8054404002/)]
- The cast-iron skillet itself is a documented, still-current tradition, often an heirloom, specifically preferred because it holds and distributes heat evenly at the lower oil volume the technique requires. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Primary protein: Bone-in chicken pieces (breast, thigh, drumstick, wing), buttermilk-brined
- Side dishes/condiments: Sides drawn from this zone's own catalog — collard greens, black-eyed peas, mashed potatoes and gravy, biscuits, cornbread — brought to the table separately, not pre-plated fast-food-style.
- Serving vessel: **Family-style on a large platter for the whole table, historically the standard for Sunday dinner** — distinct from an individually plated restaurant presentation; a meat-and-three plates individual portions instead. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Venue variation: Home Sunday dinner (family-style platter); meat-and-three cafeteria (individually plated, three sides, steam-table service); church potluck/dinner-on-the-grounds (disposable foil pans or large platters, potluck-style). All three authentic, non-hierarchical.
- Utensils/eating customs: Eaten by hand at a casual home meal; fork and knife equally acceptable at a formal table or restaurant — not a strict hand-only norm.
- **Visual/plating characteristics (texture/finish explicit)**: A **thick, craggy, irregularly-textured crust** — small raised, shaggy ridges and nubs from firmly pressed flour dredge, not a thin, smooth, uniform batter shell. Color **deep golden-brown but not uniformly even** — the hands-on shallow-fry method produces visible piece-to-piece and even within-piece color variation (a browner side where it sat longest against the pan, a paler patch elsewhere) — a genuine, correct signature, not a flaw. Crust matte and dry-crisp, never glossy or wet. Cut into or bitten, meat is opaque white (breast) or slightly darker grayish-brown (thigh/leg), fully cooked through, never pink. Skin visible and adherent, not separated or missing. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Common confusion: Distinct from a fast-food/sandwich-format cutlet (`us.md`), which uses a thinner, more uniform crust; distinct from Nashville hot chicken (Tennessee zone), which adds a cayenne-oil paste after frying, giving a reddish-orange sheen this plain version never shows.
- Confidence: HIGH for pan-fry technique and texture; MEDIUM for the layered origin narrative and family-style platter norm.
- Sources: [Crimson Coward — The Fiery History of Southern Fried Chicken](https://www.crimsoncoward.com/blog/indulging-in-a-legacy-the-fiery-history-of-southern-fried-chicken/); [Southern Kitchen](https://www.southernkitchen.com/story/recipes/2021/07/22/southern-cast-iron-fried-chicken/8054404002/); [The Seasoned Mom — Crispy Fried Chicken Recipe](https://www.theseasonedmom.com/crispy-fried-chicken-recipe/)

#### Dish: Collard greens
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (Sunday dinner, holiday, and church-gathering staple as much as an everyday side)
- Cuisine lineage: Soul food/African American-tradition specifically. Collard greens are Mediterranean/European in botanical origin, but enslaved Africans applied African culinary technique and flavor logic — smoked/preserved pork (originally scraps allotted to enslaved cooks: ham hocks, neck bones) to season and slow-cook the greens into a staple appearing at Sunday dinners, holidays, and gatherings across generations. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — a consistent, repeated narrative, recipe-blog-tier sourcing]
- Regional form variation: None documented — a nationally-recognized soul food dish with its deepest cultural anchor in the South generally.
- Serving format: Side dish, bowl or scoop on a plate — no handheld sibling.

- Side dishes/condiments: A splash of vinegar-based hot sauce added at the table; a wedge of cornbread to soak up the pot likker (the cooking liquid) — a common, near-inseparable pairing.

A modest pool of thin, translucent, brownish-green pot likker at the bowl's bottom, with small visible pork pieces mixed through. Matte and dark throughout, not glossy or vibrant — a deliberate, correct signature of the slow-cooked method, not a sign of being overcooked. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Common confusion: Not a bright, crisp, lightly-sautéed or blanched leafy green (spinach, Swiss chard) — the long-cooked, dark, soft, pot-likker-bearing presentation is the checkable marker.
- Confidence: HIGH for the finished dish's visual/textural description; MEDIUM for the origin narrative.

#### Dish: Southern skillet cornbread
- Category: Everyday — a near-universal bread accompaniment to a soul food or Southern meal
- Cuisine lineage: Layered — Indigenous corn-and-bean cake traditions predating European settlement, evolving through colonial-frontier ash cakes, pones, and hoecakes into the modern skillet-baked loaf. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]

[CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Food Republic — If You Put Sugar In Your Cornbread](https://www.foodrepublic.com/1341731/no-sugar-traditional-savory-southern-cornbread/)]

No visible glaze or sweet sheen anywhere. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]

- Confidence: HIGH for the sugar/texture North-South divide; MEDIUM for the deep-history origin narrative.

James Hemings, an enslaved chef trained in France while enslaved by Thomas Jefferson, is widely credited with an early American baked-macaroni dish ("macaroni pie"); after gaining freedom, the tradition is described as spreading through African American communities into a celebratory Sunday-dinner and holiday staple, traveling with the Great Migration (roughly 1910–1970). #### Dish: Mississippi Delta hot tamales (bonus entry — strong, well-sourced, genuinely iconic Deep South dish beyond the assigned list)
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (a genuine regional food-tourism destination dish, mapped by a dedicated "Hot Tamale Trail")
- Cuisine lineage: **A genuinely cross-cultural, contested-origin dish, stated honestly rather than resolved.** The most-cited account traces it to the early 20th century, when Mexican migrant laborers came to the Delta for the cotton harvest; African American laborers working alongside them adopted and adapted the tamale using familiar ingredients (cornmeal, pork), producing a distinct Delta version. A competing, less commonly cited account traces the connection a century earlier, to the Mexican-American War. Neither is definitively resolved. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM] [SOURCE: [Southern Foodways Alliance — Hot Tamales & The Mississippi Delta](https://www.southernfoodways.org/interview/hot-tamales-the-mississippi-delta/)]
- **Cultural documentation**: the Southern Foodways Alliance mapped a number of Delta tamale-making sites in 2005 into the Mississippi Delta Hot Tamale Trail, with accompanying oral-history interviews — a strong, specialist-institutional sourcing tier. The dish is also memorialized in blues music (Robert Johnson's 1936 "They're Red Hot"). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Regional form variation: **Form-changing and genuinely distinct from a Mexican or Tex-Mex tamale, not a lesser or diluted import** — Delta tamales are smaller, spicier, and simmered directly in their own well-seasoned, cayenne-and-paprika-forward liquid rather than steamed dry the way a traditional Mexican tamale typically is. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- Serving format: Handheld or fork-eaten, individually wrapped, typically sold in small multi-piece orders.
- Primary composition: A cornmeal-based masa (rather than fresh-corn masa wrapped in corn husk the traditional Mexican way) filled with well-seasoned, often spicy ground or shredded pork or beef, wrapped, and simmered in a spiced, reddish-orange cooking liquid.
- **Visual/plating characteristics (texture/finish explicit)**: A **narrow, cylindrical shape**, distinctly smaller/slimmer than a typical Mexican tamale, the **cornmeal masa taking on a reddish-orange tint** from the spiced simmering liquid — a real, checkable color difference from a traditional Mexican tamale's paler, more uniform pale-yellow masa. The corn-husk wrapper shows visible staining from the same liquid when unwrapped. Texture is **dense and slightly wet/moist** rather than dry and fluffy. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- Common confusion: Not identical to a traditional Mexican tamale — smaller size, reddish-tinted masa, and wet/simmered (not dry-steamed) texture are the checkable differentiators; a genuine, distinct regional dish per §4.6, not a diluted import.
- Confidence: HIGH for the dish's regional distinctness and SFA documentation; MEDIUM for the exact origin story and visual synthesis.

#### Dish: Carolina barbecue (pork) — full zone entry, expanding `us.md`'s existing HIGH-confidence index entry
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (a defining regional cuisine, central to community/church/political gatherings historically)
- Cuisine lineage: Native-American-tradition (Southern-rooted), with documented direct German-immigrant influence on South Carolina's mustard sauce and North Carolina's Piedmont/Lexington ketchup-inflected sauce.
- Regional form variation: **Form-changing**, with at least four internally distinct, coexisting sub-styles, none subordinate to the others (per `country-file-schema.md` §4.6):
  1. **Eastern North Carolina**: whole hog or a mix of cuts, chopped fine (~¼-inch), dressed with a thin, clear, peppery vinegar sauce, no tomato.
  2. **Western North Carolina/Lexington/Piedmont-style**: pork shoulder specifically, chopped coarser (~1-inch) with a cleaver, dressed with a vinegar-and-ketchup sauce; "outside brown" is a real, orderable request for extra crisp bark mixed in.
  3. **South Carolina Midlands ("Carolina Gold")**: pork, most often pulled rather than chopped, dressed with the state's signature bright yellow, mustard-based sauce.
  4. **South Carolina Pee Dee**: whole-hog, near-universally, cooked skin-side-up 12+ hours, flipped and mopped with a thin vinegar-salt-pepper sauce late in cooking, hand-pulled with lean and fatty cuts mixed together.
  [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for all four sub-styles' existence and core description]
- Serving format: Plate/platter (the dominant format), with pulled- or chopped-pork sandwiches (on a plain soft white bun) a common secondary handheld format across all four sub-styles.
- Side dishes/condiments: Coleslaw is a near-universal side, often mustard- or vinegar-dressed rather than mayonnaise-heavy in the Carolinas specifically [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]; hush puppies are also common. South Carolina barbecue hash (below) functions as an additional starch-and-protein side specifically in that state.
- **Visual/plating characteristics — texture/finish, checked explicitly per §4.5**:
  - **Eastern NC**: finely chopped, almost uniformly textured, pale pinkish-tan to light brown, thoroughly saturated and slightly glistening from thin, fully clear/translucent vinegar sauce — no thick coating, no gloss beyond a light wet sheen.
  - **Lexington/Piedmont**: coarser ~1-inch chunks with prominent dark-brown bark fragments (especially "outside brown" orders); sauce a light reddish-orange-brown wash, not a thick coating.
  - **SC Midlands/Carolina Gold**: typically pulled (long, visible shredded strands with muscle-fiber striation), coated in a thin, translucent, bright golden-yellow sauce — mustard-yellow, not cream-colored, not deep amber-brown.
  - **SC Pee Dee whole-hog**: pulled, mixed lean-and-fatty texture with visible small crisp dark-brown bark fragments distributed through paler pulled meat (not a continuous outer crust the way intact Texas brisket shows), lightly coated in thin, clear vinegar-pepper sauce.
  - Across all four: none should show KC-style thick, dark red-brown, glossy clinging sauce, or Alabama's opaque mayonnaise-white sauce.
- Common confusion: Most easily confused with Memphis-style barbecue (Tennessee zone) — Memphis is traditionally dry, rub-built bark, no integral sauce, versus every Carolina sub-style's sauce-soaked or sauce-dressed finish. Also confusable with Alabama's white sauce (mayonnaise base) and Kansas City's thick tomato-molasses sauce (`us.md`).
- Confidence: HIGH overall for the four-sub-style structure and sauce-color/texture distinctions; MEDIUM for the coleslaw-dressing-style claim.
- Sources: [Southern Kitchen — Carolina 'cue](https://www.southernkitchen.com/story/lifestyle/2021/08/10/whole-hog-guide-history-legacy-carolina-barbecue-sauces/5559222001/); [Tasting Table — North Vs South Carolina Barbecue](https://www.tastingtable.com/1672632/north-versus-south-carolina-barbecue-differences/); [Our State — Why Are There Two Styles of NC Barbecue?](https://www.ourstate.com/nc-barbecue-styles/); [BBQ Hub — An Introduction to South Carolina Barbecue](https://www.bbqhub.net/features/An-Introduction-to-South-Carolina-Barbecue)

#### Dish: Shrimp and grits
- Category: Everyday (historically) to special-occasion/destination-dining (in its modern restaurant form)
- Cuisine lineage: A genuinely layered lineage, stated explicitly rather than resolved: grits trace to Native American (Muskogee) corn-grinding technique; the shrimp-and-grits combination specifically is credited to the Gullah Geechee community of the SC Lowcountry, with a plausible (not definitively documented) deeper West/East African root. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM for the deeper African-lineage claim specifically]
- Regional form variation: **Form-changing, stated honestly rather than papered over.** The version that became the modern national "Southern restaurant" default — cheese grits, bacon, mushrooms, scallions, a light pan sauce of chicken broth and lemon juice — was developed by chef Bill Neal at Crook's Corner in Chapel Hill, **North Carolina** (Piedmont, not Lowcountry), popularized nationally after a 1985 New York Times feature. Charleston/Lowcountry restaurant versions more commonly add tomato, tasso ham, shrimp stock, or a brown gravy — heavier and more tomato-forward. Both are genuine, real, currently-served versions; a brief wanting "Charleston/Lowcountry-style" should default to the tomato/tasso-inflected version, not Neal's bacon-and-lemon original. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]

Shrimp should show a **light golden-to-reddish sear/char** from high-heat pan-searing, firm and opaque, pale pink-to-white interior — not translucent/raw or uniformly gray-pink/rubbery. The Lowcountry-style sauce is a warm reddish-brown, visibly containing tomato and rendered pork fat, pooling around rather than submerging the grits; the Piedmont/Bill-Neal-style sauce is thinner, paler, more translucent, with visible scallion and mushroom flecks rather than tomato-red color.
- Common confusion: Not a generic shrimp scampi or shrimp pasta over rice or pasta instead of grits; not a stiff, sliceable grits "cake" (a different, valid, but distinct serving format).
- Confidence: HIGH for core composition and the Bill Neal/Chapel Hill vs. Charleston-style regional-form distinction; MEDIUM for the deeper African-origin claim.

#### Dish: She-crab soup
- Category: Special-occasion to everyday (an iconic Charleston/Lowcountry restaurant dish; also made at home)
- Cuisine lineage: A documented blend — food historians link it to partan bree, a Scottish crab-and-rice soup likely brought to Charleston by Scottish settlers in the early 19th century; the specific "she-crab" version with roe is a Charleston-specific 1920s development.
- History: Widely credited to William Deas, a Black chef and butler working for then-Charleston-mayor R. Goodwyn Rhett in the early 1900s, credited with adding female crab roe to an existing crab soup recipe while preparing to host President William Howard Taft — the roe addition changed the dish's color, texture, and name (only female ["she"] crabs carry roe). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Serving format: Plate/bowl (a first-course soup); no handheld sibling.
- Side dishes/condiments: A small amount of dry sherry, traditionally added tableside or just before serving.
- **Visual/plating characteristics — texture/finish, checked explicitly per §4.5**: A **smooth, velvety, creamy-textured soup with a distinctive pale pink-to-coral cast** — this pink tint, from the roe, is the single most checkable differentiator from a plain cream-of-crab soup or bisque (ivory/off-white, no pink cast). Thickened to a smooth, unctuous, pourable consistency — notably smoother and less chunky than New England clam chowder (`us-northeast.md`), though small visible chunks/flakes of white crab meat should still float in or just below the surface. **Note on current substitutions**: South Carolina law now heavily protects female blue crabs, so genuine roe is increasingly scarce; many modern restaurant versions omit it or substitute finely grated hard-boiled egg yolk to approximate the color — a real, current, sourced supply constraint. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH for the color/texture description; HIGH for the roe-scarcity point]
- Common confusion: Most easily confused with a generic cream-of-crab soup, New England clam chowder, or a full crab bisque — the pale pink (not ivory-white) color from roe and the smoother, non-chunky texture are the confirming details.
- Confidence: HIGH for origin story, core composition, and Charleston-specificity; MEDIUM-HIGH for the exact color/texture synthesis.
- Sources: [Discover South Carolina — A Taste of SC: Just What Is She-Crab Soup](https://discoversouthcarolina.com/articles/a-taste-of-sc-just-what-is-she-crab-soup)

#### Dish: Hoppin' John (full authoritative entry — kept here per the merge decision noted in FILE ROLE & METHOD above; see the Deep South zone's shorter cross-reference entry for that zone's own New Year's prevalence)
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (a specific New Year's Day good-luck tradition, also eaten year-round)
- Cuisine lineage: Gullah Geechee-rooted — enslaved West Africans brought both deep rice-cultivation knowledge and black-eyed peas (themselves of African origin) to the SC Lowcountry, and cooking the two together in one pot with smoked pork is a Gullah Geechee-kitchen development that later spread outward through the broader South. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- History: Documented in print as early as Sarah Rutledge's 1847 cookbook *The Carolina Housewife* (as "Carolina peas and rice" or similar), and noted by Frederick Law Olmsted in his 1861 travelogue of the antebellum South. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Serving format: Plate/bowl — a scooped portion of the combined rice-and-peas dish; no handheld sibling.
- Primary protein/starch: Black-eyed peas (or, in some Lowcountry versions, the closely related Sea Island red pea) cooked together with long-grain rice and smoked pork (ham hock, bacon, or fatback) in one pot.
- Side dishes/condiments: Traditionally served alongside collard greens (symbolizing paper money) and cornbread (symbolizing gold); each diner traditionally leaves three peas on the plate for luck, fortune, and romance. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH for the folk custom]
- **Visual/plating characteristics — texture/finish, checked explicitly per §4.5**: A visibly mixed, not layered, dish — tender, plump, matte-brown-and-cream black-eyed peas distributed evenly throughout slightly translucent, tender (not sticky-clumped or crunchy-undercooked) white or very pale rice, the whole dish taking on a light grayish-tan cast from the pork cooking liquid rather than staying bright white. Visible small flecks of smoked pork throughout. Moist and cohesive, grains holding together loosely rather than swimming in visible liquid or standing perfectly separate and dry.
- Common confusion: Not plain "rice and beans" more broadly associated with other US regional or Latin American cuisines — the smoked pork, black-eyed-pea (not black bean, pinto, or red bean) variety, and New Year's ritual context are the confirming details. Also distinct from its close Lowcountry cousins red rice (tomato-based, no peas) and perloo/pilau (a broader category, often including seafood).
- Confidence: HIGH for the Gullah Geechee origin, historical documentation, and New Year's tradition; MEDIUM-HIGH for the visual/texture synthesis.
- Sources: [Spicewalla — The History of Hoppin' John](https://www.spicewallabrand.com/blogs/recipes/spice-advice-a-history-of-hoppin-john); [Farmers' Almanac — Hoppin' John Recipe](https://www.farmersalmanac.com/hoppin-john-for-new-years-luck)

#### Dish: Charleston/Gullah red rice
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (a defining Lowcountry side dish and potluck/gathering staple)
- Cuisine lineage: Gullah Geechee-rooted, a documented close cousin of West African jollof rice and Senegalese thieboudienne, both tomato-based rice dishes. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]

#### Dish: Frogmore stew / Lowcountry boil
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (a communal, crowd-feeding dish — genuinely served at scale for large community gatherings)
- Cuisine lineage: A documented blend — the specific "Frogmore Stew" name and its now-standard shrimp/sausage/corn/potato combination is credited to Richard Gay of the Gay Fish Company, from Frogmore on St. Helena Island, Beaufort County, SC, said to have improvised the dish from National Guard mess-hall leftovers sometime around the 1960s (accounts vary between "1948" and "1960s" — an unresolved date inconsistency). Separately, the underlying practice of a communal one-pot seafood-and-vegetable boil is documented as part of Gullah Geechee community food tradition predating that specific named origin story. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH for the Richard Gay/naming story; MEDIUM for its relation to the older, broader Gullah communal-boil tradition]
- Regional form variation: **Prevalence/naming variant** — "Frogmore stew," "Beaufort stew," "Lowcountry boil," or simply "a boil" depending on location within SC/coastal GA; the dish itself does not meaningfully change form by name.
- Serving format: **A genuinely distinct communal serving format, not an individually plated dish** — the cooked contents are strained and dumped directly onto a newspaper- or butcher-paper-covered table for a group to eat by hand.
- Primary protein/starch: Shrimp, smoked sausage (sliced into rounds), corn on the cob (cut into short sections), and red or new potatoes, all boiled together in one large pot of heavily seasoned water (Old Bay or similar), shrimp added last given its short cooking time; blue crab a common optional addition.
- **Visual/plating characteristics — texture/finish, checked explicitly per §4.5**: A visually busy, high-contrast pile of distinct components: bright pink-orange, firm, opaque, curled shrimp (shell-on or shell-off, either authentic); matte, deep reddish-brown to dark red sliced sausage rounds with a slightly wrinkled, well-cooked skin; bright yellow corn sections, kernels plump and glossy, cut crosswise into short cylinders; pale golden-tan, matte-skinned whole small potatoes, tender with slight give but not falling apart. Everything glistening/wet from the boiling liquid, pieces sitting loosely piled rather than arranged in rows.
- Common confusion: Not a New England clam bake/boil (a different ingredient set — clams, lobster, corn, potatoes, no sausage — already implicit in `us-northeast.md`) or a Cajun crawfish boil (Louisiana, outside this file's scope, centered on crawfish and typically far spicier). The shrimp-sausage-corn-potato combination and newspaper-table presentation are the confirming details.
- Confidence: HIGH for core composition and communal serving format; MEDIUM for the exact founding date and its relation to older Gullah communal-boil practice.
- Sources: [Explore Beaufort SC — Frogmore Stew: A local Lowcountry tradition](https://explorebeaufortsc.com/frogmore-stew-a-local-lowcountry-tradition/); [Mashed — The History Of South Carolina's Iconic Frogmore Stew](https://www.mashed.com/1732967/frogmore-stew-history-south-carolina/)

##### Dish: Memphis BBQ ribs — dry (full zone entry — authoritative depth for `us.md`'s national BBQ platter index entry's Memphis pointer)
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion/destination-dining
- Cuisine lineage: Southern-American-tradition, Memphis-specific, with a documented mid-20th-century commercial-refinement history distinct from the dish's older, more diffuse Southern pork-barbecue roots.
- Origin/history: Ribs were a comparatively overlooked cut in Memphis barbecue until Charlie Vergos began serving charcoal-cooked, dry-seasoned ribs at what became Charlie Vergos' Rendezvous starting in 1948 (the repeated founding anecdote: Vergos converted an old basement coal chute into a charcoal pit) — widely credited with popularizing and commercially defining Memphis-style dry-rub ribs, though not the literal first Memphis rib vendor (Beale Street rib stands, including John Mills's, are documented roughly two decades earlier). Rendezvous ribs are charcoal-broiled directly (not smoked low-and-slow) for a comparatively short cook time (one cited figure: ~75 minutes) — a real, sourced technique distinction, though not confirmed as generalizing to all Memphis dry-rub ribs citywide. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH for the Rendezvous origin/technique; MEDIUM for how far "charcoal-broiled, not smoked" generalizes beyond this one restaurant] [SOURCE: [AmazingRibs — Rendezvous-Style Memphis Rub Recipe](https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/spice-rubs-and-pastes/rendezvous-style-memphis-rub-recipe/)]
- Regional form variation: This *is* the "dry" half of the Memphis-specific "dry or wet" fork `us.md`'s national BBQ entry already flags at HIGH confidence; the wet version is a separate, genuinely coexisting sibling — see below, not a lesser option.
- Serving format: Plate/platter — a whole or half rack, not a sandwich; pulled pork (below) is the format's sandwich-format sibling.
- Primary protein: Pork spare ribs or St. Louis-cut ribs, dry-rubbed with a spice blend before and/or after cooking (paprika and garlic are commonly cited base components; the specific "up to 40 seasonings" claim traces only to a single lifestyle-blog-tier source and should not be treated as fact — [CONFIDENCE: LOW]).
- Side dishes/condiments: A thin, tangy, tomato-vinegar sauce is typically offered on the side as a dip, not applied to the meat — eating it with no sauce at all is also fully authentic and common.
- **Visual/plating characteristics — texture/finish specifically checked per §4.5**: A dark reddish-brown to near-black, matte, crusted "bark" exterior — visibly dry and crumbly-crisp rather than smooth or wet, with individual spice flecks discernible on close view. **No glossy sheen anywhere on the surface** — the single most important, checkable negative space; any visible wet gloss would misrepresent it as the wet style. The meat itself, where visible at a cut edge or pulled from the bone, should read well-done and fully cooked through (no pink), with a distinct pinkish "smoke ring" just under the surface bark being an authentic, expected detail (a technical color effect from combustion gases, not undercooking).
- Common confusion: Not Texas brisket bark (also dark and crusted, but on a large flat beef cut, not a bone-in rib rack); not a wet/sauced Memphis or Kansas City rack (the glossy sauce sheen, entirely absent here, is the difference).
- Confidence: HIGH for the dry/no-sauce identity and general bark appearance; MEDIUM for specific technique/origin claims as flagged.
- Sources: [Wikipedia: Memphis-style barbecue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memphis-style_barbecue); [Taste of Home — What Is Memphis-Style Barbecue?](https://www.tasteofhome.com/article/memphis-barbecue/); [AmazingRibs — A History of Memphis Barbecue](https://amazingribs.com/a-history-of-memphis-barbecue/)

##### Dish: Memphis-style pulled pork sandwich (full zone entry — the sandwich-format sibling within Memphis BBQ, pulling forward `us.md`'s existing "pulled pork sandwiches as the dominant menu item" note)
- Category: Everyday — the most commonly ordered Memphis BBQ item per `us.md`'s existing sourcing
- Cuisine lineage: Southern-American-tradition, with a specific, well-documented Memphis originator. Leonard Heuberger, who founded Leonard's Pit Barbecue in Memphis in 1922 as a lunch counter, is credited as inventor of the specific "classic Memphis pork barbecue sandwich" format: chopped or pulled pork shoulder on a bun, topped with tomato-based sauce and coleslaw — a period source reports "the standard has not changed in more than 60 years." [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Serving format: Sandwich/handheld — the genuine sibling to the plated-ribs formats above, same core pork/sauce/rub tradition, completely different visual presentation.
- Primary protein: Pork shoulder, smoked/cooked low and slow, then hand-pulled or hand-chopped into shreds (chopped is at least as traditional as pulled at the format's originating restaurants).
- Side dishes/condiments: **Coleslaw placed directly on top of the pulled pork, inside the sandwich — not served as a separate side dish** — the specific, well-documented Memphis convention: slaw on the bottom bun, pulled/chopped pork on top, a drizzle of barbecue sauce over the pork, then the top bun. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- **Visual/plating characteristics — texture/finish specifically checked per §4.5**: The pork shows visible strand/shred texture (pulled) or small irregular chunks (chopped) rather than a smooth ground-meat fill; exterior bark pieces mixed into the pull show the same dark reddish-brown, matte, spice-flecked surface as the standalone ribs entry, distinguishable from the paler pink interior meat where pulled apart. The coleslaw layer, visible at the cut edge or peeking from under the bun, reads pale, creamy white-to-ivory with fine visible cabbage shreds (mayonnaise-based Southern-style slaw is the default) — a strong color/texture contrast against the darker pork and reddish-orange sauce drizzle; the slaw's visibility at the sandwich's edge is itself a checkable authenticity marker.
- Common confusion: Not a plain pulled-pork sandwich with slaw served only as a side dish (legitimate in many other US regions/BBQ styles, but not the specific, documented Memphis assembly convention).
- Confidence: HIGH for the core protein/format and coleslaw-on-top convention; MEDIUM for the exact Leonard's origin specifics.
- Sources: [Wikipedia: Barbecue sandwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbecue_sandwich); [The Speckled Palate — Memphis-Style BBQ Pulled Pork Sandwiches](https://www.thespeckledpalate.com/memphis-style-bbq-pulled-pork-sandwiches/)

##### Dish: Memphis BBQ spaghetti
- Category: Everyday — a genuinely Memphis-original dish, not a generic Italian-American pasta dish with barbecue sauce added as an afterthought
- Cuisine lineage: A distinct Memphis fusion tradition (Southern BBQ technique/sauce applied to an Italian-American pasta base) — this dish has no meaningful claim to Italian lineage beyond the noodle itself.
- Origin: Credited to Brady Vincent, who left a job as a railroad cook to open a Memphis barbecue restaurant (Brady & Lil's) where he developed the dish; reportedly taught it to Frank and Hazel Vernon when they purchased the restaurant in 1980. Dedicated enough to the city's identity to have its own Wikipedia article. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM for the named-originator chain; the dish's Memphis-specificity is well corroborated]
- Primary composition: Memphis's tangy, thin, tomato-based barbecue sauce blended directly with a traditional Italian-American red sauce, then chopped or shredded smoked pork stirred through, and the spaghetti noodles tossed/stirred directly into the combined sauce so every strand is coated (rather than sauce ladled onto plain-boiled noodles).
- **Visual/plating characteristics — texture/finish specifically checked per §4.5**: The sauce reads as a distinctive **orange-red** (a genuinely different hue from a standard Italian-American marinara's brighter true-red) and should look glossy/wet-coated on the noodles, not dry or lightly oiled; small visible shreds of smoked pork distributed through the dish; noodles fully sauce-saturated and colored throughout, not pale with sauce pooled only at the bowl's bottom.
- Common confusion: Not spaghetti with barbecue sauce simply poured over otherwise-unchanged noodles — the noodles themselves take on the sauce's orange-red color throughout, and the sauce is a visibly more orange-toned blend, not a straightforward swap of marinara for barbecue sauce.
- Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH for the dish's existence, Memphis-specificity, and general composition; MEDIUM for the specific named-origin chain.
- Sources: [Wikipedia: Barbecue spaghetti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbecue_spaghetti); [AmazingRibs — BBQ Spaghetti: A True Memphis Original](https://amazingribs.com/memphis-style-bbq-spaghetti/)

##### Dish: Memphis BBQ nachos
- Category: Everyday/snack — a genuinely Memphis-associated BBQ-adjacent dish, distinct from generic Tex-Mex nachos
- Cuisine lineage: A Memphis BBQ-culture invention applied to a Tex-Mex-derived snack format — not a Tex-Mex dish with barbecue added, it reads and is documented as a Memphis barbecue-restaurant menu item first.
- Origin: One repeatedly cited account credits the Germantown Commissary (a Memphis-area barbecue restaurant) with originating the format in the 1980s, topping tortilla chips with melted cheese, smoked pork or chicken, and barbecue sauce; also cited as a staple concession item at Memphis Redbirds minor-league-baseball games, indicating spread beyond the originating restaurant. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- Primary composition: Tortilla chips, topped with melted cheese, smoked pulled pork (or chicken), barbecue sauce, and typically pickled or fresh jalapeños.
- **Visual/plating characteristics — texture/finish specifically checked per §4.5**: Chips show visible melted-cheese coverage, glossy and pooled/stretchy where thickest (a cheese-pull effect at a broken chip is plausible and authentic), visibly darker, matte-textured shredded/pulled pork pieces distributed across the top (retaining the same dark, spice-flecked bark-fragment appearance as the standalone pulled-pork entry, not a smooth or sauce-drenched uniform coating), a reddish-orange barbecue-sauce drizzle across the top (thinner, more Memphis-appropriate than a thick Tex-Mex queso), and bright green jalapeño slices as a sharp color accent. Chips at the bottom of the plate should look less cheese-coated than those on top — an authentic, uneven "nacho pile" distribution, not a uniformly and evenly sauced flat layer.
- Common confusion: Not standard Tex-Mex nachos (ground beef, sour cream, Tex-Mex-style yellow-cheese queso) — smoked pork and a thin, tangy barbecue sauce are the checkable differentiators.
- Confidence: MEDIUM — real and repeatedly documented as a genuine Memphis specialty, but resting on food-journalism/enthusiast-tier sourcing throughout.
- Sources: [Memphis BBQ Guide — Best BBQ Nachos in Memphis](https://memphisbbqguide.co/dish/bbq-nachos)
