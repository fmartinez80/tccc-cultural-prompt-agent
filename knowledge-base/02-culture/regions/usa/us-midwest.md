## FILE ROLE & METHOD

This is the seventh regional file built under the 13-file US structure. It holds full, authoritative depth for `us.md`'s existing index pointers to `us-midwest.md` (the Chicago-style hot dog and Coney dog within the national Hot dog entry; Chicago deep-dish and Detroit-style within the national Pizza entry; Kansas City within the national BBQ platter entry; St. Louis-style within the national Pizza entry; Cincinnati chili within the national Chili entry; Chicago Italian beef within the national city/region-specific sandwiches list) plus a large body of genuinely new dishes researched specifically for this file.

**Internal structure — three zones, one file**, decided by hand after all three research passes completed:

1. **Chicago & Great Lakes** — Illinois/Chicago, Michigan, Wisconsin, and Minnesota, unified by shared Great Lakes freshwater/dairy-belt geography and a rural German/Scandinavian plus urban industrial-immigrant settlement pattern found nowhere else in the Midwest at this concentration.
2. **Missouri & Kansas City** — Kansas City (which straddles the Missouri/Kansas state line) and St. Louis, roughly 250 miles apart, carrying two internally distinct food identities under one zone (see below).
3. **Ohio Valley** — Cincinnati/southwest Ohio and Indiana, plus one additional non-Cincinnati Ohio dish (the Cleveland Polish Boy) that cleared this file's staging-relevance bar independently.

**Cross-zone questions surfaced by multiple passes, resolved here rather than left open:**

1. **The "German Triangle" (Cincinnati, Milwaukee, St. Louis) is a real, shared, historically-documented German-immigrant-heritage pattern spanning all three of this file's zones — not a unique differentiator for any single one of them, and it is not used as one here.** Cincinnati's Over-the-Rhine, Milwaukee's brewing-and-tavern culture, and St. Louis's own German-American food layer (gooey butter cake, the pork steak) all trace to the same 19th-century German immigration wave, and a zone-distinctness argument built on "this zone has German heritage" alone would not clear this file's own "would swapping norms look visibly wrong" test. Each zone's actual distinctness argument therefore rests on other, non-shared grounds, stated in each Zone Characterization below: Cincinnati on the specific, dated 1922 Kiradjieff-brothers chili invention; the Chicago/Great Lakes zone on Great Lakes freshwater/dairy geography plus a specific rural Scandinavian-and-German settlement pattern (Wisconsin/Minnesota) layered with Chicago's and Detroit's own dated industrial-immigrant waves; Missouri/Kansas City on The Hill's specific, geographically-anchored Italian-immigrant settlement story and Kansas City's stockyard-and-rail economic history. The shared German-Triangle brewing/rowhouse heritage itself is documented once, Midwest-wide, in TRUSTED CONTENT below, rather than three times with each zone claiming exclusivity it doesn't have.
2. **Missouri and Kansas City genuinely splits into two distinct food identities under one zone, and rather than either flattening them into one undifferentiated zone or promoting St. Louis to its own separate regional file, this file gives the zone two labeled internal registers** — the same "emphasis, not a structural split" treatment `us-south.md` gives Lowcountry and `us-northeast.md` gives its three internal sub-zones. Kansas City barbecue and St. Louis's Italian-American/German-American food culture are documented as two coexisting, non-hierarchical registers within one Missouri & Kansas City zone: a Kansas City-set brief should default to the barbecue register, a St. Louis-set brief to the Italian-American/German-American register, and neither is the "default" for an unspecified Missouri brief.
3. **A real gap surfaced by this file's own research**: `us.md`'s existing national Pizza entry pointed `us-midwest.md` to only two Chicago pizza styles (deep-dish and Detroit-style), but this file's research found two additional, genuinely distinct, well-sourced, coexisting Chicago pizza registers — stuffed pizza and tavern-style pizza — with no prior `us.md` pointer. Per `country-file-schema.md` §4.6's coexisting-variants principle, both are given full entries here, and `us.md`'s national Pizza entry has been updated (see that file) to add pointers for both rather than leaving this file as an undiscoverable orphan of two real dishes.
4. **A naming trap worth flagging once, here, for the whole zone**: "Kansas City-style ribs" and "St. Louis-cut ribs" are both butchery cut designations (how a rack of pork spareribs is trimmed), unrelated to which city's overall barbecue or food-identity register a scene is set in — a KC-style-cut rack can appear on a St. Louis restaurant's menu and vice versa. This is preserved in full in the Kansas City BBQ platter dish entry below, not just noted here in passing.

**Scope inherited from `us.md`**: lunch, dinner, and snacks only (breakfast excluded); inclusion by staging relevance, not raw consumption frequency. Goetta is explicitly checked against the breakfast exclusion (see its entry) the same way `us-mid-atlantic.md` checks scrapple.

**Typical use note** (carried forward from the six prior regional files' own calibration): expect the realistic use case to skew toward general, archetypal scenes — a Chicago hot dog stand, a Kansas City barbecue joint, a Cincinnati chili parlor — more than hyper-local block-level staging.

## ZONE CHARACTERIZATIONS

### Chicago & Great Lakes (Illinois/Chicago, Michigan, Wisconsin, Minnesota)

**Does this zone clear the "would swapping this zone's norms into another Midwest zone look visibly wrong to someone who knows the place" test?** Yes, on multiple independent, causally-grounded lines of evidence, not just a shared "Midwest" label.

**1. Great Lakes freshwater geography is a real, shared, causally distinct resource base this zone's neighbors (Missouri/Kansas City, the Ohio Valley) don't have.** All four states border one or more of the Great Lakes (Michigan is bounded by four of the five) or, in Minnesota's/Wisconsin's case, sit within the same glacially-carved lake-and-dairy-land belt feeding into them. This produces a genuinely distinct food base found nowhere else in the Midwest at this concentration: freshwater whitefish, walleye, perch, and lake trout as an everyday protein (a real fishery in its own right, not a coastal-seafood substitute); a dairy-farming economy dense enough that Wisconsin brands itself "America's Dairyland" and supplies the specific cheese (brick cheese) that defines two of this zone's own signature pizzas. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the geographic/dairy-industry facts; MEDIUM-HIGH for the causal "this is why the cuisine differs" framing, this file's own synthesis rather than one source making the argument end-to-end]

**2. The zone's rural immigrant settlement pattern is specific and different in character from the rest of the Midwest, not just a smaller dose of the same waves.** German and Scandinavian immigrants disproportionately chose the rural Upper Midwest (Wisconsin, Minnesota, the Dakotas) over the era's more common urban-immigrant-enclave pattern, becoming a plurality or majority in most counties within decades; from 1840–1930 over 1.3 million Swedes emigrated to the US, concentrating specifically around the Great Lakes (the first permanent Swedish settlement, 1851, was in Chisago County, Minnesota). This is the direct causal root of Minnesota's Lutheran-potluck hotdish tradition and Wisconsin's dairy/brewing/supper-club culture below — a rural-Scandinavian-and-German settlement pattern with no equivalent concentration in Missouri/Kansas City or the Ohio Valley. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [WSU Libraries — German and Scandinavian Immigrants in the American Midwest](http://digitalexhibits.libraries.wsu.edu/exhibits/show/2016sphist417/immigration/germans-and-scandinavians); [Nordic immigration to North America — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordic_immigration_to_North_America)]

**3. Chicago and Detroit's industrial-immigrant histories are each specific, dated, and causally tied to this zone's own signature dishes, not generic "big city with immigrants" backstory.** Chicago's Union Stock Yards (opened 1865) grew into the world's largest livestock market, drawing waves of Italian, Polish, Lithuanian, Irish, and Greek immigrant labor plus, from 1916–1919, a 148% increase in Chicago's Black population during the early Great Migration — the direct population base behind both the Italian beef sandwich (invented by Italian immigrant stockyard workers making tough cuts of beef edible) and the South Side's Black-owned fried-chicken-and-rib-tip tradition that produced Chicago mild sauce. Detroit's auto industry drew a comparably specific, dated wave — from 1904 to 1925 Detroit's Polish population grew from roughly 13,000 to 115,000, concentrated around the Dodge Brothers' plant, while the same industrial pull later drew one of the largest Arab-American populations in the US (91% of the nation's Arabic speakers are Michigan-based today) — the reason Detroit, not Chicago or St. Louis or Cincinnati, is where the Greek-immigrant-founded Coney dog took root as a civic dish. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [WTTW Chicago — The Union Stockyards](https://www.wttw.com/chicago-stories/union-stockyards/the-union-stockyards-a-story-of-american-capitalism); [History of Polish Americans in Metro Detroit — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Polish_Americans_in_Metro_Detroit); [Michigan Daily — Detroit's culture and growth shaped by immigrant communities](https://www.michigandaily.com/uncategorized/detroits-immigration/)]

**4. Milwaukee's brewing industry is a specific, dated, German-immigrant-driven institution that directly explains a still-visible zone-wide social pattern (the tavern/supper-club) found nowhere else in the Midwest at this density — and, per this file's FILE ROLE & METHOD, this is the one place the shared "German Triangle" heritage is put to work as an actual differentiator, since it's the *institutional chain* (brewing → tavern-as-community-center → Prohibition-era roadhouse → supper club), not the German ancestry itself, that is zone-specific.** German immigration to Milwaukee from the 1840s onward produced over two dozen breweries by 1856 (Pabst, Schlitz, Blatz all Milwaukee-founded); these breweries and their taverns functioned as neighborhood community centers, the direct institutional ancestor of the Wisconsin supper club (see below). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the brewing history; MEDIUM-HIGH for the causal line to the modern supper club] [SOURCE: [History.com — How 19th-Century German Immigrants Revolutionized America's Beer Industry](https://www.history.com/articles/beer-history-german-immigrants-american-beer-industry-schlitz-pabst); [The Splendid Table — The History and Renaissance of the Wisconsin Supper Club](https://www.splendidtable.org/story/2016/08/04/the-history-and-renaissance-of-the-wisconsin-supper-club)]

**What this zone characterization is not claiming**: Illinois/Chicago, Michigan, Wisconsin, and Minnesota are not food-culturally identical — Chicago's identity is urban/industrial/immigrant-street-food-coded, Wisconsin's is rural/dairy/tavern-coded, Minnesota's is rural/Scandinavian-Lutheran-coded, and Michigan splits between Detroit's urban/auto/Middle-Eastern-and-Polish-coded identity and the Upper Peninsula's separate Cornish/Finnish-mining-coded identity (see the pasty entry below). No single one of this zone's signature dishes would plausibly relocate to another Midwest zone without a visible authenticity error, which is why they're grouped as one zone rather than four.

**Caricature-avoidance note**: avoid defaulting every scene in this zone to either a single downtown-Chicago skyline register as if the whole zone were one city, or a flat, featureless rural-farmland cliché as if nothing but corn and cows exists outside Chicago. Milwaukee, Minneapolis-St. Paul, and Detroit are themselves large, dense cities with their own genuine urban registers, and Wisconsin/Minnesota's rural and small-town registers (supper clubs, lake cabins, dairy country) are just as real and common as their metro areas.

**Michigan's own internal split, worth stating plainly**: Southeast Michigan (Detroit and its suburbs) is auto-industry-, Polish-, and Middle-Eastern-immigrant-coded; the Upper Peninsula is Cornish- and Finnish-mining-immigrant-coded, geographically and culturally closer to northern Wisconsin/Minnesota's mining-and-forestry belt than to Detroit.

### Missouri & Kansas City (Kansas City and St. Louis)

**Does this zone clear the same test?** Yes, on food-identity, settlement-history, and economic-history grounds simultaneously — not just a different city name for similar food.

**St. Louis's Italian-American identity is causally tied to a specific, named, still-intact neighborhood — "The Hill" — with its own distinct immigration story, not a diffuse citywide "Little Italy."** Italian immigrants, primarily from Lombardy and Sicily, began settling the neighborhood from the 1880s onward specifically to work its clay mines and brick factories (the neighborhood's literal topography — a slight rise over clay-rich ground — gives it its name); the two immigrant groups initially kept separate identities before developing a documented symbiotic relationship, and St. Ambrose parish (founded 1903) anchored the community. As of a 2003-era count, roughly three-quarters of the neighborhood's residents were still Italian-American. This is the direct causal root of toasted ravioli, St. Louis-style pizza, and the zone's Italian-American restaurant register below — it would look wrong staged as generic "American Italian" without this specific neighborhood anchor, and it would look wrong swapped into Chicago's own separate, not-interchangeable Italian-immigrant settlement story. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [STLPR — In 'The Hill,' A Third-Generation Author Explores St. Louis' Italian Bastion](https://www.stlpr.org/show/st-louis-on-the-air/2020-11-16/tuesday-in-the-hill-a-third-generation-author-explores-st-louis-italian-bastion); [theSTL — From bocce to red sauce](https://thestl.com/community/from-bocce-to-red-sauce-dig-into-the-enduring-legacy-of-st-louis-little-italy/); [Italian Genealogy — Little Italy, The Hill](https://italiangenealogy.blog/the-hill/)]

**Kansas City's barbecue identity is causally tied to the city's specific role as a national-scale livestock and rail hub, not just "the Midwest has cattle."** The Kansas City Stockyards (established 1871) grew to rank second only to Chicago among the nation's livestock markets; by the end of World War I the yards covered roughly 230 acres and adjoining packinghouses employed 20,000+ workers, handling a daily flow of 150,000–170,000 animals drawn from the Flint Hills of Kansas, Iowa hogs, and Missouri/Nebraska ranches. This industrial-scale meat supply is the direct economic substrate Henry Perry's barbecue trade (from 1908) grew out of, and explains causally why Kansas City barbecue has the **broadest range of smoked meats** of any major regional BBQ style (beef, pork, and poultry all commercially plentiful locally, unlike Texas's beef-cattle-country focus or the Carolinas'/Memphis's pork-focused hog-country economies). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [KC History/Missouri Valley Special Collections — Kansas City Cattle King](https://kchistory.org/blog/kansas-city-cattle-king-relics-stockyards); [The Pendergast Years — Making Meat: Race, Labor, and the Kansas City Stockyards](https://pendergastkc.org/articles/making-meat-race-labor-and-kansas-city-stockyards); [Delve Experiences — The Origins of Kansas City Barbecue](https://delveexperiences.com/explore-local-farms/kansas-city/history-of-kansas-city-barbecue)]

**This zone genuinely splits into two distinct food identities under one metro-and-state umbrella, and per this file's FILE ROLE & METHOD, that split gets two labeled internal registers rather than being flattened or promoted to a 14th regional file.** Kansas City (which physically straddles the Missouri/Kansas state line — Kansas City, MO and Kansas City, KS are two separate, adjoining cities) and St. Louis are roughly 250 miles apart, with barbecue as the dominant food identity in one and Italian-American/German-American food culture as the dominant identity in the other. A Kansas City-set brief should default to the barbecue register (see Dish Catalog); a St. Louis-set brief should default to the Italian-American/German-American register (pizza, toasted ravioli, gooey butter cake, pork steak) rather than barbecue, even though Missouri-wide barbecue culture exists in both cities to some degree. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the geographic/identity-split facts; the register-default framing is this file's own editorial rule built on that evidence]

**Contrast test, stated explicitly**: KC's sweet, tomato-and-molasses, thick-sauced barbecue would look wrong swapped into Ohio Valley content — Cincinnati chili's identity is rooted in early-20th-century Greek/Balkan immigrant cooks applying Mediterranean spice to a beef sauce over spaghetti, a completely different immigrant lineage and flavor logic. St. Louis's cracker-thin, Provel-topped, square-cut pizza would look wrong swapped into Chicago deep-dish content — the two are visually opposite (thin/crisp vs. tall/thick; Provel's fully-melted uniform layer vs. deep-dish's cheese-under-sauce construction using Wisconsin brick cheese) despite both being "Midwestern pizza."

**A secondary, less load-bearing but real settlement thread**: St. Louis also has a documented German-American food-culture layer distinct from its Italian-American layer — gooey butter cake's accidental invention is attributed to a south St. Louis German-American baker, and the city's grilled pork-steak tradition sits alongside, not instead of, the Hill's Italian-American register. This is the St. Louis-specific expression of the shared German Triangle heritage discussed in FILE ROLE & METHOD above, not claimed as unique to St. Louis. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — the German-American attribution for gooey butter cake is consistent across multiple sources but at journalism/enthusiast tier]

**What this zone characterization is not claiming**: this pass covers only two named cities within Missouri/Kansas; broader Missouri/Kansas small-town or rural architecture was not independently researched and should not be assumed covered.

### Ohio Valley (Southwest Ohio/Cincinnati and Indiana)

**Southwest Ohio / Cincinnati — does it clear the test?** Yes, on a narrow but well-evidenced ground: a single, dated, non-shared immigrant-invention event with no equivalent in Chicago, Milwaukee, or St. Louis. Cincinnati chili was invented in October 1922 by Macedonian immigrant brothers Tom and John Kiradjieff at their Empress restaurant on Vine Street — a Greek-restaurant sandwich-and-chili stand that adapted a Macedonian lamb-stew spice profile (cinnamon, cloves, nutmeg) to ground beef and served it over spaghetti, a dish tradition documented nowhere else in the Midwest. This sits on top of, but is genuinely distinct from, Cincinnati's shared "German Triangle" brewing/rowhouse heritage (Over-the-Rhine is architecturally similar in kind — though not identical in specific built form — to German immigrant neighborhoods in Milwaukee and St. Louis, so that layer alone would **not** clear the swap test on its own, per FILE ROLE & METHOD above). The chili-parlor institution built on top of the Kiradjieff invention (Empress → Skyline, 1949, founded by former Empress employee and Greek immigrant Nicholas Lambrinides; Gold Star, 1965, founded by four Jordanian immigrant brothers) is itself a second, reinforcing, non-shared immigrant-restaurateur thread specific to Cincinnati. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the dates/founders] [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: Cincinnati chili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cincinnati_chili); [Wikipedia: Kiradjieff brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiradjieff_brothers); [NKyTribune — Our Rich History: Cincinnati chili](https://nkytribune.com/2024/04/our-rich-history-cincinnati-chili-a-greek-immigrant-tradition-a-fun-food-that-really-caught-on/); [Wikipedia: Gold Star Chili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_Star_Chili); [Wikipedia: Skyline Chili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skyline_Chili)]

**Indiana — does it clear the test?** Yes, on similar grounds: a single, dated, documented sandwich invention (the breaded pork tenderloin sandwich, Nick's Kitchen, Huntington, Indiana, 1908) diffused statewide into a still-thriving small-town diner culture with no equivalent regional claim in Illinois, Michigan, Wisconsin, or Missouri. The sandwich is a German immigrant community's Americanization of wiener schnitzel — thin-pounded, breaded, fried pork, eaten by hand in a bun rather than by knife and fork — and its diffusion mechanism (small-town, family-run diners across the state, celebrated by named "Tenderloin Trail"-style culinary-tourism efforts in multiple counties) is itself a distinct, checkable Indiana-specific pattern. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — the Nick's Kitchen origin claim is the most consistently repeated account, though, like several other "first sandwich" claims already logged elsewhere in this project, a claimed rather than airtight-proven origin] [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: Pork tenderloin sandwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pork_tenderloin_sandwich); [Visit Indiana — This diner is where the breaded pork tenderloin originated](https://visitindiana.in.gov/blog/post/this-diner-is-where-the-breaded-pork-tenderloin-originated/); [Uncultured Palate — Pork Tenderloin Sandwich](https://www.unculturedpalate.com/this-food-in-history/pork-tenderloin-sandwich)]

**A second, independent Indiana-specific thread**: sugar cream pie, traced to Quaker and Shaker farm communities in eastern Indiana between 1810–1825, popularized commercially by a still-operating Winchester, Indiana bakery, and given unofficial "state pie" status by a 2009 Indiana Senate resolution (which passed the Senate but did not proceed to a full state-law designation — stated carefully as "unofficial," not "official"). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Food Republic — The Sweet History Of Indiana's Iconic Sugar Cream Pie](https://www.foodrepublic.com/1716323/sugar-cream-pie-history-indiana/); [WRTV — National Pie Day: The history of Indiana's state pie](https://www.wrtv.com/lifestyle/food/national-pie-day-the-history-of-indianas-state-pie); [Indiana General Assembly — Senate Resolution 0059 (2009)](https://archive.iga.in.gov/2009/bills/SRESP/SR0059.html)]

**What this zone characterization is not claiming**: Indiana is not a single uniform food culture any more than Ohio is — the tenderloin sandwich and sugar cream pie are two real, independently-sourced anchors, not proof every Indiana dish is distinct from its Midwest neighbors. Ordinary Midwestern-American food (casual dining chains, backyard grilling per `us.md`'s national baseline) is the correct default for anything not tied to one of these two anchors.

**Rest of Ohio, beyond Cincinnati — a real, logged gap, not a claim of coverage**: this file's research concentrated on Cincinnati/southwest Ohio specifically, plus one additional non-Cincinnati Ohio dish (the Cleveland Polish Boy) that surfaced as clearly strong enough to include. Columbus, Dayton, Toledo, and Ohio more broadly were not independently researched to the same depth — logged in the Gap Log, not silently treated as covered.

## TRUSTED CONTENT

### Midwest-wide

- **The "German Triangle" (Cincinnati, Milwaukee, St. Louis) is a real, documented, shared 19th-century German-immigrant-heritage pattern spanning this entire file, not a unique claim for any one zone — stated once, here, rather than three times.** All three cities absorbed major German immigration, developed brewing industries, and carry German-immigrant-built rowhouse/tavern architecture of a broadly similar kind. Per this file's FILE ROLE & METHOD, each zone's actual distinctness argument rests on other, non-shared evidence (Cincinnati's specific 1922 chili invention; Milwaukee's specific brewing→tavern→supper-club institutional chain; St. Louis's specific Hill neighborhood settlement story) — a scene simply labeled "German-heritage Midwestern city" without one of these more specific anchors is not confidently placeable in any single one of the three. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the shared-pattern framing itself]
- **Sauce/topping color and consistency is this file's single most recurring, most checkable visual-authenticity marker across multiple unrelated dishes** — the same logic `us.md` already applies nationally to BBQ and enchilada sauce: Kansas City BBQ sauce (thick, dark red-brown, glossy, clinging) vs. Cincinnati chili (thin, glossy, deep reddish-brown, never chunky) vs. Chicago mild sauce (thin, glossy, orange-red) vs. a Detroit coney's wet chili vs. a Flint coney's dry chili are all genuinely different, non-interchangeable sauces that a generated image could easily conflate if not anchored to their specific color/consistency description.
- **Cheese-under-sauce or cheese-not-fully-melted construction recurs across several of this file's signature dishes and is a genuinely non-obvious, checkable visual trait worth naming as a cross-cutting pattern**: Chicago deep-dish, stuffed, and Detroit-style pizza all place a visible cheese layer directly against the crust with sauce spooned on top rather than the reverse; Cincinnati chili's shredded-cheddar topping sits as a loose, largely un-melted mound rather than a fused sheet; St. Louis pizza's Provel is engineered for a smooth, non-stringy melt rather than mozzarella's characteristic pull. A generated image defaulting to a standard "melted cheese on top, fully fused" look for any of these dishes is a checkable error.

### Illinois / Chicago

- **Chicago's food-cart/street-stand economy is specific and dated, not a generic "big city has street food" claim.** The Chicago-style hot dog's canonical vegetable toppings were reportedly added by Greek and Italian pushcart vendors during the Great Depression, layering onto a base frankfurter tradition (Vienna Beef, founded by Jewish immigrants for the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition, made all-beef specifically to serve kosher-observant customers). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [The Takeout — Chicago's Hot Dogs Are Variations On A Theme](https://www.thetakeout.com/chicago-hot-dog-versions-vienna-history-1838264745/); [Mashed — The Untold Truth Of The Chicago-Style Hot Dog](https://www.mashed.com/226340/the-untold-truth-of-the-chicago-style-hot-dog/)]

[CONFIDENCE: HIGH for all three styles existing and being visually distinct; MEDIUM for the "dramatically outsells" claim, sourced to a single food-journalism piece rather than sales data] [SOURCE: [Tasting Table — What's The Difference Between Chicago Deep Dish And Tavern-Style Pizza?](https://www.tastingtable.com/1613854/chicago-deep-dish-vs-tavern-style-pizza/); [The Takeout — What Sets Tavern-Style Pizza Apart From The Other Chicago Pies](https://www.thetakeout.com/1615994/tavern-style-pizza-chicago-explained/)]

### Wisconsin

- **The supper club is a real, historically continuous, statewide Wisconsin dining institution, not a themed restaurant concept invented for tourism.** Supper clubs trace to Prohibition-era roadhouses in the 1920s/early 1930s; when Prohibition ended in 1933, these roadhouses evolved into the supper-club format — a genuine "third category," not quite a bar, not quite a restaurant. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Red Circle Inn — What Exactly Is a Supper Club?](https://www.redcircleinn.com/post/what-exactly-is-a-supper-club-and-why-wisconsin-perfected-it)]
- **The Wisconsin Friday fish fry is a three-cause tradition (Catholic religious observance, Prohibition-era tavern economics, and Great Lakes freshwater-fish abundance), not a single-cause story.** Wisconsin's heavily Catholic Polish- and German-descended settler population observed Friday meat abstinence; Prohibition-era taverns used fish-fry nights to stay financially afloat; cheap, plentiful freshwater perch and walleye made fish the practical choice. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Travel Wisconsin — A Brief History of the Wisconsin Fish Fry](https://www.travelwisconsin.com/stories/a-brief-history-of-the-wisconsin-fish-fry); [Marquette Wire — How did 'Friday fish fries' become a Wisconsin tradition?](https://marquettewire.org/4137650/news/how-did-friday-fish-fries-became-a-wisconsin-tradition-learn-about-the-history-of-the-meal/)]

[CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Gardners Wisconsin Cheese — Wisconsin Cheese Curds: History, Culture & Freshness](https://www.gardnerswisconsincheese.com/blogs/articles/wisconsin-cheese-curds-the-snack-that-built-a-dairy-empire)]

### Chicago & Great Lakes

- **"Never ketchup" is a real, checkable, near-universal Chicago hot dog rule, and its absence is itself a visual/compositional fact worth stating explicitly for a staged photo** — no red ketchup bottle or ketchup streak should appear in a Chicago hot dog scene. The national layer, including how US large gatherings work and the Thanksgiving, Fourth of July, Christmas, Easter, birthday, graduation, wedding and baby-shower entries, lives in `us.md`'s CELEBRATIONS & LARGE GATHERINGS section and applies here unchanged. This file has no festivals register, so this section sits after ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES. Party size means the place settings in frame, never the size of the gathering.)

**What differs from the national "how gatherings work" block:** the Midwest's recurring large gatherings run through **church halls and church basements** more than backyards: Catholic parish fish fries in Lent, Lutheran potlucks and lutefisk suppers, and the graduation open house that fills a family's garage every June. Volunteers cook in quantity, food is served from a line or a buffet onto plain plates, and long folding tables in a fellowship hall or gym are the table form. The Wisconsin tavern and supper-club fish fry (see catalog) is a restaurant register, separate from the parish fish fry below. [EDITORIAL, grounded in ENVIRONMENT, Chicago & Great Lakes, and the sources in each entry]

#### Celebration: Lenten parish fish fry
- Type: calendar holiday (Fridays in Lent, roughly February to April; many Wisconsin venues run fish fries every Friday year-round)
- When: Friday evenings, roughly 4:30pm to 7:30pm. Intake time of day: **evening** (fluorescent or warm hall light), or **golden-hour** through hall windows in late Lent.
- Gathering: parish families, neighbours and non-Catholics alike, hundreds over an evening; run by parish volunteers, the Knights of Columbus or other groups [HIGH — The Miscellany (Catholic diocesan paper), Chowhound, Block Club Chicago, Signal Cleveland, Ladue News, Flatwater Free Press]. The tradition traces to 19th-century Polish, German and other Central European Catholic immigrants, Friday meat abstinence and Great Lakes fish [HIGH — same; see TRUSTED CONTENT for the Wisconsin three-cause history]. Venue: other (parish hall, school cafeteria, KofC hall); drive-thru and carry-out lines are common.
- The spread: fried fish (usually cod, also perch, walleye or whitefish in the Great Lakes states), fried shrimp, French fries, coleslaw, macaroni and cheese, a soft roll; potato pancakes and rye in Wisconsin; desserts from a bake table [HIGH for the core menu — same sources]. See catalog: **Wisconsin Friday fish fry — fried perch or walleye plate** for the batter, plate composition and proportions (the parish plate is the same food on a paper or styrofoam plate or compartment tray, usually cod in larger, thicker pieces).
- Snapshot staging:
  - **1 setting:** a long folding table with a white paper cover; one compartment tray or paper plate with two pieces of golden battered cod, fries, a cup of coleslaw, a soft roll, a tartar cup and a lemon wedge. In frame: the next identical tray, a ketchup bottle and a napkin dispenser. Cues: the table runs out of frame; a serving line with steam and volunteers in aprons soft behind.
  - **2 settings:** two identical trays side by side; a bake-sale plate of brownies or bars between them. Cues: rows of tables in a gym or hall receding; a basketball hoop folded up at the top edge (school-gym hall).
  - **Small group (3 to 4 settings):** a stretch of long table with identical plates and a shared basket of rolls. Cues: blurred families at the next table; a take-out stack of clamshells on a cart at the edge.
- Decor and cues: paper table covers, folding chairs, a parish hall or school gym, hand-lettered signs (not legible). Clichés to avoid: nuns or priests as the subject, a pub look.
- Never stage: beer (the Wisconsin fish fry is often beer-battered and served with beer or an old fashioned; never shown; a few parish fries have beer stands too); raffles; crucifixes, Stations of the Cross or other religious items in frame with the product.
- Confidence and sources: [HIGH — six independent news and diocesan sources]; staging [EDITORIAL].

#### Celebration: Graduation open house (Upper Midwest)
- Type: life event
- When: late May to July, weekend afternoons, a 3-to-4-hour drop-in window; families in one class often stagger dates so guests can attend several. Intake time of day: **midday**.
- Gathering: relatives, neighbours, church members, teammates' families and teachers dropping in; guests often give a card with money toward college. Open houses are described as traditional for graduations in Minnesota and Michigan [MEDIUM — Patch (Minnesota) and forum and lifestyle sources; weak tier, flagged]. ~50 to 150 over the afternoon [EDITORIAL]. Venue: home outdoor, specifically the **cleared-out attached garage** with the door up, plus the driveway and backyard; or a church hall or park shelter.
- The spread: one hot sandwich meat in quantity kept warm in an electric roaster (sloppy joes, shredded beef or barbecue pulled pork on soft buns), potato salad, macaroni or pasta salad, chips, a veggie tray, a fruit tray, a decorated sheet cake and pans of bars [MEDIUM — Minnesota sources via Patch and forums; LOW for any single menu]. See `us.md` Graduation party for the national form. **Sloppy joe / shredded-beef sandwich** has no catalog entry: a soft white bun ~9 cm across heaped with loose, glossy, tomato-red sauced ground beef (or shredded beef), spilling slightly, on a paper plate (see CANDIDATE QUEUE). **Bars** have no catalog entry: pan-baked squares ~5 cm (brownies, lemon bars, Scotcheroos with a chocolate-butterscotch top) cut in a 9x13 pan or arranged on a tray (see CANDIDATE QUEUE).
- Snapshot staging:
  - **1 setting:** a paper plate on a folding table in the garage: a shredded-beef sandwich, a scoop of potato salad, chips, a few veggie-tray carrots, a bar. In frame: the electric roaster with its lid tipped and serving spoon, partly cropped. Cues: the garage door track overhead or a photo board soft behind; balloons in school colours.
  - **2 settings:** two identical plates at a round folding table on the driveway; a tray of bars between them. Cues: the buffet tables in the garage soft behind; a card box (no legible writing); blurred guests.
  - **Small group (3 to 4 settings):** a stretch of folding table with identical plates, the veggie tray and a salad bowl in the centre, cropped. Cues: a sheet cake with piped lettering (school name not legible); more tables and lawn chairs on the grass; mown lawn and a neighbouring house soft.
- Decor and cues: plastic tablecloths in school colours, a memory board of photos and trophies, a garage with tools and shelves tidied or draped. Clichés to avoid: everyone in cap and gown, a beer fridge in the garage (real; keep closed and out of frame).
- Never stage: alcohol; legible school names, mascots or crests; cash or money cards beside the product.
- Confidence and sources: [MEDIUM/LOW — Patch, forum and lifestyle sources; flag for a stronger source]; staging [EDITORIAL].

#### Celebration: Lutheran lutefisk and meatball supper
- Type: community or family gathering (yearly church supper, October to February; Scandinavian Christmas tables at home are the private version)
- When: a weekday or weekend evening, often served in seatings from mid-afternoon. Intake time of day: **evening** (church basement light, early winter darkness outside).
- Gathering: the congregation feeding the town and visitors: a single dinner can draw 1,000+ diners and need 150 to 200 volunteers [HIGH — Atlas Obscura, MPR News, Southern Minn (Blooming Prairie Leader), Minnesota Monthly; numbers are per-church examples, not statewide]. Venue: other (Lutheran church basement or fellowship hall, long tables). Minnesota, the Dakotas, Wisconsin, Iowa; Norwegian, Swedish and Finnish heritage.
- The spread: lutefisk (lye-cured dried cod, reconstituted and baked or boiled) with melted butter or white sauce, Swedish or Norwegian meatballs in gravy, boiled potatoes, mashed rutabaga, cranberries or lingonberries, lefse (soft potato flatbread, buttered and sugared), and rice pudding with lingonberry; served family-style in bowls passed along the table, or as a buffet line [HIGH — same sources]. Not in the catalog (see CANDIDATE QUEUE); visual anchors: **lutefisk** is translucent, pale, gelatinous white flakes of fish glistening with butter, in a white serving bowl; **lefse** is a thin, soft, pale cream round ~30 cm across with brown griddle spots, folded into quarters or rolled; **meatballs** are small (~3 cm) brown balls in a light-brown cream gravy.
- Snapshot staging:
  - **1 setting:** a plain white church-basement plate with a spoon of lutefisk with butter, three meatballs in gravy, a boiled potato, mashed rutabaga, a folded lefse. In frame: the passing bowls (meatballs, potatoes) partly cropped and a butter dish. Cues: the long table runs out of frame with identical plates; a church-basement serving window soft behind.
  - **2 settings:** two identical plates side by side; a plate of rolled lefse and a bowl of lingonberries between them. Cues: volunteers in aprons carrying bowls (blurred); stackable chairs; a coffee urn on a side table.
  - **Small group (3 to 4 settings):** a stretch of long table with identical plates and four serving bowls down the middle, cropped. Cues: more tables receding under fluorescent light; a rice-pudding bowl at one place; a snowy window.
- Decor and cues: white paper on long tables, plain church china or heavy white plates, coffee cups, Scandinavian rosemaling-pattern details (not national flags). Clichés to avoid: Viking helmets, horned-helmet jokes, an Ikea-showroom look.
- Never stage: alcohol (aquavit at a home Scandinavian Christmas; never); the church sanctuary, altar or hymn singing; jokes about lutefisk's smell as a visual gag.
- Confidence and sources: [HIGH — Atlas Obscura, MPR News, Southern Minn, Minnesota Monthly]; staging [EDITORIAL].

#### Celebration: Church-basement potluck (hotdish supper)
- Type: community or family gathering (recurring: church events, funerals, reunions, holidays)
- When: after a Sunday service, a weekday evening event or a funeral lunch; meal at midday or early evening. Intake time of day: **midday**.
- Gathering: congregation and family, ~30 to 150 [EDITORIAL]. Venue: other (church basement or fellowship hall). See ENVIRONMENT, Chicago & Great Lakes, Lutheran-church-basement potluck [MEDIUM-HIGH there].
- The spread: a buffet line of 9x13 glass baking dishes, each family bringing one: hotdishes (see catalog: **Minnesota hotdish**), a Jell-O or "salad" made with whipped topping, a ham-bun tray, a relish tray, pans of bars, and church coffee in an urn [MEDIUM — catalog sources; the Jell-O salad and ham buns are EDITORIAL from common practice, not verified this pass]. A real potluck line carries 15 to 40 dishes.
- Snapshot staging:
  - **1 setting:** a plain plate with a square of tater-tot hotdish, a spoon of a pastel Jell-O salad, a ham bun, a bar. In frame: the hotdish's glass pan with a spatula, partly cropped. Cues: the buffet line of foil-lidded pans soft behind; a coffee urn.
  - **2 settings:** two identical plates at a long table; a relish tray and a pan of bars between them. Cues: rows of folding tables; blurred older parishioners in cardigans.
  - **Small group (3 to 4 settings):** a stretch of long table with identical plates and two casserole pans in the middle, cropped. Cues: the buffet line running out of frame; stacked coffee cups; a bulletin board (not legible).
- Decor and cues: plastic or paper table covers, 9x13 pans with masking-tape name labels (not legible), slow cookers, a pass-through kitchen window. Clichés to avoid: a mocking "Minnesota nice" caricature; overly retro 1950s styling.
- Never stage: a funeral or casket context with the product (stage only the meal if the brief is a funeral lunch, with nothing in frame indicating a death); religious items beside the product; alcohol.
- Confidence and sources: [MEDIUM — ENVIRONMENT and hotdish catalog sources]; headcount and specific dishes [EDITORIAL].

### Chicago & Great Lakes

#### Dish: Chicago-style hot dog (full authoritative entry — pulls forward and substantially expands `us.md`'s index entry)
- Category: Everyday, strongly occasion-linked (ballgames, casual lunch, on-the-go)
- Cuisine lineage: Native-American-tradition with a German-sausage/Jewish-immigrant-frankfurter root (Vienna Beef, founded by Jewish immigrants for the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition, made the dogs all-beef specifically to serve kosher-observant customers), with the now-canonical vegetable toppings added later by Greek and Italian pushcart vendors during the Great Depression.
- Regional form variation: This *is* the authoritative Chicago-style variant `us.md`'s national hot dog entry points to.
- Serving format: Sandwich/handheld only
- Primary protein: An all-beef, natural-casing frankfurter, steamed (not grilled) — standard length roughly 6 inches, with natural casing producing real, checkable diameter/shape irregularity and a distinct "snap" texture when bitten, not a uniformly smooth surface. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Webstaurant Store — Vienna Beef 6" Natural Casing Beef Frank](https://www.webstaurantstore.com/vienna-beef-6-81-natural-casing-beef-frank-5-lb-case/871VB10980.html); [Hot Dog Chicago Style — Fun Facts](https://www.hotdogchicagostyle.com/funfacts.php)]
- Primary starch/accompaniment: A steamed poppy-seed bun — soft, pale, with visible poppy seeds scattered across the top crust. A Chicago bakery operating since 1909 is the most commonly cited traditional bun supplier, cited for authenticity context, not as a literal branded prompt ingredient. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Side dishes/condiments, and how served: The full topping list, always pre-applied by the vendor/cook rather than added at the table — yellow mustard, neon-green sweet pickle relish, chopped raw white onion, two tomato wedges, a whole dill pickle spear laid alongside the dog (not chopped), pickled sport peppers (small, whole, bright yellow-green), and a dusting of celery salt over the top — **and never ketchup**, a near-inviolable rule since ketchup's sweetness/acidity is described as clashing with the other tart/briny toppings. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Serving vessel: Wrapped in a thin paper wrapper/foil sleeve or served in a paper boat/basket — not plated at a counter-service stand.
- Real-world size/scale: The frank itself runs roughly 6 inches long in a bun of matching length — a useful, concrete size anchor comparable in scale to a standard 4.83-inch (12.3cm) Coca-Cola can laid on its side, per `01-brand/coca-cola-guidelines.md` §3.
- Visual/plating characteristics: The frank is a deep reddish-brown, glossy from steaming (never grill-marked or charred, since it is never grilled in the classic preparation), with a taut, slightly irregular natural casing visible along its length. The relish is a distinctly artificial, bright neon-green — a genuinely different, more saturated color than a typical pickle-relish green found on a generic hot dog elsewhere in the US. Tomato wedges sit propped against the dog rather than laid flat; the whole pickle spear runs the length of the bun on one side. Celery salt appears as a faint, dry, pale-green-flecked dusting across the very top, visible as texture/speckling, not a wet coating. Toppings are piled in a specific, non-random arrangement — mustard drizzled first in a thin zigzag, then relish/onion/tomato/pickle/peppers arranged in distinct un-mixed zones along the dog's length — frequently described as "dragged through the garden," implying many distinct, individually identifiable toppings rather than one blended mass. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Common confusion: Should not be depicted with ketchup, grill marks/char, or a plain white bun without poppy seeds. Should not be confused with the New York cart dog (`us-northeast.md`, boiled in seasoned brine, sauerkraut and onion sauce) or the Coney dog (below, topped with chili) — the Chicago dog carries no chili and no sauerkraut.
- Confidence: HIGH overall
- Sources: [Pequod's Pizza — What Is on a Chicago-Style Hot Dog?](https://pequodspizza.com/blog/what-is-on-chicago-style-hot-dog/); [Wikipedia: Chicago-style hot dog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago-style_hot_dog); [The Takeout — Chicago's Hot Dogs Are Variations On A Theme](https://www.thetakeout.com/chicago-hot-dog-versions-vienna-history-1838264745/); [Time Out — The 7 Toppings of the Chicago Hot Dog](https://www.timeout.com/chicago/food-drink/the-7-toppings-of-the-chicago-hot-dog)
- Composition & proportions (§4.7) — one dog in a paper boat [EDITORIAL unless tagged]:
  - What dominates: from a three-quarter view the bun and frank are ~50% of the dog's area, the pickle spear ~20%, all other toppings together ~30%. Seven toppings, each a small distinct zone, none heaped.
  - Components: frank 1, ~15 cm long (6 in, per the entry's HIGH sourcing), ~2.5 cm thick, ends showing just past the bun. Bun 1, same length, poppy seeds sparse. Dill pickle spear 1, ~12–15 cm, laid along one side of the dog. Tomato 2 wedges [MEDIUM — Wikipedia and recipe sources, via search], ~5–6 cm, tucked along the other side. Sport peppers 2–3 [MEDIUM — same, via search], ~3–4 cm, bright yellow-green, whole. Relish a ~1.5 cm-wide neon-green strip; onion a light scatter of ~0.5 cm dice; mustard one thin zigzag; celery salt faint specks.
  - Arrangement: pickle spear and tomato wedges flank the frank lengthwise, so the dressed dog is wider than the bun; relish and onion sit on top of the frank.
  - Vessel fill: a ~18–20 cm paper boat or an opened paper wrap; the dog fills ~80% of the boat's length.
  - State cues: frank glossy from steaming, no char; bun soft and matte; tomato wedges moist.
  - Absent on purpose: ketchup (bottle, streak or packet); grill marks; sauerkraut; chili; cheese; fries heaped on top; a toasted or plain seedless bun.
  - Prompt-ready line: "One Chicago-style hot dog in a paper boat, a little longer than the can is tall: a soft pale bun sprinkled with poppy seeds, a glossy reddish-brown steamed frank, a thin yellow mustard zigzag, a strip of neon-green relish and a scatter of chopped white onion on top, two tomato wedges tucked along one side, a whole dill pickle spear along the other, two small yellow-green peppers, faint celery-salt specks. No ketchup, no grill marks."

#### Dish: Chicago Italian beef (full authoritative entry — pulls forward and expands `us.md`'s existing index entry)
- Category: Everyday
- Cuisine lineage: Italian-American, Chicago-specific, tied directly to the Union Stockyards immigrant-labor history in the Zone Characterization above — the most commonly repeated origin account credits Italian immigrant stockyard workers stretching tough, less-desirable cuts of beef by slicing it paper-thin and slow-simmering it in seasoned jus; a competing account credits a home-delivery beef-sandwich business. A Little Italy business founded 1938 is the most commonly cited still-operating early business. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH for the general stockyards-immigrant-labor origin; LOW-MEDIUM for crediting one single specific inventor]
- Regional form variation: This *is* the Chicago-specific dish `us.md`'s index entry points to.
- Serving format: Sandwich/handheld — always wet/saturated rather than a dry sandwich; no plated/knife-and-fork sibling.
- Primary protein: Beef (top round or sirloin most commonly cited), seasoned with garlic/Italian herbs, roasted, then sliced paper-thin — genuinely thinner than a deli-sliced roast beef sandwich, closer to shaved-steak thickness — and simmered/held in a seasoned au jus.
- Primary starch/accompaniment: A long Italian-style roll, sturdier than a hot dog bun, meant to absorb liquid without disintegrating.
- Side dishes/condiments, and how served: Two named topping choices, both applied by the counter person, not self-served — **sweet peppers** (pale-green Melrose peppers, sliced or whole, sautéed until soft, not raw or crisp) or **giardiniera** (a visibly chunky, oil-packed relish of chopped hot peppers, celery, and carrots/olives); ordering both together is "mixed" or "combo." [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Serving vessel: Wrapped tightly in foil or butcher paper to manage the dripping jus rather than plated.
- Utensils/eating customs: **The "dip" ordering system is a real, specific, three-level choice that should be reflected in how wet the sandwich looks**: "dry" (little to no jus), the default "wet"/"dipped" (the whole sandwich briefly submerged in jus before serving), and "double-dipped" (visibly wetter/more saturated still). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Visual/plating characteristics: The beef is shaved paper-thin and piled loosely and unevenly into the roll — not stacked in neat, uniform layers — giving a visibly tangled, irregular cross-section with visible dark-brown seasoned edges rather than a uniform pink interior. At "wet"/"dipped" level, the roll's exterior is visibly darkened, glistening, and saturated with jus — the bread's surface should read as translucent/soaked, especially at the bottom and ends, sometimes with a small pool of jus visible in the foil/paper wrap. Sweet peppers appear as soft, collapsed, pale-green strips with a slight oil sheen; giardiniera appears chunkier and more varied-colored (bright red/orange pepper pieces, pale celery, sometimes olive slices). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Common confusion: Should not be depicted as a dry sandwich (a defining trait is that "wet" is the default order, not an optional upgrade) or with deli-style thick-cut/cubed beef; should not be confused with a Philly cheesesteak (`us-mid-atlantic.md`, no cheese, no griddled onions mixed into the meat) or a French dip (thicker-cut beef, no giardiniera/sweet-pepper tradition).
- Confidence: HIGH for composition, dip system, and topping options; MEDIUM-LOW for the specific single-inventor origin claim
- Sources: [Choose Chicago — Italian Beef in Chicago](https://www.choosechicago.com/blog/dining/italian-beef-chicago/); [Chef Mike Hard — Wet, Sweet, Hot, Dipped: Classic Chicago Italian Beef Sandwich](https://www.chefmikehard.com/blog/wet-sweet-hot-dipped-classic-chicago-italian-beef-sandwich); [Red Sauce America — The History of Chicago's Italian Beef Sandwich](https://www.redsauceamerica.com/blog/the-history-of-chicagos-italian-beef-sandwich/)
- Composition & proportions (§4.7) — one "wet" sandwich on opened foil [EDITORIAL unless tagged]:
  - What dominates: in side view the roll is ~55% of the height, the tangle of beef ~35%, peppers or giardiniera ~10%. The roll is hinged, not cut through, and the beef spills past its ends by 1–2 cm.
  - Components: roll 1, ~15–20 cm long (6–8 in) [MEDIUM — Wikipedia, via search], ~7–8 cm across — longer than the can is tall. Beef: dozens of shaved slices ~1–2 mm thick, loosely folded, ~85–150 g per sandwich (home recipes give 60–90 g; stand sandwiches read heavier) [EDITORIAL; home-recipe range via search]. Sweet peppers 3–6 soft strips ~1–1.5 cm wide, or giardiniera a line of ~0.5–1 cm pieces along the top of the meat.
  - Arrangement: meat piled unevenly inside the hinge, taller in the middle; toppings laid along the meat, not spread over the bread.
  - Vessel fill: the sandwich lies on opened foil or butcher paper ~30 cm across, a jus puddle 2–5 cm wide at one end.
  - State cues: bread darkened and translucent with jus, especially the bottom and ends; beef glistening; a light wisp of steam.
  - Absent on purpose: melted cheese (unless briefed); lettuce or tomato; a dry, crisp-toasted roll; a French-dip ramekin of jus as the default; neat stacked deli slices.
  - Prompt-ready line: "An Italian beef sandwich lying on opened foil, a little longer than the can is tall: a long soft roll soaked dark and glistening with meat juices, hinged open around a loose, tangled pile of paper-thin seasoned beef slices with browned edges, a few soft pale-green pepper strips along the top, a small puddle of juice on the foil. Steam rising, no cheese, no lettuce."

#### Dish: Chicago mild sauce, with South Side fried chicken and rib tips
- Category: Everyday
- Cuisine lineage: African-American/soul-food tradition, Chicago South Side-specific — genuinely distinct from the Italian-immigrant hot-dog-and-beef-stand tradition above.
- Regional form variation: Chicago South Side-specific; not documented as spreading nationally the way Nashville hot chicken has. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- Serving format: Condiment/sauce, drizzled over or served alongside fried chicken, rib tips, wings, or french fries — not a standalone dish, but included here as the single most checkable, Chicago-specific visual/flavor marker for this restaurant tradition.
- Origin: Popularized from the 1950s–70s onward by South Side chicken shacks. Cited for historical/authenticity context; a generic South Side chicken-shack counter is the appropriate prompt subject, not any one specific still-operating chain by name, per `country-file-schema.md` §7.5. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Primary composition: A ketchup-and-barbecue-sauce base blended with hot sauce and brown sugar — sweet, tangy, and mildly (not aggressively) spicy despite the name-adjacent association with heat.
- Visual/plating characteristics: A smooth, glossy, deep orange-red sauce, thinner and more pourable than a thick Kansas City-style BBQ sauce (see the Missouri & Kansas City zone below for that contrast) — drizzled in visible ribbons or pooled at the base of fried chicken pieces, rib tips, or a mound of fries, rather than fully coating/submerging the food. Rib tips themselves (a distinct cut — the cartilaginous ends trimmed from spare ribs, cut into bite-sized pieces) present as small, irregular, deep-fried-or-grilled dark-brown chunks rather than a full rack/slab shape.
- Common confusion: Should not be confused with a standard Buffalo wing sauce (thinner, vinegar-forward, orange rather than red, never sweet) or a thick, clinging Kansas City-style BBQ sauce — mild sauce is thinner and glossier than KC sauce but sweeter and less vinegar-forward than Buffalo sauce.
- Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH for the sauce's composition and South Side origin; MEDIUM for the specific visual/consistency description
- Sources: [Strangers Guide — The Gospel of Mild Sauce](https://strangersguide.com/articles/the-gospel-of-mild-sauce/); [InsideHook — Your Fried Chicken Is Begging for Chicago Mild Sauce](https://www.insidehook.com/food/chicago-mild-sauce-condiment-recipe); [Wikipedia: Mild sauce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mild_sauce)
- Composition & proportions (§4.7) — one chicken-shack order of wings or rib tips over fries [EDITORIAL throughout]:
  - What dominates: the meat ~45% of the tray surface, fries ~40% as the bed, sauce ribbons ~10–15%. The sauce is a drizzle, not a flood; fries show between the ribbons.
  - Components: fried wings 4–6 whole wings (~10–12 cm each), or rib tips ~8–12 irregular chunks ~3–5 cm; fries a bed of ~40–60 sticks; 1–2 slices of plain white bread under or beside the meat; mild sauce in 3–5 ribbons ~1 cm wide or in a ~5 cm plastic cup alongside.
  - Arrangement: fries on the bottom, meat heaped over the centre, bread tucked at one end, sauce drizzled across the top.
  - Vessel fill: a paper-lined tray or foam clamshell ~22×22 cm, filled to the edges, ~6–8 cm tall at the centre (about half the can's height).
  - State cues: chicken crust craggy and dry-crisp except where sauce lies; sauce glossy and orange-red; fries golden, slightly limp under the sauce.
  - Absent on purpose: thick dark KC sauce; Buffalo-orange coating; celery and ranch; a fully sauce-submerged tray; plated restaurant presentation.

- Serving vessel: A plain plate or paper-lined basket, classic bar-food presentation.
- Visual/plating characteristics — **the single most important compositional note for this dish**: **An uncut, whole Juicy Lucy is visually indistinguishable from an ordinary cheeseburger from the outside** — there is no external visual cue (no cheese visible on top, no bulge, no color difference) that would tell a viewer this is a Juicy Lucy rather than a plain burger. The patty exterior itself shows a well-seared, evenly browned crust from griddling, consistent with `us.md`'s existing classic-burger doneness conventions.
- Common confusion: An uncut Juicy Lucy photographed whole, with no bite taken and no cross-section shown, does not visually communicate the dish at all and would be indistinguishable from any other cheeseburger — this is a genuine staging risk specific to this dish, and should be treated as effectively mandatory: any prompt for this dish needs either a bite or a cut cross-section to be recognizable as a Juicy Lucy rather than a plain cheeseburger.

#### Dish: Kansas City burnt ends (full authoritative entry — supersedes the `us.md` national-index bullet)
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion — the signature order at a dedicated KC barbecue joint, but common enough to also appear at cookouts/backyard gatherings
- Cuisine lineage: Native-American-tradition, Kansas City-specific invention; traces to Henry Perry, credited as "the father of Kansas City barbecue," who began selling smoked meats from an alley stand in KC's Garment District in 1908, cooking "low and slow" over hickory and oak (sometimes with pecan, cherry, or apple wood). Perry trained apprentices including Charlie Bryant, whose brother Arthur Bryant later took over, renamed, and relocated the business — still operating today. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [KCUR — Meet Henry Perry, the Black entrepreneur who created Kansas City barbecue](https://www.kcur.org/arts-life/2021-02-13/kansas-city-barbecue-bbq-henry-perry-gates-arthur-bryants-history); [Wikipedia: Arthur Bryant's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Bryant%27s)]

Food writer Calvin Trillin is credited with popularizing the item nationally through his writing, including a widely-cited 1972 Playboy article. Demand eventually pushed burnt ends onto the menu as a paid item. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Going — Burnt Ends: The Barbecue Scraps That Became a Kansas City Staple](https://www.going.com/guides/burnt-ends-kansas-city); [Wikipedia: Burnt ends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnt_ends)]

#### Dish: St. Louis-style pizza (full authoritative entry — supersedes the `us.md` national-index bullet)
- Category: Everyday
- Cuisine lineage: Italian-American, but a genuinely distinct St. Louis-specific reinterpretation rather than a direct import from any single Italian regional tradition — a Hill restaurant opened in 1964 is widely credited as the style's popularizer and namer, though the underlying components (thin cracker crust, Provel, square cut) are described by multiple sources as a broader St. Louis restaurant-scene convention that restaurant scaled up and commercialized through early carryout/delivery, rather than a single, uncontested single-inventor claim. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the founding/role; MEDIUM for "sole inventor" framing — the same kind of popularizer-vs-inventor ambiguity already documented elsewhere in this KB for other dishes]
- Regional form variation: This **is** the authoritative "St. Louis-style" entry `us.md`'s national pizza index points to.
- Serving format: **Always cut into squares/rectangles ("party cut"), never triangular wedges** — a genuine, defining, checkable structural difference from every wedge-cut style in this KB (NY-style, Trenton tomato pie, New Haven apizza, Chicago deep-dish). Whole pie, shared at the table (a full pie yields roughly a 3x4 or similar grid of small squares) — no individually-folded handheld slice format exists for this style the way it does for NY-style.
- Primary composition: An unleavened, yeast-free dough (distinguishing it structurally from every other pizza style in this KB, which are all yeast-leavened), rolled very thin, docked before baking to prevent puffing, brushed with olive oil (which limits gluten development, contributing to the crisp, cracker-like rather than chewy texture); a slightly sweet tomato sauce spread edge-to-edge to the very perimeter (no bare crust border); topped with Provel cheese — a manufactured blend of provolone, cheddar, and Swiss with added liquid smoke, chosen specifically for its low melting point and non-stringy melt (see TRUSTED CONTENT above).
- Side dishes/condiments: Commonly ordered alongside toasted ravioli as a starter (see below) in a sit-down Hill restaurant context; crushed red pepper flakes and grated Parmesan are standard table condiments.
- Serving vessel: A large round metal pizza tray or pan, cut pie left whole/assembled on the tray or served pre-cut on a plate for takeout.
- Real-world size anchor: a standard cut square runs roughly 2-3 inches per side — smaller and flatter than a typical NY-style triangular slice, useful for staging scale relative to a classic red Coca-Cola can.
- Visual/plating characteristics: See VISUAL & PLATING NORMS above for full texture/finish detail (flat, unpuffed, pale golden crust; Provel's smooth, uniform, glossy melted layer with no browning or char spots the way mozzarella develops; no crust rim).
- Common confusion: Should not be depicted with mozzarella's stringy cheese-pull when a slice is lifted or pulled apart — Provel stays put on the slice rather than stretching. Should not be cut into triangular wedges. Should not show a puffed, blistered, or charred crust edge — all three would misrepresent it as NY-style, Neapolitan, or Chicago deep-dish respectively. Also distinct from Detroit-style and Sicilian square-cut pizzas: St. Louis's crust is thin and cracker-crisp throughout, not thick, airy, and focaccia-like the way Detroit-style's pan-baked crust is; also distinct from Chicago tavern-style pizza (above), which is also thin/cracker-crisp and square-cut but uses regular mozzarella-forward cheese rather than Provel's specific melt behavior.
- Confidence: HIGH overall
- Sources: [Wikipedia: Imo's Pizza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imo's_Pizza); [Wikipedia: St. Louis-style pizza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Louis%E2%80%93style_pizza); [Wikipedia: Provel cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provel_cheese); [KCUR — St. Louis pizza is the style everyone loves to hate](https://www.kcur.org/arts-life/2022-11-30/st-louis-pizza-provel-imos-missouri); [The Kitchn — The Crispy, Golden, Cracker-Style Pizza](https://www.thekitchn.com/king-arthur-flour-st-louis-style-pizza-23076625)
- Composition & proportions (§4.7) — whole pie and one diner's share:
  - What dominates: Provel-covered surface ~95% of the pie, no bare rim. [EDITORIAL]

Crust ~3 mm. Toppings, when briefed, small and flat (thin pepperoni rounds ~3 cm, sausage bits ~1 cm).
  - Arrangement: grid left assembled on the round tray; 3–4 squares lifted onto a plate.
  - Served portion: 3–4 squares per person. [EDITORIAL]
  - State cues: cheese smooth, glossy, lightly golden, not stretching; crust dry and flat.
  - Absent on purpose: cheese strings; wedges; a puffed or charred rim; a deep crust.
  - Prompt-ready line: "A round, cracker-thin pizza on a metal tray, cut into a grid of small squares about the can's width, a smooth, glossy, lightly golden melted cheese and slightly sweet red sauce running right to the edge with no crust rim; a few small edge pieces. #### Dish: Cincinnati chili — "ways" (chili spaghetti) (full authoritative entry — pulls forward and expands `us.md`'s national index pointer)
- Category: Everyday
- Cuisine lineage: **A distinctive case worth stating explicitly, on the same "the popular name implies an origin it doesn't have" pattern `country-file-schema.md` §4.1 flags for spaghetti and meatballs**: Cincinnati chili is Greek/Macedonian-immigrant-American in actual origin, not a variant of Texas/Mexican-American chili con carne despite sharing the name "chili" — its spice lineage (cinnamon, cloves, nutmeg, adapted from a Macedonian lamb-stew tradition) and its serving format (over pasta) both trace to the Balkans by way of Greek immigrant restaurateurs, not to the Southwest.
- Regional form variation: Native to, and still overwhelmingly concentrated in, Greater Cincinnati and adjacent Northern Kentucky — not a documented tradition elsewhere in Ohio or the wider Midwest at the same density. `us.md`'s Chili section's Texas chili is a **form-changing** contrast, not a regional variant of the same dish.
- Serving format: **Two genuine, coexisting formats, both built from the identical chili sauce** — (1) the "ways" (chili spaghetti), a plated, fork-and-knife dish; (2) the cheese coney, a handheld hot dog. Each gets its own visual entry (see the coney sibling below).
- History: Invented October 1922 by Macedonian immigrant brothers Tom and John Kiradjieff, who ran a hot dog/Greek-restaurant stand called the Empress (in a corner of the Empress Burlesque Theater on Vine Street, Cincinnati) that was struggling until Tom began serving a Middle Eastern/Balkan-spiced chili over spaghetti. Washington neighborhood. Numerous smaller, independent, still-operating parlors round out the category. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: Kiradjieff brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiradjieff_brothers); [Wikipedia: Cincinnati chili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cincinnati_chili); [Wikipedia: Skyline Chili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skyline_Chili); [Wikipedia: Gold Star Chili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_Star_Chili)]

- Side dishes/condiments, and how served: **The "ways" ordering system, in full**: a "two-way" is chili over spaghetti alone; a "three-way" adds a substantial mound of finely shredded cheddar on top; a "four-way" adds either diced raw onion or kidney beans (the diner's choice) between the chili and the cheese; a "five-way" adds both onion and beans. - Utensils/eating customs: Fork and knife for the "ways" (never eaten by hand); a plated coney is eaten by hand like an ordinary hot dog.
- Visual/plating characteristics: See VISUAL & PLATING NORMS above for full detail — in summary, a thin, glossy, deep reddish-brown sauce (never a thick, chunky stew) blankets a bed of spaghetti completely, topped with a tall, voluminous mound of finely shredded, largely unmelted cheddar; raw diced white onion and/or whole light-red kidney beans, when ordered, appear as their own distinct, visible layer beneath the cheese.
- Common confusion — **a three-way comparison, not just two**: (1) **Texas chili** (`us-texas.md`, per `us.md`'s existing pointer) — a thick, chunky, standalone beanless beef stew eaten from a bowl with a spoon, no pasta, no cheese mound; (2) **generic/Midwestern chili con carne** (the thicker, chunkier, tomato-forward, bean-and-coarse-ground-beef stew most Americans picture by default when they hear "chili," typically eaten as a standalone bowl) — flagged here as a real, equally important confusable alternative even though it does not yet have its own sourced KB entry — Cincinnati chili's thin, spiced, pasta-topping form is the checkable differentiator against both. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the Texas-chili distinction; MEDIUM for the generic-chili-con-carne distinction, since that comparison point isn't itself a sourced KB entry yet]

#### Dish: Buckeye candy
- Category: Everyday snack, strongly seasonal/gift-giving-coded (especially around the winter holidays and Ohio State football season) but genuinely eaten year-round as a casual snack.
- Cuisine lineage: Native-American-tradition, Ohio-specific — a home-candy-making tradition rather than a restaurant dish.
- Regional form variation: Native to, and still most strongly identified with, Ohio specifically (named for the buckeye nut, the seed of Ohio's state tree) — not a documented tradition elsewhere in the Midwest at the same intensity, though the underlying peanut-butter-and-chocolate combination itself isn't unique to Ohio.

#### Dish: Cleveland Polish Boy
- Category: Everyday
- Cuisine lineage: **A genuine cross-cultural fusion worth stating explicitly** — Eastern European (Polish immigrant) kielbasa sausage combined with Southern-style barbecue traditions brought to Cleveland by Black migrants during the Great Migration, credited to a Black barbecue restaurateur (Mount Pleasant neighborhood, dated to the 1940s), not a Polish-American dish in the way its name might suggest to someone unfamiliar with the history. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — food-journalism tier sourcing, corroborated across multiple independent outlets, but not an institutional/primary-source-level citation]
- Regional form variation: Native to, and still specifically associated with, Cleveland — described by sources as the closest thing the city has to its own signature/unofficial dish, though it lacks the kind of single-chain commercial ubiquity Cincinnati chili has.
- Serving format: Sandwich/handheld
- Primary protein/composition: A whole link of kielbasa sausage in a bun, topped with a layer of french fries, a layer of barbecue sauce, and a layer of coleslaw — all three toppings stacked directly on the sausage inside the bun, not served as side dishes.
- Side dishes/condiments, and how served: French fries, BBQ sauce, and coleslaw are not optional add-ons here — they are structural components of the sandwich itself, piled directly on top of the sausage before the bun closes, a genuinely distinct construction from a standard hot dog or bratwurst with condiments on the side.
- Serving vessel: A hot dog-style bun, visibly overstuffed and bulging from the fries/slaw volume inside it.
- Visual/plating characteristics: A tall, visibly overstuffed sandwich — a plump, taut-cased kielbasa link (grilled or griddled, showing browned char marks/grill lines along its surface) as the base layer, topped with a visible pile of golden fried potato sticks, a glossy reddish-brown barbecue sauce coating and pooling around the fries, and a final, pale creamy-white layer of coleslaw on top, its shredded cabbage texture visibly distinct from the fries beneath it. **Texture/finish**: the kielbasa's casing should look taut and glossy with visible char/grill marks; the fries should read as a genuine mass of individual fry sticks, not a mash; the coleslaw should be visibly creamy/wet and pale, contrasting sharply against the barbecue sauce's dark glossy red-brown.
- Common confusion: Should not be confused with a plain Chicago-style or Coney-style hot dog (above) — the fries-and-coleslaw-as-sandwich-filling construction, rather than any topping combination on a plain dog, is the defining, checkable difference; also distinct from a generic bratwurst-and-sauerkraut plate, which has no fries or coleslaw and is typically plated rather than handheld.
- Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH overall — consistent across multiple independent food-journalism and public-radio sources, though not corroborated by an institutional/primary source at the same tier as, e.g., the Reuben or gyro entries in `us.md`
- Sources: [Wikipedia: Polish Boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Boy); [Ideastream Public Media — Cleveland's sandwich? Say hello to the Polish Boy](https://www.ideastream.org/community/2024-12-27/clevelands-sandwich-say-hello-to-the-polish-boy); [Cleveland Scene — The Polish Boy: A Brief History](https://www.clevescene.com/food-drink/the-polish-boy-a-brief-history-4703188/)
- Composition & proportions (§4.7) — one sandwich on foil [EDITORIAL unless tagged]:
  - What dominates: in side view, fries and slaw together ~55% of the height, bun ~25%, sausage ~15% (mostly hidden), sauce ~5% but covering the fries.
  - Components: kielbasa 1 link, ~15–20 cm, ~3 cm thick, grill-marked; bun a hot-dog bun or hoagie roll [MEDIUM — Wikipedia / Today, via search], barely closing; fries ~20–30 sticks piled on; BBQ sauce ladled over the fries; slaw a mound ~3–4 cm tall on top. Whole sandwich ~10–12 cm tall, close to the can's height.
  - Arrangement: sausage in the bun, fries across it, sauce over them, slaw crowning; fries and slaw spilling onto the foil.
  - Vessel: opened foil or a foam clamshell ~25 cm; a fork is plausible.

#### Dish: Indiana breaded pork tenderloin sandwich
- Category: Everyday
- Cuisine lineage: **A genuine, explicit cross-cultural translation worth stating directly, on the same pattern as the gyro/döner or the Reuben's Jewish-deli lineage elsewhere in this KB**: sources describe it as a direct American reinterpretation of German wiener schnitzel (a thin, breaded, pan-fried veal cutlet eaten with a knife and fork), re-conceived as a handheld sandwich to be eaten like a hamburger — German-American in origin, not a native-American-tradition invention from scratch.
- Regional form variation: Native to, and still overwhelmingly concentrated in, Indiana specifically — described by multiple sources as the state's de facto signature sandwich, reinforced by ongoing, currently-operating "Tenderloin Trail"-branded culinary tourism programs in several Indiana counties. Present in neighboring states in a thinner, more generic form, but Indiana's version and cultural intensity around it are the checkable differentiator.
- Serving format: Sandwich/handheld — no standard plated/knife-and-fork sibling documented; the entire concept is built around eating the schnitzel-derived cutlet by hand.
- History: Credited to Nick Freienstein, son of German immigrants, who opened a diner in Huntington, Indiana in 1908 and adapted wiener schnitzel into a handheld sandwich form. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — the single most consistently repeated origin account, treated with the same "claimed, not airtight" caution this project applies to other single-restaurant "first" claims]
- Primary protein: A whole pork tenderloin cutlet, pounded thin (roughly 1/8 to 1/4 inch), breaded in seasoned flour/cracker crumbs or breadcrumbs, and deep- or pan-fried until golden and crisp.
- Primary starch/accompaniment: A standard-size (not jumbo) hamburger bun — the ordinary bun size is deliberate, since the entire visual point of the dish depends on the meat dramatically exceeding the bun's diameter.
- Side dishes/condiments: Typically dressed simply — mayonnaise, mustard, chopped onion, pickle slices, and shredded lettuce are common, standard hamburger-style condiments; onion rings and a root beer float are cited as a classic accompanying order, a pairing custom rather than a plated side.
- Serving vessel: Plate (fast-casual/diner) or paper wrap/basket, with the cutlet's overhang typically left visible rather than trimmed to fit the bun.
- Visual/plating characteristics: See VISUAL & PLATING NORMS above for the full detail — in summary, a thin, wide, craggy golden-brown breaded cutlet, several inches wider in diameter than the standard bun beneath it, with the bread visible only as a small base/cap in the center of the sandwich rather than framing its full circumference.
- Common confusion: Should not be confused with a standard breaded/fried chicken sandwich (`us.md`'s national Fried Chicken Sandwich entry) — the pounded, wide, thin, dramatically bun-overhanging silhouette of the tenderloin is the primary checkable differentiator against a fried chicken sandwich's thicker, bun-sized-or-smaller cutlet; also distinct from a German schnitzel plate (a knife-and-fork dish with no bun at all) despite the shared culinary ancestry.
- Confidence: HIGH for the overhang/size signature and current cultural prominence; MEDIUM-HIGH for the specific 1908 origin claim
- Sources: [Wikipedia: Pork tenderloin sandwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pork_tenderloin_sandwich); [Visit Indiana — This diner is where the breaded pork tenderloin originated](https://visitindiana.in.gov/blog/post/this-diner-is-where-the-breaded-pork-tenderloin-originated/); [InsideHook — An Indiana Pork Tenderloin Sandwich Recipe to Make at Home](https://www.insidehook.com/food/make-indianas-beloved-pork-tenderloin-sandwich); [Festival Country Indiana — The Top Hoosier Tenderloins](https://www.festivalcountryindiana.com/the-top-hoosier-tenderloins-and-the-full-story); [Uncultured Palate — This Food in History #21 Pork Tenderloin Sandwich](https://www.unculturedpalate.com/this-food-in-history/pork-tenderloin-sandwich)
- Composition & proportions (§4.7) — one sandwich [HIGH for overhang, per entry; rest EDITORIAL]:
  - What dominates: from above, the breaded cutlet ~70% of the visible area, the bun crown ~25% at the centre, toppings ~5% peeking out.
  - Components: cutlet ~20–30 cm across (6–10+ in per entry) — two to two and a half times the can's height, ~0.3–0.6 cm thick, irregular; bun ~10 cm (standard), overhang ~7–10 cm each side; pickles 3–4 chips, onion, lettuce shreds, mustard/mayo mostly under the crown.
  - Arrangement: cutlet flat, bun centred, crown on; edges slightly curled.
  - Vessel fill: the cutlet overhangs a ~26 cm plate or fills a paper-lined basket; onion rings, if briefed, beside.
  - State cues: breading craggy, golden, dry-crisp; slight oil sheen.
  - Absent on purpose: a jumbo bun; a thick chicken-sandwich cutlet; a cutlet trimmed to the bun; knife-and-fork schnitzel plating.
  - Prompt-ready line: "A breaded pork tenderloin sandwich on a white plate: a thin, craggy, golden fried cutlet more than twice as wide as the can is tall, flaring far past an ordinary soft round bun set in its centre, a few pickle chips and onion peeking from under the bun. The plate is almost hidden by the cutlet."

#### Dish: Indiana sugar cream pie
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (an everyday "desperation pie" by origin, but also served at holidays/gatherings today)
- Cuisine lineage: Native-American-tradition, specifically traced to Quaker and Shaker farm communities in eastern Indiana.
- Regional form variation: Native to, and specifically identified with, Indiana — given informal/unofficial "state pie" status via a 2009 Indiana Senate resolution (the resolution passed the Senate but did not proceed to a full state-law designation, so "unofficial state pie" is the accurate framing). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Serving format: Plate/dessert-course slice, always served cold or at room temperature, never handheld.
- History: The earliest known recipe dates to 1816 (Indiana's statehood year); most closely associated with Quaker settlers in eastern Indiana between 1810-1825, and separately with regional Amish communities. Its "desperation pie" nickname reflects that it requires no seasonal fruit filling — just pantry staples available year-round regardless of harvest. A still-operating Winchester, Indiana bakery (founded 1944) is credited with popularizing it commercially at scale. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH for the origin community and dates; HIGH for the "desperation pie"/pantry-staple framing]
- Primary composition: A single-crust pie shell filled with a cooked custard of cream (or a cream/milk blend), sugar, vanilla, and a small amount of flour or cornstarch as a thickener, baked until set, and dusted with ground nutmeg on top before or after baking.
- Side dishes/condiments: Typically served plain, unaccompanied by ice cream or whipped cream in its traditional form — the plain, unadorned slice is the more traditional presentation.
- Visual/plating characteristics: See VISUAL & PLATING NORMS above (a single, unfussy pie crust filled with a pale, uniformly cream-colored, custard-smooth filling; a soft-set, jiggly-but-sliceable texture).
- Common confusion: Should not be confused with a generic vanilla custard pie or buttermilk pie (both real, coexisting American pie traditions with a similar pale, eggy-custard look) — sources don't offer a sharp, universally-agreed visual differentiator between sugar cream pie and buttermilk pie specifically, so this is flagged honestly as a real, unresolved look-alike pair rather than an invented distinction; the nutmeg-dusted top and the specific Indiana/Quaker origin story are the claim's real differentiators, not necessarily anything visually unique in a photograph. [CONFIDENCE: LOW-MEDIUM for the visual distinction from buttermilk pie specifically]
- Confidence: HIGH for origin/history and composition; MEDIUM for the finished-slice visual specifics
- Sources: [Food Republic — The Sweet History Of Indiana's Iconic Sugar Cream Pie](https://www.foodrepublic.com/1716323/sugar-cream-pie-history-indiana/); [Atlas Obscura — The Hoosier Specialty of Sugar Cream Pie](https://www.atlasobscura.com/foods/hoosier-desperation-sugar-cream-pie-indiana); [WRTV — National Pie Day: The history of Indiana's state pie](https://www.wrtv.com/lifestyle/food/national-pie-day-the-history-of-indianas-state-pie); [Wikipedia: Sugar cream pie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_cream_pie); [Indiana General Assembly — Senate Resolution 0059 (2009)](https://archive.iga.in.gov/2009/bills/SRESP/SR0059.html)
- Composition & proportions (§4.7) — one slice, or the whole pie [EDITORIAL throughout]:
  - What dominates: filling ~75% of the slice's visible area, crust ~25%.
  - Components: pie 9 in (~23 cm); slice 1/8, ~11 cm long; filling ~2.5–3 cm deep (about a quarter of the can's height); crimped rim ~1.5 cm; nutmeg fine brown flecks over the top.
  - Arrangement: slice on a small plate, tip toward camera, cut face showing smooth cream colour; whole pie behind, missing one slice.
  - State cues: surface lightly browned in patches; filling soft-set, faint jiggle at the tip.
  - Absent on purpose: whipped cream, meringue, ice cream, fruit, a lattice top.
  - Prompt-ready line: "One slice of sugar cream pie on a small white plate, about a quarter of the can's height: a smooth, pale cream-coloured custard filling, lightly browned in patches and flecked with ground nutmeg, in a simple golden crust with a crimped edge. The rest of the pie behind, one slice missing. No whipped cream, no fruit."
