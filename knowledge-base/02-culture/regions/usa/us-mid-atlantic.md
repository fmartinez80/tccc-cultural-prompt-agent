## FILE ROLE & METHOD

This is the second regional file built under the 13-file US structure decided in `us.md`. It holds the full, authoritative depth for the Philly cheesesteak (the only dish `us.md`'s national index pointed here for), plus a large body of genuinely new dishes researched specifically for this file.

**Internal structure — two zones, one file**, decided by hand after both research passes completed:

1. **Philadelphia & Pennsylvania** (including Delaware — see below) — Philadelphia proper and Pennsylvania Dutch Country.
2. **Chesapeake Bay** — Maryland, Washington DC, and Tidewater/coastal Virginia.

**Delaware's placement**: the Pennsylvania/Philadelphia research found Delaware's food culture largely overlapping with Pennsylvania's (scrapple is equally native to Delaware's Sussex County; the cheesesteak/hoagie tradition crosses the PA/DE border without a visible seam), plus one genuinely independent Delaware contribution (the Capriotti's Bobbie sandwich, Wilmington). The Chesapeake research did not cover Delaware at all (its scope was Maryland/DC/Virginia). **Decided: Delaware is folded into the Philadelphia & Pennsylvania zone**, on the strength of the sourced overlap finding — but Delaware's own coastal/Delaware Bay food culture, independent of its Pennsylvania-Dutch-adjacent inland identity, was not researched by either pass and is a real, logged gap, not something this file claims full coverage of.

**Virginia's placement — a real scope question, decided here rather than left open**: `us.md` scopes `us-south.md` to exclude Appalachia, Florida, and Louisiana Creole/Cajun, but never addressed whether Virginia belongs to the Mid-Atlantic or the South. The Chesapeake research found genuinely split evidence (a tourism consortium groups Virginia with the Mid-Atlantic; Wikipedia's own Southern-cuisine article names Tidewater cuisine as a Southern subregion; general cultural sourcing describes Virginia as primarily Southern-dialect) and recommended a geographic split rather than a single verdict. **Decided: split, following that recommendation** — Tidewater/coastal Virginia (Chesapeake-facing: Hampton Roads, the Northern Neck, the Eastern Shore, Smithfield) is treated as part of this file's Chesapeake Bay zone, since it shares the same blue crab, oyster, and Smithfield ham identity documented below. Inland/Southside/Piedmont Virginia (Brunswick stew's home territory, Virginia barbecue) belongs to `us-south.md` instead, where it will be picked up during that file's own research. This mirrors how New Mexico's split from the Desert Southwest was decided earlier in this project — a real internal distinction resolved by geography and evidence, not left to sit unresolved.

**Placement rule reminder (per `country-file-schema.md` §4.3)**: every dish below that had a thin index entry in `us.md` gets its full authoritative depth here; that summary is superseded by the entry below.

**Typical use note** (carried forward from `us-northeast.md`'s own calibration): expect the realistic use case to skew toward general, archetypal scenes — a Chesapeake crab house, a classic sandwich-stand counter, a rowhouse interior — more than hyper-local block-level staging. The quick-reference table below surfaces those first; the deeper city/neighborhood detail further down remains real and sourced but is depth to consult when a brief specifically calls for it.

## ZONE CHARACTERIZATIONS

### Philadelphia & Pennsylvania (including Delaware)

This zone clears the "would swapping norms into another Mid-Atlantic zone look wrong" test on two separate grounds. First, a dense cluster of independently dated, single-origin sandwich inventions with no equivalent elsewhere in the Mid-Atlantic: the cheesesteak (Pat Olivieri, 1930), the roast pork Italian sandwich (Domenico Bucci, 1930), and the Philadelphia soft pretzel's distinctive shape (Federal Pretzel Baking Company, 1920s mechanization) are each independently documented, dated, South Philadelphia/Italian-immigrant-and-Pennsylvania-Dutch-coded inventions — a different causal cluster from Chesapeake's seafood-driven identity or the Northeast's NYC deli/halal-cart cluster. Second, Pennsylvania Dutch Country (Lancaster/Berks and surrounding counties) is a real, geographically distinct rural region — German-speaking "Deutsch," anglicized "Dutch," 18th-century settlement — directly responsible for scrapple and the pretzel tradition, giving this zone a genuine urban/rural range (Philadelphia rowhouse density at one end, Lancaster County farm stands at the other) bridged concretely by Reading Terminal Market's Amish-vendor presence inside an urban market hall.

**Caricature-avoidance note**: ordinary suburban Pennsylvania, and the Pittsburgh area entirely outside this file's researched scope, look like unremarkable American suburbia per `us.md`'s national baseline — a Philadelphia-set brief shouldn't default to a cheesesteak stand or Amish farm stand any more than an NYC-set brief should default to Times Square.

### Chesapeake Bay (Maryland, DC, Tidewater Virginia)

The clearest anchor is the Chesapeake blue crab itself as a defined regional identity, not just a shared ingredient — the Bay's brackish water is repeatedly cited as the reason Chesapeake-caught crab is considered sweeter and more delicate than blue crab caught elsewhere along its wider Atlantic/Gulf range, and Maryland formally designated it the state crustacean in 1989. Layered on top is Old Bay seasoning's specific Baltimore immigrant-history origin — a signature reddish-orange, coarse, celery-salt-and-paprika seasoning register genuinely absent from New England's plain-butter shellfish register in `us-northeast.md`. A third thread, Chesapeake oyster culture (one translation of "Chesapeake" is "Great Shellfish Bay"), reinforces the same water-body identity from a second species. Washington DC layers a fourth, non-seafood thread on top: a Black carryout-counter food culture (half-smoke, mumbo sauce) with no equivalent in the crab-house register, giving this zone real internal texture the way `us-northeast.md`'s three sub-zones have.

**What this zone characterization is not claiming**: Maryland, DC, and Tidewater Virginia are not food-culturally identical — Maryland centers on Baltimore/Annapolis crab-house and Old Bay culture, DC on its carryout-counter tradition, Virginia's Tidewater on ham/peanut/oyster history. They're grouped because they share the same defining ingredient and water body. **Flagged, not yet resolved**: whether Maryland's Eastern Shore (rural, waterman-centric) and the Baltimore-Annapolis corridor (denser, restaurant-driven) eventually warrant their own internal split the way `us-northeast.md` splits its zones — logged in the Gap Log below rather than decided now.

## TRUSTED CONTENT — ZONE-WIDE NORMS

### Philadelphia & Pennsylvania

- **The hoagie is Philadelphia's official city-designated sandwich** — Mayor Ed Rendell named it so in 1992, additive to `us-northeast.md`'s already-established naming-regionalism entry (Philadelphia = "hoagie"). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Philadelphia Magazine — How Wawa Is Spreading the Gospel of Hoagies](https://www.phillymag.com/news/2015/05/03/wawa-expansion-hoagie-sub-hero-sandwich/)]
- **The convenience-store hoagie counter is a real, distinctly Philadelphia-area institution**, not a generic gas-station food case — a Pennsylvania-founded chain built specifically around made-to-order hoagies has a documented cult following specific to this region. **Prompt-writing note**: describe the generic category (a clean, brightly-lit convenience-store deli counter with a self-service ordering kiosk and a hoagie assembly line), never the chain's name or storefront branding. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Philadelphia Magazine](https://www.phillymag.com/news/2015/05/03/wawa-expansion-hoagie-sub-hero-sandwich/)]
- **Sharp provolone (not mild/generic) is a recurring cheese identity across this zone's sandwiches** — the defining cheese on the roast pork Italian sandwich and one of the three legitimate cheesesteak cheeses. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]

### Chesapeake Bay

- **Old Bay seasoning is the zone's single most cross-cutting seasoning norm, used far beyond crabs.** Created 1939–1940 in Baltimore by Gustav Brunn, a German-Jewish immigrant spice merchant recently released from a Nazi concentration camp; originally sold as "Delicious Brand Shrimp and Crab Seasoning," renamed after the Old Bay Line steamship company in 1945. McCormick & Company — the same Baltimore spice giant that had fired Brunn decades earlier for being Jewish — purchased the recipe outright in 1990. The blend is 18 herbs and spices including celery salt, red pepper, black pepper, and paprika. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Preservation Maryland — History of Old Bay](https://preservationmaryland.org/history-of-old-bay-spice-blend/); [JMORE — The Jewish Roots of Old Bay Seasoning](https://jmoreliving.com/2021/04/02/the-jewish-roots-of-old-bay-seasoning/)]
  - **Visual identity**: a bright reddish-orange, visibly coarse/granular seasoning (paprika drives the color, celery salt the texture) — a heavy, visible dusting of small reddish-orange flecks clinging to a wet or steamed surface is correct; a smooth, uniform surface is wrong. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- **Watermen culture is this zone's defining occupational/settlement thread** — parallel in function to Maine's lobstering economy in `us-northeast.md`, but tied to crabbing and oystering. The crab-house format itself emerged in the early 20th century once refrigeration let people eat crab away from the docks. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- **DC's carryout-counter tradition (half-smoke, mumbo sauce) is a genuinely distinct Black urban food-culture thread, not a seafood-adjacent one.** Mumbo sauce took hold in DC's Black-owned carryout counters from the 1960s onward, plausibly evolved from a Chicago-style barbecue sauce but developed its own sweeter, tangier register locally; by the 1980s it was the city's defining condiment for wings, fried chicken, and fries. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [AFRO American Newspapers — From mambo sauce to half smokes](https://afro.com/from-mambo-sauce-to-half-smokes-d-c-culture-shines-through-its-staple-foods/)]
- **Baltimore rowhouse architecture is a real, distinct, causally-explained built environment.** A defining housing type since the 1790s, with two specific checkable markers: white marble front steps (driven by local Cockeysville Marble availability) and Formstone, a patented (1937) faux-stone stucco cladding historically iconic to working-class rowhouse blocks. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Baltimore Heritage — Anatomy of a Rowhouse](https://baltimoreheritage.org/resources/anatomy-of-a-rowhouse/); [Wikipedia: Formstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formstone)]

## VISUAL & PLATING NORMS (cross-cutting)

### Philadelphia & Pennsylvania

- **Melted cheese in this zone's hot sandwiches tends toward "melded into the meat" rather than "a visible separate layer"** — true of the cheesesteak (cheese melds into the chopped steak, glossy/cohesive, not distinctly striped) and the roast pork sandwich (provolone melts into visible drips at the cut edge). Neither should show cold, unmelted, sharply-defined cheese slices.
- **Room-temperature vs. hot-and-melted is a genuine, checkable split**: the cheesesteak and roast pork sandwich are hot with actively melting cheese; Philadelphia tomato pie, by contrast, is served at room temperature with no melted-cheese layer at all. Getting this backward is a visible authenticity error.
- **Deep golden-brown/glossy vs. matte-crisp finishes should not be flattened into one generic "browned" description**: the Philadelphia soft pretzel is glossy (from its lye/baking-soda bath) with a deep golden-to-mahogany crust; scrapple is matte, with a hard, almost-blackened crisp crust from pan-frying, against a pale, soft, grainy interior once cut. Two distinct finishes, not interchangeable defaults.

### Chesapeake Bay

- **Steamed-crab red/orange is the zone's single most checkable color signature.** Live blue crabs are dull blue-green/olive; steaming with Old Bay turns the shell bright reddish-orange, with a visible speckled Old Bay dusting clinging to the wet shell — not a uniform smooth orange. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- **Old Bay dusting vs. no seasoning at all is the primary differentiator between this zone's crab-house register and `us-northeast.md`'s New England lobster-shack register** — New England serves chilled or butter-coated shellfish with no dry-spice dusting; Maryland crab houses show a heavy, visible reddish-orange seasoning coating. Depicting either register with the other's finish is a real, visible cross-region authenticity error.
- **Whole picked crab meat is never uniform or paste-like** — jumbo lump and backfin crabmeat both show distinct, irregularly-shaped white chunks with visible fibrous striations, whether loose or composed into a dish.
- **A crab cake's crust finish splits by cooking method, not by "well-made vs. sloppy."** A broiled crab cake shows little to no browned crust — pale, barely-tanned, matte, slightly domed. A pan-fried crab cake shows an evenly golden-to-deep-amber, crisp, thin crust on the flat surfaces, with the sides (never touching the pan) staying pale and craggy. Neither should look like a hamburger-style deep char — the crust should always be thin, and the cut interior should show large, distinct, barely-broken lump chunks, not a fine, uniform, meatloaf-like crumb. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]

## ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES — zone deltas from `us.md`'s national baseline

### Philadelphia & Pennsylvania

- **The classic South Philadelphia sandwich stand is a distinct counter-service setting, not a generic fast-food counter.** Two coexisting, equally-authentic registers exist: a plain, unadorned white storefront with a walk-up window and simple outdoor seating (the older, no-frills register), or a brightly neon-lit stand with dual order/pickup windows operating around the clock (a flashier, modern-signage register). Ordering is walk-up only, fast and terse, with cooks working a flat-top griddle in full view of the line. **Prompt-writing note**: any neon signage or menu board must stay unreadable/out-of-focus per the standing no-legible-text rule. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- **Counter-ordering culture is fast, terse, and jargon-driven** — regulars compress cheese/onion preference into a phrase ("one whiz wit"); hesitating at the counter reads as a visible tourist tell. A dialogue/behavior detail, not something to render as on-screen text. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- **Philadelphia's rowhouse density is genuinely distinct from NYC's brownstones already in `us-northeast.md`.** The "trinity" or "bandbox" house — a narrow (roughly 14 feet wide), three-or-four-story rowhouse with one room per floor and a low-headroom basement kitchen — is smaller and more vertically compressed than NYC's wider brownstones, originally built as affordable worker/artisan housing. A Philadelphia "meal at home" scene in the older, denser neighborhoods should default to this narrow, vertically-stacked register rather than NYC's wider brownstone or a generic suburban house. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the physical description] [SOURCE: [Atlas Obscura — Philadelphia's Trinity Houses are the Original Tiny Houses](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/philadelphias-trinity-houses-are-the-original-tiny-houses)]
- **Pennsylvania Dutch Country offers a genuinely distinct rural staging register** with no equivalent in Philadelphia proper or anything documented for the Northeast: a roadside farm stand with hand-lettered signage (kept unreadable per the no-legible-text rule), baskets of produce and baked goods, sometimes Amish- or Mennonite-run, set against rolling farmland with a covered bridge or barn rather than any urban streetscape — most relevant for staging scrapple in a genuinely rural context. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the setting's existence, travel-tier sourcing]
- **Reading Terminal Market genuinely bridges this zone's urban and rural registers** — an 1893-built historic market hall that, since a 1980s revitalization recruited Amish and Pennsylvania Dutch vendors from Lancaster County, hosts a dedicated Amish produce/baked-goods section alongside urban food stalls. **Prompt-writing note**: describe the generic category (a large historic indoor public market hall, mixed food stalls, high ceiling, no legible signage), not this specific market. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- **"Meal on the go" plausibly follows the same pedestrian/transit-scaled contrast with the national car-centric default that `us-northeast.md` documents for NYC** — a reasonable analogical extension, not independently re-quantified for Philadelphia this pass. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]

### Chesapeake Bay

- **The Chesapeake crab house is the zone's single most visually distinct "away from home" register**, and real sourced differences exist versus `us-northeast.md`'s New England seafood shack: brown wrapping paper (not a paper-lined plastic basket) covers the table; a wooden mallet plus a small knife/pick for extraction are standard tools, versus New England's already-extracted-meat or whole-but-untooled format; corn on the cob, coleslaw, and Old Bay-dusted steamed shrimp are common accompaniments; the setting is a casual, often waterfront building with outdoor or screened-porch picnic-table seating. **Prompt-writing note**: describe the generic category, not a specific named business. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- **Utensil-requirement check**: crabs are eaten entirely by hand/mallet/pick, but coleslaw, if served alongside, requires a fork — a full spread scene shouldn't default to "no utensils anywhere" just because the headline dish needs none.
- **A Baltimore-cast "meal at home" scene should default toward a narrow rowhouse interior** rather than the national detached-home default, with white marble front steps and, for an older working-class block, Formstone-clad fronts. **Caricature-avoidance note**: suburban Maryland and rural Eastern Shore Maryland are both real, common, non-rowhouse settings within the same state.
- **The DC half-smoke/mumbo-sauce register is most authentically staged at a walk-up carryout counter** — a menu board, a steam table or flat-top grill visible behind the counter, food in a styrofoam or paper container, rather than a sit-down restaurant. **Prompt-writing note**: describe a generic classic DC carryout/chili counter, not a specific named business. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the register's authenticity]
- **No zone-specific override found** for "meal outdoors at home" or "meal on the go" beyond the above — nothing in this pass's sourcing contradicts `us.md`'s national grill-culture or car-centric findings for this zone specifically.

### Philadelphia & Pennsylvania

#### Dish: Philly cheesesteak (full authoritative entry — pulls forward and expands `us.md`'s index entry, which pointed here)
- Category: Everyday
- Cuisine lineage: Native-American-tradition (Italian-American immigrant origin)
- Serving format: Sandwich/handheld only — no plated sibling
- Primary protein: Thinly sliced ribeye steak, chopped on the flat-top griddle
- Cheese: Molten cheese — provolone, American, or Cheez Whiz are all three legitimate, not just Whiz as popular caricature suggests
- Primary starch/accompaniment: Griddled onions; hoagie roll
- History: Credited to Pat Olivieri, who ran a hot dog stand at 9th and Passyunk in South Philadelphia; in 1930 he grilled a ribeye steak for his own lunch, chopped it, and put it in a roll with grilled onions — no cheese in this original version. Cheese was added roughly a decade later, in the 1940s, credited to a Pat's manager, Joe Lorenza, who began adding sliced provolone; Cheez Whiz (first sold commercially in 1952) was adopted at Pat's in the 1950s for speed of service. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Venue — real rival businesses cited for authenticity: two of the best-known cheesesteak stands sit directly across the street from each other at 9th and Passyunk, a real, dated, still-operating rivalry popularly (though not definitively) traced to publicity following a 1976 film. **Per the landmark rule, this history is evidence the "rival stands across the street" register is real — an actual prompt should describe a generic version of this setting, never name or reproduce either specific business.** [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the founding dates and rivalry facts; MEDIUM for the film-driven-escalation claim, described by sources as "legend"]
- Preparation detail — chopped vs. sliced, a real, sourced difference between the two named rival stands, useful as evidence both preparations are legitimate: one stand's steak is chopped fine and mixed loosely with onions and cheese; the other's is cut into thicker, more distinct slices layered rather than mixed through. Both are documented, coexisting preparations. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Roll detail: a specific, named, hearth-baked bakery roll is the most commonly cited standard — thin, light, crisp crust; soft-but-durable, tightly-plush crumb capable of holding hot filling without tearing or turning soggy. **Prompt-writing note**: describe a generic soft-crusted, hearth-baked Italian-style long roll, not the bakery's name. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Ordering culture: compressed counter-order phrasing ("wiz wit," "provolone witout") is a real, sourced, fast-paced verbal custom.
- Visual/plating characteristics: the roll's exterior is thin and lightly crisp, the interior soft/airy/chewy; melted cheese visibly melds into the chopped steak rather than sitting as a separate layer, giving the filling a glossy, cohesive look; caramelized onions appear as browned, translucent strands mixed through the meat, not raw white rings. **Texture/finish**: the meat should read as well-done and uniformly browned/gray-brown throughout — never pink or rare, since it's chopped fine on a screaming-hot flat-top rather than seared as a whole cut; the roll's crust should look thin, matte-to-lightly-sheened, and show visible tearing/give at the cut edge under the filling's weight rather than a clean, rigid cross-section.
- Common confusion: **the chopped cheese sandwich (NYC, `us-northeast.md`) is the single most likely confusable alternative** — a cheesesteak keeps thin-sliced steak as visible ribbons with cheese melted over/into them, while a chopped cheese is hand-chopped ground beef mixed into a coarser, uniformly marbled filling. Getting this backward is a real, visible error either direction.
- Confidence: HIGH overall
- Sources: [Smithsonian — History of Five Uniquely American Sandwiches](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/history-five-uniquely-american-sandwiches-180967078/); [Charleys Cheesesteaks — The History of the Original Cheesesteak](https://www.charleys.com/blog/the-history-of-the-cheesesteak/); [Tasting Table — Does A Philly Cheesesteak Need To Have Whiz?](https://www.tastingtable.com/1437458/does-philly-cheesesteak-need-whiz-truth-myth/); [Guide to Philly — Pat's vs Geno's](https://guidetophilly.com/pats-vs-genos-rivalry-cheesesteak/); [Tasting Table — Pat's Vs Geno's Cheesesteaks](https://www.tastingtable.com/2092490/pats-vs-genos-cheesesteaks-difference/); [Tasting Table — The Best Bread For Cheesesteaks Is A Philadelphia Classic](https://www.tastingtable.com/2207574/best-bread-for-cheesesteaks-philadelphia-classic-amorosos/)

#### Dish: Philadelphia soft pretzel (expands `us.md`'s national "Soft pretzel" entry)
- Category: Everyday snack, strongly city/street-vendor coded
- Cuisine lineage: German/Pennsylvania-Dutch-American (same as `us.md`'s entry)
- Regional form variation: **A genuine, additional, form-relevant detail** — Philadelphia's soft pretzel is distinctively flatter and wider than a classic hand-twisted German pretzel or a symmetrical bakery pretzel elsewhere — resembling a paper clip or a squashed figure-eight. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- History of the shape: credited to the Federal Pretzel Baking Company of South Philadelphia, founded 1922 by Maria and Giuseppe Nacchio, industrialized in the 1920s by their son Edmund, who combined hand-twisting with an imported conveyor-belt system that flattened/compressed the shape as a side effect of mechanization. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the shape-history claim; **one source names a different equipment supplier for the same 1933 mechanization — this specific company attribution is MEDIUM, though both agree Philadelphia mechanization in the 1920s–30s is the cause**]
- Side dishes/condiments: Yellow mustard is the standard, near-universal dip, one account attributing this to soft pretzels historically being sold from the same hot-dog carts that already stocked mustard. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM for the causal story; HIGH for the pairing itself]
- Visual/plating characteristics: A flattened, compressed, wide "paper clip" shape, shorter and wider than a tall, symmetrical loop knot. **Texture/finish**: the surface is glossy (from a lye or baking-soda bath before baking, distinct from a plain matte baked-bread surface) and deep golden-brown to mahogany, with coarse, unevenly-scattered visible salt crystals; the crumb is soft, dense, and chewy — closer to a bagel's chew than a light dinner roll, with no visible large air holes. Genuinely different from `us.md`'s "Hard pretzel (bagged)" sibling — matte, dry, and brittle rather than glossy and chewy.
- Common confusion: Should not be depicted with the tall, narrow, symmetrical twisted-loop shape of a generic mall-bakery or German-style pretzel — the flattened, wide, compressed shape is the checkable Philadelphia-specific differentiator.
- Confidence: HIGH overall; MEDIUM for the single-company mechanization attribution
- Sources: [Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia — Pretzels](https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/pretzels/); [Pennsylvania Center for the Book — Pennsylvania's Twist](https://pabook.libraries.psu.edu/literary-cultural-heritage-map-pa/feature-articles/pennsylvanias-twist); [The Takeout — The Thing That Sets Philly Pretzels Apart](https://www.thetakeout.com/2064008/philadelphia-pretzel-unique-shape/)

#### Dish: Scrapple
- Category: Everyday — traditionally breakfast, but genuinely also a lunch/snack item in Philadelphia specifically (a "scrapple sandwich," scrapple at lunch counters). **Flagged since `us.md`'s overall scope excludes breakfast**: scrapple's lunch/snack presence is real but secondary; stage it as a snack/lunch item, not a breakfast-plate scene. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM for the lunch/snack-format claim specifically]
- Cuisine lineage: German/Pennsylvania-Dutch-American — descended from "panhaas," a German meat pudding of pork scraps with buckwheat and herbs, brought by German-speaking immigrants who settled the Philadelphia area from 1683 onward. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Regional form variation: Native to, and still most strongly associated with, the Pennsylvania Dutch/Philadelphia region and Delaware's Sussex County specifically.
- Serving format: Plate (pan-fried slices, typically with eggs, breakfast form) or handheld/sandwich (fried scrapple on a roll, sometimes with egg and cheese)
- Primary protein/starch: Pork scraps and trimmings (historically including offal), simmered and shredded, bound with cornmeal and traditionally some buckwheat flour into a thick mush, seasoned with black pepper, sage, thyme, and savory, poured into a loaf pan and chilled firm. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Preparation for eating: The chilled loaf is sliced (roughly 1/4- to 3/4-inch) and pan-fried in fat until a hard, crisp crust forms on both cut faces — cooked hard and long enough that food writers describe the exterior as "nearly burnt," while the interior stays soft and creamy. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Side dishes/condiments: Ketchup is most common; maple syrup, apple butter, and (less commonly) mustard or honey are also documented, genuinely coexisting accompaniments.
- Visual/plating characteristics: Rectangular or half-moon-shaped slices, roughly a scant half-inch thick. **Texture/finish — the dish's single most important checkable trait**: the two pan-fried cut faces show a hard, matte (not glossy), deeply browned-to-near-black crisp crust — darker and harder than a typical pan-seared meat patty's sear, verging on over-cooked/charred at the very edge, which is correct and sought-after, not a mistake. The uncooked side edges stay a duller, unfried grayish-brown. Cut open, the interior is soft, moist, visibly coarse-grained/crumbly — a grainy, mottled grayish-brown throughout (not a smooth, uniform meat color), with small dark flecks of pepper and herb. A render showing scrapple as a smooth, sausage-like, uniform patty would misrepresent the dish. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the crisp-exterior/soft-interior contrast]
- Common confusion: Should not be depicted as a smooth, uniform breakfast sausage patty (no visible grain, uniform color) or corned beef hash (visible chunks of potato/meat rather than a smooth mush) — scrapple's fine, grainy, cornmeal-bound interior with visible pepper flecks is the confirming detail.
- Confidence: HIGH overall for composition/preparation/texture; MEDIUM for the lunch/snack serving-format claim
- Sources: [Taste Cooking — A Brief History of Pennsylvania Scrapple](https://tastecooking.com/brief-history-pennsylvania-scrapple/); [PA Eats — PA Food Icons: Scrapple](https://www.paeats.org/feature/pa-food-icons-scrapple/); [The Philadelphia Inquirer — Your complete guide to Pennsylvania's scrapple history](https://www.inquirer.com/philly-tips/best-scrapple-philadelphia-20210818.html); [Delaware Today — This Is the Cuisine That Defines Delaware](https://delawaretoday.com/food/delaware-cuisine/)

#### Dish: Philadelphia water ice
- Category: Everyday, strongly seasonal (a summer treat)
- Cuisine lineage: Italian-American — part of the broader Italian-ice tradition, developed into a distinct local style and name in Philadelphia specifically. - Category: Everyday — described by multiple food-journalism sources as a sandwich Philadelphians rank alongside, or even ahead of, the cheesesteak. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — journalism-tier commentary, not a quantified survey]

[CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]

- Side dishes/condiments: Sharp aged provolone, melted directly onto the hot pork; broccoli rabe (sautéed with garlic, notably bitter) or, in the older version, sautéed spinach — both real, coexisting choices per §4.6; a light drizzle of the pork's own gravy spooned over just before serving.

- Common confusion: Most easily confused with (1) a cold Italian sub/hoagie (this sandwich is hot, with melted cheese and gravy-moistened meat); (2) Chicago Italian beef (`us-northeast.md`) — Italian beef is fully dip-soaked/dripping with no cheese, while this sandwich is pork, always includes melted sharp provolone, and is only lightly moistened.
- Confidence: HIGH for basic composition and current form; MEDIUM for the origin narrative and exact cut of pork

#### Dish: Maryland crab cake
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (served at both casual crab houses and upscale restaurants)
- Cuisine lineage: Native-American-tradition, with a documented pre-colonial Indigenous root — Powhatan and other Indigenous peoples in the 1600s already roasted/boiled blue crabs in the shell; European settlers refined this into a picked-meat, breadcrumb-bound cake starting in the early 19th century. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- Serving format: **Two genuine, coexisting formats per §4.4** — a plated crab cake (one or two cakes, a side, often on a bed of lettuce with lemon and tartar sauce, knife-and-fork) and a crab cake sandwich (a single cake on a bun, handheld). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM for the sandwich format's relative prevalence, not independently researched to the same depth]
- Primary protein: Blue crab meat, graded — **jumbo lump** (whole, largest muscle segments, the premium grade, large intact white chunks) and **backfin** (smaller, flakier pieces, still sweet, less chunky, more affordable). A documented grading-hierarchy inversion: as recently as the 1980s, "backfin lump" was considered the top grade; today jumbo lump outranks it. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Primary starch/binder: Very little filler — a small amount of egg, mustard, mayonnaise, crumbled saltines or breadcrumbs, and Old Bay, just enough to bind the lump meat rather than pad it out. A cake heavy with visible breadcrumb filler is a real, documented departure from tradition. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Side dishes/condiments: Tartar sauce is standard; a lemon wedge is common; served over a bed of lettuce in the classic plated presentation; Old Bay often sprinkled on top in addition to being mixed into the cake.
- Visual/plating characteristics: **A crab cake's crust finish splits by cooking method — see the zone-wide Visual & Plating Norms above.** Beyond crust: the cake is lightly domed rather than flattened like a hamburger patty, distinctly smaller in diameter than a standard burger bun but taller/thicker in profile; the cut or broken-open interior shows large, distinct, barely-broken lumps of white crabmeat with only a light, pale, barely-visible binder — never a dense, uniform, meatloaf-like interior. Pale ivory-white crabmeat throughout, exterior crust ranging from barely-tanned pale gold (broiled) to deeper golden-amber (pan-fried), with a light reddish-orange Old Bay dusting visible either way.
- Common confusion: Should not be depicted as a generic "seafood cake" or crab imperial (a related but structurally different creamy-sauced casserole, not independently researched to this file's standard) — the defining, checkable difference is the visible dominance of large, distinct lump chunks over any binder or filler.
- Confidence: HIGH overall for composition, grading terminology, and the light-filler norm; MEDIUM for the origin narrative and sandwich format's prevalence
- Sources: [Old Line Plate — Crab Cakes: True History Of](https://oldlineplate.com/crabcakes-true-history-of/); [Sizzlefish — A History of Maryland Crab Cakes](https://www.sizzlefish.com/blogs/news/a-history-of-maryland-crab-cakes); [Savory Simple — Broiled Jumbo Lump Maryland Crab Cakes](https://www.savorysimple.net/authentic-maryland-jumbo-lump-crab-cakes/)

#### Dish: Steamed blue crabs (the Maryland crab feast)
- Category: Special-occasion to everyday-in-season (a defining summer social ritual, priced/portioned as a group event)
- Cuisine lineage: Native-American-tradition, same pre-colonial Indigenous root as the crab cake
- Serving format: Whole animal, shell-on, communal — the ritual of picking crabs at a shared table is as much the point as the meat itself
- Primary protein: Whole live blue crabs, steamed (never boiled — boiling would wash out the seasoning), typically over a vinegar-and-beer or vinegar-and-water base, each layer generously coated in Old Bay and salt before the next layer goes on. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Side dishes/condiments: Corn on the cob and coleslaw are the most commonly cited standard sides; Old Bay-seasoned steamed shrimp is a common companion order; melted butter and/or extra Old Bay for dipping are common at the table. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Serving vessel: **No plate at all in the classic form** — crabs are piled directly on a brown-paper-covered (or, at home, newspaper-covered) table, the paper itself the serving surface, later gathered up with the shell debris.
- Venue variation: A dedicated crab house, or a home backyard/deck version using the same paper-covered-table convention — one of relatively few dishes in this file where the home and away-from-home versions look nearly identical.
- Utensils/eating customs: Wooden mallets to crack claws, small knives or picks for tight cavities, bare hands for the bulk of the work — a tool-and-hands-driven, unhurried, social process; bibs are commonly worn. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Visual/plating characteristics: A pile of steamed crabs reads as bright orange-red shells (a sharp contrast to their live blue-green color) flecked visibly with reddish-orange Old Bay residue, pooled in ridges and leg joints; the brown paper beneath develops a scattered, informal mess of seasoning dust, cracked shell fragments, and crab "mustard" (the hepatopancreas, a soft yellow-orange substance some diners eat and others discard) as the meal progresses — a genuinely messy, hands-on table is the correct, authentic look. Wooden mallets and picks scattered among the shells, paper towels, and beverage cans/bottles are standard accompanying objects.
- Common confusion: Should not be confused with a New England lobster bake/clambake (`us-northeast.md`) — a different shellfish, melted butter with no dry-seasoning register, and typically already-cracked shellfish rather than whole live-steamed crabs requiring mallets.
- Confidence: HIGH overall for ritual format, tools, and Old Bay/color-change detail; MEDIUM for the brown-paper-vs-newspaper convention specifically
- Sources: [Baltimore Magazine — How to Throw the Perfect Crab Feast](https://www.baltimoremagazine.com/section/fooddrink/how-to-throw-the-perfect-crab-feast/); [Cravin' Crabs — The Core of a Crab Feast](https://cravincrabs.com/the-core-of-a-crab-feast-exploring-what/); [Soul of America — Maryland Crab Tradition](https://www.soulofamerica.com/us-cities/annapolis/maryland-crab-tradition/); [McCormick — OLD BAY Steamed Blue Crabs](https://www.mccormick.com/blogs/old-bay-recipes/old-bay-steamed-blue-crabs)

#### Dish: Smithfield ham / Virginia ham (Tidewater Virginia)
- Category: Special-occasion (a traditional holiday-table dish) to everyday (as ham biscuits)
- Cuisine lineage: Native-American-tradition, specifically Tidewater Virginia; a form of country ham but legally distinguished from generic country ham by geography
- Regional form variation: **Legally, geographically form-defining, a genuine parallel to `us.md`'s already-documented Hatch chile geographic designation.** Since 1926, Virginia state law has required that any ham labeled "Genuine Smithfield" be cut from hogs raised on the peanut-belt of Virginia or North Carolina, and cured/treated/smoked/processed specifically within the town of Smithfield, Virginia — sitting on the James River near the mouth of the Chesapeake Bay, tying this dish to the same water body anchoring this zone's other content. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- History: Traced to a mid-1700s Smithfield resident who began salt-curing and smoking hams; the earliest documented commercial sale is a 1779 receipt for export to the Dutch Caribbean. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Serving format: **Two genuine, coexisting formats per §4.4** — a whole cured ham, sliced and plated as a holiday main (special-occasion, knife-and-fork), and the **ham biscuit** — a thin slice of the same ham in a small, single-bite biscuit, an everyday/party-tray format completely different in scale and context.
- Visual/plating characteristics: **Whole ham**: a dark, dry, deeply mahogany-brown-to-blackish exterior rind (from long aging/smoking — distinctly darker and drier than a glazed, pale "city ham"), sliced to reveal a dense, dark reddish-brown interior with minimal visible marbling compared to a fresh-cooked pork roast — lean and firm-textured rather than moist and pull-apart-tender. **Ham biscuit**: a small, pale golden-brown, slightly craggy buttermilk biscuit (flaky, layered cross-section when broken) with a thin, dark reddish-brown, almost translucent-at-the-edges slice of ham peeking out — the ham slice should not extend much beyond the biscuit's own edges, a thin single slice rather than a thick stack.
- Common confusion: Should not be confused with a generic pink, moist, glazed "holiday ham" — the dry, dark, dense, salty country-ham form is the defining difference. Should also not be confused with a generic fast-food breakfast biscuit — the ham biscuit's ham is thin-sliced and dark reddish-brown/aged-looking, not a thick, pale, griddled breakfast ham steak.

#### Dish: Chesapeake oysters (raw bar / on the half shell)
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (a raw-bar staple; historically a cheap, everyday food, now often positioned as a more special/upscale order)
- Cuisine lineage: Native-American-tradition, Chesapeake-specific (*Crassostrea virginica*)
- Regional form variation: Prevalence/identity-coded rather than form-changing — one translation of the Algonquian word "Chesapeake" is "Great Shellfish Bay," and the bay's oyster population was reportedly so vast at the time of the 1607 Jamestown colonization that period accounts describe "literally trillions" present. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the species identity and historical abundance; MEDIUM for the specific etymological claim]
- Serving format: Raw, on the half shell, at a raw bar — the format most relevant to this file's staging purpose; oysters are also served stewed, fried, or in stuffing, but the half-shell presentation is the most visually distinct format worth a dedicated entry.
- Serving vessel: A large plate or platter of crushed/shaved ice, oysters nestled cut-side-up in their bottom (cupped) shell, arranged in a circle or fan.
- Visual/plating characteristics: Each oyster shows a pale, grayish-beige, glistening, translucent-at-the-edges body sitting in the deep, cupped half of a rough, ridged, dull grayish-white-to-brown exterior shell — a genuinely irregular, non-uniform shell shape from oyster to oyster, nested in bright white crushed ice for contrast. **Texture/finish**: the oyster meat should look wet/glistening and slightly firm-but-yielding, never dry or shriveled at the edges (a sign of sitting too long after shucking) — freshness is itself a visual cue worth getting right.
- Common confusion: Should not be confused with a West Coast oyster variety (smaller, deeper-cupped, often more strongly flavored) — not independently confirmed as a specific visual comparison this pass, flagged as plausible but unconfirmed.
- Confidence: HIGH for species identity, historical abundance, and general presentation; MEDIUM for the etymological claim and Chesapeake-vs-West-Coast visual comparison
