## FILE ROLE & METHOD

This is the twelfth regional file in the 13-file US structure (`us.md` FILE ROLE & METHOD table, row 12). **Scope, per `us.md`'s table: Oregon and Washington state.** Unlike several prior regional files, `us.md`'s national index currently carries **no dish-level pointers into Pacific Northwest content at all** — this file is not resolving deferred pointers, it is building almost entirely new material from scratch, the same "no `us.md` pointer exists" situation `us-west-coast.md` was in for its own California-native content (California burrito, Kogi tacos, etc.), just at a larger scale here since there is no partial existing entry to build on for anything in this file.

**Boundary inheritance from `us-west-coast.md`, explicitly respected, not re-litigated.** That file's own FILE ROLE & METHOD and GAP LOG flagged three items as deliberately deferred to this file, and this file claims them here for the first time: Dungeness crab as a defining regional identity, Pacific salmon traditions (cedar-plank cooking, salmon bakes), and Seattle/Pacific Northwest coffee culture. Two boundary findings that file surfaced are respected here rather than re-claimed: (1) **San Francisco's clam-chowder-in-a-sourdough-bread-bowl pairing is a documented California claim, not a Pacific Northwest one** — this file's own Pacific Northwest clam chowder entry below is deliberately never staged in a hollowed sourdough bread-bowl format; it is served in an ordinary bowl or mug, consistent with this region's own real, sourced serving convention. (2) **Oakland, California — not Seattle — is the documented birthplace of Blue Bottle Coffee**, per `us-west-coast.md`'s own boundary flag; this file does not claim Blue Bottle, or credit Seattle with inventing third-wave coffee outright, since Oakland has an independent, real claim to part of that same movement. This file's own coffee-culture content (see ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES) documents Seattle's own real, independently-sourced coffee history (Starbucks' 1971 founding, the world's first espresso cart, Seattle's own early third-wave pioneers) without overclaiming exclusive ownership of the broader West Coast coffee-culture story.

**Beverage scope**: lunch, dinner, and snacks only (breakfast excluded, consistent with every other US regional file); inclusion by staging relevance, not raw consumption frequency. **No beverage other than Coca-Cola is cataloged as a dish subject** — this excludes both the region's real, extensive craft-beer and wine culture (Willamette Valley wine country) and coffee itself as a beverage, the same beverage-scope exclusion `us-west-coast.md` applied to Napa/Sonoma wine country. A coffeehouse, roastery, or drive-through espresso stand is documented here **only as a staging environment/venue register** — the physical space, not a beverage catalog entry — the same "food-only dining register" treatment `us-west-coast.md` gave Napa/Sonoma restaurants.

**Structural question tested, not assumed: does Washington/Seattle need to split from Oregon/Portland into two files?**

This file ran the same swap test used throughout this project (`country-file-schema.md` §1.1: "would swapping this sub-region's norms into the other look visibly wrong to someone who knows the place?") on Washington/Seattle vs. Oregon/Portland, rather than assuming either a split or a merge going in.

**Finding: one file, with zone-level callouts and dish-level variant handling — the same resolution this project has consistently reached for Texas's four BBQ styles, Arizona's Tucson/Phoenix gradient, and California's NorCal/SoCal split, not a further file split.**

Evidence for a genuine, real gradient (this is not a "no difference found" result):
- Washington/Seattle's most staging-distinctive content is **maritime/seafood-and-coffee-coded**: Dungeness crab, Pacific salmon, Pacific oysters, the Seattle-style teriyaki bowl, Ballard's Scandinavian-immigrant fishing-industry heritage, Puget Sound ferry geography, and the region's own, real coffee-industry origin story (Starbucks' 1971 founding; the world's first drive-thru espresso stand opened in *Portland*, not Seattle, complicating a clean state-line split further — see ENVIRONMENT below).
- Oregon/Portland's most staging-distinctive content is **agriculture-and-DIY-food-culture-coded**: the Willamette Valley produce/marionberry/hazelnut economy, Portland's dense, community-run food-cart-pod culture, and Portland's eccentric/artisan doughnut scene.
- A journalism source directly comparing the two states' food identities (Wanderlust, "The flavours of West Coast USA") independently converges on almost exactly this same split: Washington "highlights premium seafood and coffee culture," Oregon "emphasizes innovation, foraging, and diverse food trucks" — this is a real, externally observed pattern, not a construct of this file's own framing. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — a single detailed journalism-tier comparison, not an academic culinary-geography study]

Evidence against a further split (why this doesn't clear the bar New Mexico cleared against Arizona, or Scotland cleared against the UK):
- **No dedicated Wikipedia article or specialist-institutional source treats "Washington cuisine" and "Oregon cuisine" as two separate named traditions.** The consistent framing across encyclopedic and journalism sources (including a dedicated Seattle Times feature explicitly titled "What is Pacific Northwest cuisine?") is a single named regional tradition — "Pacific Northwest cuisine" — that both states' food writers, chefs, and institutions (the James Beard Foundation's own origin story is anchored in Portland-born James Beard's boyhood memories of *both* Oregon wild huckleberries *and* shared Pacific Coast Dungeness crab/razor clams) treat as one thing, not two. This is the opposite pattern from New Mexico vs. Arizona, where a specialist culinary school explicitly framed the comparison as "Tex-Mex vs. New Mexican," a narrower, state-specific grain. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- **The two states share one, single, overwhelming causal engine for their food identity — Pacific salmon, the Pacific Coast fishery, and the temperate-rainforest/evergreen-forest Cascadia ecosystem — that neither state's version would look "visibly wrong" without.** Unlike Arizona/New Mexico's split (different primary starch — wheat vs. corn tortillas — and a genuinely different chile-sauce grammar), Washington and Oregon do not differ in cuisine *grammar*: both are built on the same wild Pacific salmon runs, the same evergreen-rainforest climate and geography, and largely the same Coast Salish/Chinookan Indigenous salmon-culture substrate (this file does not find, and does not claim, a Washington-specific vs. Oregon-specific *Indigenous* salmon tradition distinct enough to warrant separate treatment — the same tribal nations' traditional territories cross the Columbia River border that divides the two states). The difference is one of **specific signature dishes and institutions** (teriyaki bowls vs. artisan doughnuts; a coffee-industry hub vs. a food-truck-pod hub) layered on top of one shared ecological and culinary foundation — exactly the kind of variation this project's schema (§4.2) calls "prevalence/emphasis," handled as dish-level variant tables and zone callouts, not the "form-changing, different-cuisine-family" bar that justified New Mexico's split.
- **Practical/project-convention consideration**: unlike Arizona's Navajo Nation problem (a reservation genuinely spanning three states, which argued against a clean state-line file), Washington and Oregon are each fully within this file's own two-state scope — there is no cross-border-nation complication cutting against a split the way there was for Arizona. But this cuts both ways: it removes an argument *for* keeping them together (no external constraint forcing it) just as cleanly as it removes an argument *against* — so the deciding factor here is the cuisine-grammar test above, not this consideration.

**Decision: one file (`us-pacific-northwest.md`), covering both states, with Washington/Seattle and Oregon/Portland content organized as explicit zone callouts throughout (ZONE CHARACTERIZATION below) and any form-changing dish variation disclosed via the standard `country-file-schema.md` §4.2/§4.6 variant mechanism.** This was submitted as a research recommendation with cited evidence and **accepted by the orchestrating session** on the strength of that reasoning, consistent with this project's established precedent for internal-gradient cases (Texas, Arizona, California) — logged transparently in `DECISIONS.md` per `country-file-schema.md` §7, so a human reviewer can revisit if a future pass finds Washington-specific or Oregon-specific Indigenous salmon-culture content deep enough to constitute a genuinely different cuisine-grammar tradition.

**Typical use note** (carried forward from prior regional files' own calibration): expect the realistic use case to skew toward general, archetypal scenes — a Seattle waterfront crab shack, a Portland food-cart pod, a historic public produce-and-seafood market, a Puget Sound ferry deck — more than hyper-local block-level staging.

## ZONE CHARACTERIZATION

The Pacific Northwest file clears the "would swapping this region's norms into elsewhere in the US look visibly wrong" test on multiple independent, causally-explained grounds — and, per the FILE ROLE & METHOD swap test above, resolves its own internal Washington/Oregon variation as zone callouts within this one file rather than a further split.

**A temperate-rainforest climate and evergreen-conifer forest ecosystem with no equivalent anywhere else in this project's US files.** The region west of the Cascade mountain range receives some of the heaviest rainfall in the continental US (roughly 50–200 inches per year in parts of the coastal temperate rainforest), producing a persistently moist, overcast climate and forests dominated by Douglas fir, western hemlock, and western red cedar, with red alder, Sitka spruce, and bigleaf maple in wetter pockets — some trees in the old-growth rainforest exceed 1,000 years old. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Defenders of Wildlife — Temperate Rainforest](https://defenders.org/wild-places/temperate-rainforest); [USDA Climate Hubs — Northwest Forests and Woodlands](https://www.climatehubs.usda.gov/hubs/northwest/topic/northwest-forests-and-woodlands)] The Cascade Range itself is a volcanic mountain chain running through both Washington and Oregon, including the tallest, most heavily glaciated peak in the contiguous US (over 14,000 feet, carrying more than 25 named glaciers) — a real, dramatic, snow-capped volcanic-peak backdrop genuinely unlike any other US regional file's landscape documented so far. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] **Per `country-file-schema.md` §7.5's landmark-avoidance rule**, this file does not name this specific peak — describe it generically as "a glaciated, snow-capped volcanic peak visible on the horizon," which conveys the same real, sourced landscape category without reproducing one specific named natural landmark's identifiable silhouette as a literal prompt subject. [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: Cascades (ecoregion)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cascades_(ecoregion))] This climate/geography is the direct causal seed of this file's Dungeness crab, Pacific salmon, and Pacific oyster content below, and is shared identically by both Washington and Oregon — it is not a Washington-specific or Oregon-specific feature, which is itself part of why this file resolves as one region rather than two (see FILE ROLE & METHOD).

**A deep, well-documented Indigenous salmon culture — Coast Salish, Chinookan, and other Pacific Northwest nations' traditional fisheries — that predates, and causally explains, both states' modern seafood identity.** Coast Salish peoples have harvested Puget Sound's shellfish and salmon for thousands of years, incorporating them into ceremony and communal gathering long before European settlement; salmon specifically functioned as a foundational "First Food," with salmon runs shaping seasonal rhythms, subsistence systems, and origin stories across the region. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [48° North — Pinch Me: Chasing Crabs in Puget Sound](https://48north.com/instructional/history/pinch-me-chasing-crabs-in-puget-sound/); [CRITFC — Tribal Salmon Culture](https://critfc.org/salmon-culture/tribal-salmon-culture/)] This culture persists institutionally and legally, not only historically: the 1974 *United States v. Washington* ruling (the "Boldt Decision") affirmed that treaty tribes retained the right to fish in their traditional territories and to a full equal share of the harvestable salmon catch, a landmark of tribal sovereignty still actively co-managed with the state today. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH — a federal court ruling, corroborated by the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service's own retrospective] [SOURCE: [U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service — 50 Years of the Boldt Decision](https://www.fws.gov/story/50-years-boldt-decision); [Puget Soundkeeper Alliance — Tribal Treaty Rights and Salmon](https://pugetsoundkeeper.org/2024/07/15/tribal-treaty-rights-and-salmon-the-legacy-of-the-boldt-decision/)] **Indigenous-heritage sourcing discipline, following this project's own established precedent** (`us-appalachia.md`'s ramps entries; `us-new-mexico.md`'s Pueblo-bread entry; `us-arizona.md`'s Navajo/Hopi/Tohono O'odham entries; `us-hawaii.md`'s Kanaka Maoli entries): where this file attributes a tradition to Coast Salish or other Pacific Northwest Indigenous nations specifically, it distinguishes institutional/tribal-commission-tier sourcing (the Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission, CRITFC — a formal agency of four member tribal nations; the federal court record and U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service retrospective on the Boldt Decision) from journalism/enthusiast-tier sourcing (regional lifestyle and recipe outlets), rather than smoothing the two together into one undifferentiated confidence level. CRITFC and the Boldt Decision material sit at the stronger end of this file's sourcing; the cedar-plank cooking technique's specific historical practice details (see DISH CATALOG) rest more heavily on journalism/enthusiast-tier synthesis and are flagged accordingly in that entry.

**A distinct, dated, large-scale Scandinavian/Nordic immigration wave into Seattle's maritime and fishing economy — a real, sourced settlement pattern with no equivalent in this project's other West Coast files.** Norwegian, Swedish, Danish, Finnish, and Icelandic immigrants arrived in the Pacific Northwest in large numbers beginning in the late 19th century, drawn by a landscape reminiscent of home (fjords, forests, mountains) and by work in fishing, logging, and boatbuilding; by 1910, Scandinavians made up more than 30% of Washington State's foreign-born population — the state's single largest immigrant group at the time — and Norwegians specifically came to dominate large segments of the Pacific Coast fishing industry, holding roughly 40% of Pacific Coast and Alaska fishermen's-union membership by 1908. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [HistoryLink.org — Norwegians in Seattle and King County](https://www.historylink.org/File/3476); [Visit Seattle — Nordic Heritage](https://visitseattle.org/things-to-do/arts-culture/cultural-heritage/nordic/)] Seattle's Ballard neighborhood became, and remains, the visible anchor of this heritage — a real, checkable, non-caricature immigrant-settlement story structurally different in mechanism from California's Pacific Rim immigration waves (`us-west-coast.md`) or the Southwest's Mexican-border settlement patterns (`us-arizona.md`, `us-new-mexico.md`): this is a labor-and-industry-driven, climate-affinity-driven Northern European immigration, not a border relationship or a Gold-Rush-style sudden influx.

**A large-scale, causally distinct agricultural economy anchoring Oregon's own zone identity — the Willamette Valley's produce, berry, and nut economy — parallel in role to California's Central Valley/Salinas produce economy but built on a genuinely different climate and crop mix.** The Willamette Valley's climate and soils are specifically credited as ideal for trailing blackberry cultivars; the marionberry (a cross of the Chehalem and Olallie blackberries, bred by USDA plant breeder George Waldo, first crossed in 1945 and released in 1956, named for Marion County where it was extensively field-tested) is now the most widely planted trailing blackberry cultivar in the world, with more than 90% of its worldwide acreage grown in Oregon specifically. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Oregon Encyclopedia — Marionberry](https://www.oregonencyclopedia.org/articles/marionberry/); [Oregon Raspberries & Blackberries — Marionberry](https://oregon-berries.com/variety/marionberry/)] Oregon separately accounts for roughly 99% of all US hazelnut production, an even more extreme national dominance than the marionberry's own — a real, sourced, checkable agricultural-economy fact on the scale of Idaho potatoes or Florida citrus, grounding Oregon's produce-forward food identity in hard economic data rather than a vague "farm-to-table" gesture. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [USDA — Measuring Hazelnuts in Oregon](https://www.usda.gov/about-usda/news/blog/measuring-hazelnuts-oregon); [KGW — Oregon produces 99% of the US hazelnut supply](https://www.kgw.com/article/life/food/fresh-from-oregon/oregon-hazelnuts-99-percent-us-supply/283-22c4a9a8-d8d1-405e-8485-43b7be8db736)]

**Internal variation, tested rather than assumed: the Washington/Seattle vs. Oregon/Portland gradient is real (see FILE ROLE & METHOD's swap-test finding above) but resolves as zone callouts and dish-level variant tables inside this one file, the same treatment this project gave Texas's BBQ styles, Arizona's Tucson/Phoenix gradient, and California's NorCal/SoCal split — not a further file split.** Concretely: Seattle/Washington content in this file skews maritime and coffee-industry-coded (Dungeness crab, Pacific salmon, Pacific oysters, the Seattle-style teriyaki bowl, Ballard's Scandinavian fishing-industry heritage, the ferry/Puget Sound built environment, coffeehouse/espresso-stand culture); Portland/Oregon content skews agriculture-and-DIY-food-scene-coded (the marionberry and hazelnut economy, the food-cart-pod phenomenon, the eccentric/artisan doughnut register). Both share the same underlying rainforest-climate/evergreen-forest/Cascade-volcano landscape and the same broad Pacific-salmon-culture substrate documented above — the differences are in specific signature dishes and institutions layered on a shared foundation, not a different cuisine grammar.

**What this zone characterization is not claiming.** The Pacific Northwest is not internally uniform, and this file does not treat it as such. **Caricature-avoidance note (editorial judgment, not a sourced claim):** not every Pacific Northwest scene should default to a rain-soaked, flannel-and-espresso cliché, and not every Seattle scene needs a houseboat or a ferry in frame — the region's population is largely suburban and urban like the rest of the country, and an ordinary suburban Puget Sound kitchen or a Portland apartment is at least as representative as a waterfront or forest-backdrop scene. Equally, avoid treating Washington and Oregon as fully interchangeable just because they share one file — a Seattle-set brief calling for a teriyaki bowl and a Portland-set brief calling for an artisan doughnut are not swappable, even though both draw on the same regional foundation.

## VISUAL & PLATING NORMS

- **Doneness convention for salmon**: Pacific Northwest salmon (whether cedar-planked, grilled, or otherwise cooked) should read as opaque, uniformly light-pink-to-coral throughout with visible large, moist flakes when pulled apart with a fork — not translucent/raw-looking at the center, but not dried-out or pale gray either. This is a checkable, non-negotiable doneness marker distinguishing a properly cooked Pacific Northwest salmon dish from an undercooked (still translucent/glossy at the center) or overcooked (chalky, pale, dry) result.
- **Dungeness crab's color transformation is a checkable, sourced authenticity marker**: raw/live Dungeness crab is a mottled purplish-brown; once cooked, it turns a uniform brick-red-to-orange across the entire shell — a whole cooked crab should never be depicted with any of its raw purplish coloring remaining. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Down to Earth Cuisine — Dungeness Crab in the Pacific Northwest](https://www.downtoearthcuisine.com/dungeness-crab-in-the-pacific-northwest/)]
- **Texture/finish specifically**: a cedar-plank salmon fillet's surface should show light grill/plank scoring or a subtle char at the edges where it contacted the hot plank, not a fully seared, deeply browned crust like a pan-seared steak — the plank cooks the fish gently via indirect heat and imparted smoke rather than direct high-heat searing. Dungeness crab meat itself, once picked, is bright white with occasional coral-pink shell-adjacent streaks, in fine, moist, slightly stringy flakes — visually and texturally distinct from the denser, more uniform white meat of a Gulf Coast blue crab or a Maine lobster. A Pacific oyster's raw interior should look plump, glossy, and translucent-gray with a pool of its own briny liquor visible in the shell's cupped half; a properly shucked oyster should never look dry or shrunken away from its shell's edge. Marionberry filling in a pie or cobbler should read as a deep, near-black purple — distinctly darker and more saturated than a standard blackberry or blueberry filling — with whole or partially-burst berries visible rather than a smooth, fully-pureed filling.
- **Serving-vessel convention, restated per §4.5's fallback rule**: this file's dishes lean toward casual, communal, or disposable-takeout presentation (a whole crab on newspaper or a large platter; a teriyaki bowl in a styrofoam clamshell; oysters on a bed of crushed ice) rather than the plated fine-dining register documented for, e.g., `us-west-coast.md`'s Napa/Sonoma content — flag any brief calling for an upscale Pacific Northwest restaurant register as a real, valid but *secondary* register to this file's more casual default (see the contemporary "Pacific Northwest cuisine" fine-dining note in ENVIRONMENT below).

### Dish: Dungeness crab (whole cooked/cracked)

- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (a genuine communal-feast anchor dish, also sold and eaten as an everyday market item).
- Cuisine lineage: Native-Pacific-Coast-tradition, with a documented Indigenous-heritage baseline (Coast Salish and other Puget Sound-area tribes harvested and prepared Dungeness crab at low tide for thousands of years, cooking it over open fire, steamed, or boiled — methods that persist essentially unchanged today) and a later immigrant-driven commercial layer (Croatian immigrants who settled in Anacortes, Washington, in the late 19th century built a major commercial Dungeness crab supply industry serving Seattle, Portland, and San Francisco). - Serving format: **A genuine §4.4/§4.6 coexisting-format case, surfaced as an explicit choice**: (1) **Whole cooked and cracked, communal-feast style** — the crab is boiled or steamed whole, then cracked and served with minimal adornment (lemon, melted butter) for diners to pick themselves; (2) **Picked crab meat as a composed dish** — used cold in a Crab Louie salad (see below) or hot in a crab cake, where the meat is removed from the shell before serving. **Default when unspecified**: the whole cracked-crab communal format is the more iconic, singularly Pacific-Northwest-coded register and this file's default; the picked-meat format should be used specifically when a brief calls for a composed salad or crab-cake dish.

[CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [SF Standard — A pro-chef's guide to hosting an at-home Dungeness crab feast](https://sfstandard.com/2026/01/17/live-dungeness-crab-boil-chef-jason-halverson/)]

[CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Chef's Resource — How Much Does a Dungeness Crab Weigh?](https://www.chefsresource.com/faq/how-many-pounds-is-a-dungeness-crab/); [Washington Department of Fish & Wildlife — commercial crab fishery](https://wdfw.wa.gov/fishing/commercial/crab/coastal/about)] A 6.5-inch carapace is roughly 2.5× the Coca-Cola can's 2.6-inch diameter — a whole crab, legs extended, should read as noticeably larger across than the can laid on its side, and a single crab leg segment is comparable in length to about half the can's 4.83-inch height.

Picked crab meat (for a Crab Louie or crab cake) is bright white with faint coral-pink streaks near the shell line, in fine, moist, slightly stringy flakes.
- Common confusion: A Chesapeake/Atlantic blue crab (not documented in this KB's Atlantic-region files as of this writing) — ruled out by size and shell shape (Dungeness is notably larger and rounder-bodied) and by the West Coast/Pacific-only harvest range. A snow crab or king crab cluster (a different, harder-shelled, spinier Alaskan species typically sold as leg clusters rather than a whole cracked body) — ruled out by Dungeness's rounder overall carapace shape and by the whole-body communal presentation documented here.

### Dish: Pacific oysters (raw half-shell and BBQ/roasted)

- Category: Everyday to special-occasion.
- Cuisine lineage: Native-to-Japan species, introduced commercially to Washington's Willapa Bay and Hood Canal beginning in 1904, naturalized and thriving there by the 1930s — layered onto a much older native Olympia oyster fishery that predates it.
- History: Washington's oyster industry began in Willapa Bay (then Shoalwater Bay) in 1850, initially built on the small native Olympia oyster and shipped largely to Gold-Rush-era San Francisco; overharvesting, pollution, and increased silt from logging drove the native fishery into serious decline by around 1915. Pacific oysters, introduced from Japan starting in 1904 and commercially established by the 1920s, became the industry's new backbone; Willapa Bay alone now produces more than half of Washington's total oyster output and a large share of the entire US West Coast's. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [HistoryLink.org — Oyster Farming in Washington, Part 1](https://www.historylink.org/file/21070); [Explore Hood Canal — Hundreds of varieties yet only five species](https://www.explorehoodcanal.com/stories/oysters)]
- Regional form variation: **A genuine §4.6 coexisting-species/variant case, surfaced as an explicit choice**: (1) **The Pacific oyster** — the large, commercially dominant species, pear-to-teardrop shaped with a heavily ridged shell, capable of growing quite large (up to roughly 12 inches, though typically harvested well before that size); (2) **The Olympia oyster** — the only oyster species native to the West Coast of North America, dramatically smaller (maximum roughly 2–3.5 inches, commonly described as about half-dollar-sized), with a rounder, smoother, less-ridged shell and a distinct, more mineral/coppery flavor profile; now a boutique, lower-volume specialty rather than the commercial mainstay. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for both species' size/shape distinction] [SOURCE: [Hama Hama Oysters — Oyster Species: A Primer](https://hamahamaoysters.com/blogs/oyster-blog/oyster-species-a-primer); [Washington Department of Fish & Wildlife — Olympia oyster](https://wdfw.wa.gov/species-habitats/species/ostrea-lurida)] **Default when unspecified**: the Pacific oyster is the far more commercially common, widely available, and visually standard "Pacific Northwest oyster" for a general brief; the smaller, rounder Olympia oyster should be used only when a brief specifically calls out that heritage/boutique variety.
- Serving format: **A second, independent §4.4 coexisting-format case**: (1) **Raw, on the half shell** — the standard restaurant/raw-bar presentation; (2) **BBQ/grilled or roasted in the shell** — a real, common outdoor/casual preparation, particularly associated with oyster-farm and coastal community events. Both are genuinely common; default to raw half-shell for a restaurant/raw-bar scene, BBQ/roasted for an outdoor coastal cookout scene.
- Side dishes/condiments: For raw service, a mignonette (vinegar-shallot) sauce, lemon wedges, and/or cocktail sauce, plus crushed ice as the plating bed; condiments are served on the side, never pre-applied.
- Serving vessel: Raw oysters on the half shell are conventionally served on a large platter or tiered stand bedded in crushed ice, per `tableware-composition-reference.md` §2's communal-platter dimension (30–35cm) for a shared order; a standard first-course serving is about 6 oysters per person, or 3–4 as a lighter appetizer portion. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Metropolitan Market — Oysters on the Half Shell 101](https://www.metropolitan-market.com/learn/oysters-on-the-half-shell-101)]
- Real-world scale (§4.5): A standard market-size Pacific oyster on the half shell runs roughly 3–4 inches long — comparable to, or slightly larger than, the Coca-Cola can's 2.6-inch diameter — while an Olympia oyster's shell is roughly half-dollar-sized, well under half that length. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — synthesized from species-size ranges rather than an independently sourced half-shell-specific measurement]
- Visual/plating characteristics: See VISUAL & PLATING NORMS above for the raw oyster's plump, glossy, translucent-gray interior and visible pooled liquor. A Pacific oyster's shell exterior is deeply ridged, elongated, and pear-shaped, typically gray-white with occasional purple new-growth streaks; an Olympia oyster's shell is smaller, rounder, smoother, gray outside with an iridescent greenish-white interior. BBQ/roasted oysters, still in the shell, should show the shell just barely cracked open by heat with the edges of the meat visibly firmed and slightly browned at the rim, often finished with a pat of garlic-herb butter pooling in the shell.
- Common confusion: An Atlantic/East Coast oyster (not yet documented in this KB's Atlantic-region files) — ruled out by the more heavily ridged, elongated Pacific oyster shell versus a typically smoother, rounder Atlantic oyster shell. A Gulf Coast char-grilled oyster (also not yet documented in this KB) — a similar BBQ/roasted format exists there too; this file does not claim exclusivity for the technique, only for the specific Pacific/Olympia oyster species used here.
