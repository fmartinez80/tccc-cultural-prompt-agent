## ZONE CHARACTERIZATION

Arizona clears the "would swapping this region's norms into New Mexico or Texas look visibly wrong" test on multiple independent, causally-explained grounds, and this file's research found **real internal variance** worth surfacing explicitly.

**A distinct, later, and differently-mechanized annexation history than New Mexico's.** Southern Arizona (everything south of the Gila River, including Tucson) was not part of the US at all until the Gadsden Purchase, signed December 30, 1853 and finalized 1854 — the United States paid Mexico $10 million for roughly 29,640 square miles specifically to secure a flatter, cheaper route for a proposed southern transcontinental railroad, resolving a boundary dispute left open by the 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Britannica: Gadsden Purchase](https://www.britannica.com/event/Gadsden-Purchase); [EBSCO Research Starters — Gadsden Purchase](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/gadsden-purchase)] This is a genuinely different mechanism and timeline from New Mexico's: New Mexico's Spanish-colonial settlement began in 1598 (Oñate) and continued through a single continuous Spanish/Mexican/American administrative succession along the Rio Grande, while southern Arizona joined the US nearly a decade after the rest of the Mexican Cession (1848) specifically as a railroad corridor — a transactional, infrastructure-driven annexation rather than a colonial settlement's gradual absorption. This is the direct historical reason Tucson's food culture reads as a border-adjacent Sonoran outpost (closely tied to the Mexican state of Sonora just across the modern line) rather than a Rio-Grande-valley Spanish-colonial-inheritance culture the way Santa Fe's is.

**The Sonoran Desert itself is a distinct, non-transferable ecological and cultural region spanning the US/Mexico border in a way New Mexico's Rio Grande valley does not.** The Sonoran Desert is the only place on Earth the saguaro cactus grows natively, and it straddles southern Arizona and the Mexican state of Sonora as one continuous ecological and, historically, cultural region — not a border drawn through two separate desert types the way, say, the Chihuahuan Desert (which New Mexico and El Paso instead sit in) is shared with a different Mexican state (Chihuahua). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Houzz — Meet the Mighty Saguaro of the Desert Landscape](https://www.houzz.com/magazine/meet-the-mighty-saguaro-of-the-desert-landscape-stsetivw-vs~31877207)] This ecological continuity is the direct causal explanation for Sonoran-style Arizona cuisine's defining marker: **wheat flour tortillas, not corn**. Spanish colonists introduced wheat to Sonora in 1542; in the desert heat of Sonora (and Chihuahua), wheat could be grown as a dryland crop across various northern microclimates, while corn required more water and specific soil alkalinity — a real, geography-driven agricultural divide, not an arbitrary preference, that separated the wheat-tortilla north (Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila, Nuevo León) from the corn-tortilla center/south of Mexico. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Peppers of Key West — Flour Tortillas Explained: Why Northern Mexico Chose Wheat Over Corn](https://www.peppersofkeywest.com/flour-tortillas-explained-why-northern-mexico-chose-wheat-over-corn/); [Tucson Foodie — Sonoran flour tortillas are special to Tucsonans. But why?](https://tucsonfoodie.com/2019/08/07/sonoran-flour-tortillas/)] This is the single sharpest, most checkable authenticity marker distinguishing Sonoran-style Arizona food from **both** Tex-Mex (which uses both flour and corn, with corn dominant for tacos/enchiladas) and New Mexican cuisine (corn-tortilla-centric, chile-pod-sauce-structured) — Arizona's story is not a chile-pod-sauce story at all in the New Mexican sense.

**A distinct set of present-day Indigenous nations, genuinely different in kind from New Mexico's 19 Pueblos.** Arizona's Indigenous culinary heritage is carried by nations with no equivalent in New Mexico's file: the **Tohono O'odham Nation** (the second-largest reservation in Arizona, 2.8 million acres/4,460 square miles — roughly the size of Connecticut — sited within the Sonoran Desert itself in southern Arizona) [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Arizona Commerce Authority — Tohono O'odham Nation](https://www.azcommerce.com/media/vbhpr4nd/tohono-1.pdf)]; the **Navajo Nation** (the largest reservation in the US at ~27,413 square miles, spanning northeastern Arizona, northwestern New Mexico, and southeastern Utah — genuinely cross-border rather than Arizona-exclusive, unlike Tohono O'odham) [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: Navajo Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navajo_Nation)]; and the **Hopi Reservation**, entirely surrounded by the Navajo Nation in northeastern Arizona. None of these is a Pueblo in New Mexico's specific 19-Pueblo sense, and their foodways (mutton stew, blue corn mush, kneel-down bread, and piki bread for the Navajo/Hopi; saguaro-fruit harvest, tepary beans, and mesquite for the Tohono O'odham) are their own distinct traditions, not a lesser or overlapping version of New Mexico's Pueblo foodways.

**Internal variation, tested rather than assumed.** This file finds two axes of real variance:

- **Tucson/southern Arizona vs. Phoenix/central Arizona is a genuine gradient within one shared Sonoran-Mexican cuisine family — not a different cuisine.** Tucson was designated the first UNESCO Creative City of Gastronomy in the United States in December 2015, explicitly citing over 4,000 years of continuous agricultural history (the longest continually cultivated area on the continent) and a documented blending of Indigenous, Spanish, Mexican, Chinese, and African-American food traditions. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Smithsonian Magazine — What Makes Tucson Deserving of the Title of the United States' First Capital of Gastronomy](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/travel/introducing-americas-first-capital-gastronomy-180957793/); [Visit Tucson — UNESCO City of Gastronomy](https://www.visittucson.org/eat-drink/city-of-gastronomy/)] Phoenix's Mexican-food scene, by contrast, is documented as drawing more on a broader range of Mexican regional cuisines (Mexico City, Oaxaca) and a more fusion/upscale-restaurant register, with Tucson's chefs more consistently framed as preserving handmade-tortilla, roasted-chile, slow-cooked-meat Indigenous-and-Sonoran tradition specifically. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — consistent across multiple journalism-tier sources, not an academic culinary-geography study] [SOURCE: [KJZZ — This Arizona chef says Phoenix vs. Tucson isn't a debate. It's a statewide food collaboration](https://www.kjzz.org/the-show/2026-06-08/this-arizona-chef-says-phoenix-vs-tucson-isnt-a-debate-its-a-statewide-food-collaboration)] Both cities share the Sonoran hot dog and the chimichanga as common, statewide-recognized anchor dishes — this is a difference of *emphasis and cosmopolitanism*, the same kind of gradient `us-texas.md` documented for its own North/South/West Tex-Mex intensity variation, not a different dish grammar or a different primary starch the way New Mexico's split from Arizona was.
- **Navajo/Hopi (northeastern Arizona) and Tohono O'odham (southern Arizona Sonoran Desert) foodways are a genuinely different cuisine family layered geographically within the same state — not a gradient of the Sonoran-Mexican cuisine above.** Mutton stew, blue corn mush, kneel-down bread, and fry bread share no ingredient or technique lineage with carne seca, flour tortillas, or the chimichanga; this is the same kind of qualitative, dish-grammar-level difference that justified splitting New Mexico out of this file's own original slot in the first place, not a stylistic variant of the same cuisine.

**Decision on internal structure: one file, no further split — Navajo/Hopi and Tohono O'odham content carried as a substantial, explicit internal callout, the same "emphasis, not structural split" treatment `us-south.md` gives Lowcountry.** Two considerations argue against a further split despite the genuine cuisine-family difference just described: (1) the Navajo Nation is not Arizona-exclusive (it spans three states, the same complication that led `us-new-mexico.md` to defer full treatment rather than claim it outright), so a hypothetical `us-navajo-nation.md` would not map cleanly onto this project's state/region-file convention the way `us-new-mexico.md` or `us-texas.md` do; (2) the currently-sourced, staging-relevant dish count for Navajo/Hopi/Tohono O'odham content (four to six dishes, several at MEDIUM confidence pending stronger tribally-authored sourcing — see GAP LOG) is real but not yet at the depth `us-appalachia.md` or `us-florida.md` needed to justify carving out of a larger file. This mirrors the Lowcountry resolution in `us-south.md`: a genuinely distinct food tradition, carried as a clearly-labeled internal section rather than a separate file, with a note to revisit if a future dedicated pass (ideally with tribally-authored or academic sourcing) finds deeper, more staging-relevant material than this pass located.

**What this zone characterization is not claiming.** Arizona is not internally uniform, and this file does not treat it as such. **Caricature-avoidance note (editorial judgment, not a sourced claim):** not every Arizona scene should default to a rural saguaro-desert-and-cowboy register — Phoenix is one of the largest, most modern metro areas in the US, and a contemporary Phoenix strip-mall Mexican restaurant or a Scottsdale upscale dining room is at least as representative an Arizona setting as a rural Sonoran Desert scene. Equally, avoid flattening Tucson and Phoenix into an interchangeable single "Arizona Mexican food" register just because both share the Sonoran hot dog and the chimichanga — the sourced UNESCO framing above is what makes Tucson specifically distinguishable. Avoid, above all, treating Navajo, Hopi, or Tohono O'odham foodways as interchangeable with Sonoran-Mexican content or with each other; they are three separate, sourced traditions, not one generic "Native American Southwest" default.

## TRUSTED CONTENT

- **Wheat-flour tortillas, not corn, are Sonoran-style Arizona cuisine's single defining, structural marker — the equivalent role New Mexico's chile-pod sauce plays in that file.** Sonoran flour tortillas are distinctively thin — described as nearly paper-thin — made from a short ingredient list (flour, water, salt, lard, no baking powder), producing a flaky, minimally-chewy texture distinct from a thicker, more bread-like flour tortilla found elsewhere in the US. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Tucson Foodie — Sonoran flour tortillas are special to Tucsonans. But why?](https://tucsonfoodie.com/2019/08/07/sonoran-flour-tortillas/)]
- **"Burro" is a real, regionally-specific Arizona/Sonoran term for what is elsewhere called a burrito, not a distinct dish.** In Sonoran-style Arizona restaurants and in parts of the Southwest generally, "burro" has historically served as an interchangeable or informal regional variant of "burrito" — reflecting Arizona's Sonoran cultural ties rather than a different food. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — consistently described across several sources, though at enthusiast/blog tier rather than an academic linguistic source] A widely-repeated folk etymology claiming the term arose because some dubious taco stands sold donkey meat is treated here as a colorful but unverified legend, not a sourced fact. [SOURCE: [Roving Decanter — What is Sonoran-style Mexican Food?](https://rovingdecanter.com/2014/10/22/what-is-sonoran-style-mexican-food/)]
- **Carne seca and machaca are the dried-and-shredded-beef backbone of Sonoran-style Arizona cooking, appearing across multiple dish formats (tacos, enchiladas, cheese crisps, chimichangas, and scrambled with eggs) rather than being a single standalone dish** — see the dedicated entry below.
- **Indigenous-heritage sourcing discipline, following this project's own established precedent** (`us-appalachia.md`'s ramps/leather-britches entries; `us-new-mexico.md`'s Pueblo-bread entry): where a claim attributes a food tradition to the Navajo (Diné), Hopi, or Tohono O'odham specifically, this file states the claim's evidentiary tier honestly. ## VISUAL & PLATING NORMS

- **Sonoran-style Arizona plating reads as flour-tortilla-pale and cheese-forward, distinct from both Tex-Mex's glossy roux-gravy palette and New Mexico's chile-pod-red/green palette.** Where present, cheese (often a meltable Jack-style cheese) dominates the visual field of a cheese crisp or a chile relleno; carne seca/machaca contributes a deep reddish-brown, shredded, slightly dry-looking meat texture rather than a sauce-drenched one — Sonoran cooking is comparatively light on heavy, thick chile sauce as the plate's structural element.
- **The Sonoran hot dog's defining visual marker is the bacon itself, rendered into the frank rather than sitting as a separate garnish.** The hot dog cooks wrapped in bacon in a shared trough alongside other bacon-wrapped dogs, where the shed bacon grease functions like a shallow deep-fry — the bacon should show streaked, unevenly crisped, lean-pork patches directly against the frank's casing (not a neat spiral wrap with crisp uniform edges), and the frank itself should look slightly glossy/greasy from the shared cooking fat rather than dry-grilled. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Daily Beast — El Guero Canelo Serves Tucson's Most Mexcellent Hot Dog](https://www.thedailybeast.com/el-guero-canelo-serves-tucsons-most-mexcellent-hot-dog/)]
- **Texture/finish specifically**: Sonoran flour tortillas should look thin enough to be faintly translucent at the edges when held up to light, with small toasted brown blister spots rather than a uniform pale surface — a visibly thinner, flakier product than a standard supermarket flour tortilla. A cheese crisp's edges should show the tortilla crisped to a golden-brown, slightly curled/bubbled rim where it extends past the melted cheese, contrasting with the smooth, glossy, still-soft cheese pooled at the center — it is not folded over (unlike a quesadilla) and should always read as a flat, open-faced disc. Carne seca should look visibly dry and finely shredded — almost cotton-candy-like in its fine, airy strands — rather than the moist, clumped shred of a slow-braised pulled meat; when rehydrated into machaca, it should look slightly moistened and mixed through visibly separate, fluffy scrambled-egg curds, not a wet stew. Chimichanga shells should show a blistered, deep-golden, shatteringly crisp fried surface with visible small cracks — a drier, more uniformly fried crust than a chile relleno's puffier, more irregular egg-batter coating.
- **Doneness convention**: carne seca/machaca should never show any raw pink — it is fully dried and then rehydrated/cooked, reading as a uniform deep brown throughout. A chile relleno's egg batter should be light golden (not deep brown), visibly puffed and slightly craggy from being pan-fried in oil rather than deep-fried to a uniform crisp shell.

## ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES — zone deltas from `us.md`'s national baseline

- **The Sonoran Desert saguaro landscape is Arizona's single most non-transferable environmental marker — genuinely distinct from anything in `us-new-mexico.md` or `us-texas.md`.** The saguaro cactus grows natively only in the Sonoran Desert, reaching 40–50 feet and living up to 200 years; it does not occur naturally in New Mexico's Chihuahuan Desert terrain or in Texas. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Houzz — Meet the Mighty Saguaro of the Desert Landscape](https://www.houzz.com/magazine/meet-the-mighty-saguaro-of-the-desert-landscape-stsetivw-vs~31877207)] A saguaro-studded desert floor with paloverde trees and ocotillo, rocky and scrubby rather than sandy-dune, is a real, checkable, Arizona-specific outdoor backdrop.
- **Territorial-style architecture is Arizona's own regional built-environment register, distinguishable from New Mexico's Pueblo Revival.** Territorial style emerged after the Gold Rush era (from roughly 1849) when American settlers added milled-wood porch trim and window/door surrounds onto existing flat-roofed adobe construction — a hybrid Spanish-colonial Anglo-American register named for Arizona's and New Mexico's shared pre-statehood territorial status, but with straighter, more decoratively trimmed lines than New Mexico's rounded, viga-beam-forward Pueblo Revival register (`us-new-mexico.md`). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [SearchScottsdale — Arizona's Architectural Journey: Blending Tradition with Innovation](https://www.searchscottsdale.com/blog/arizonas-architectural-journey.html)] **Per the trademark/landmark-avoidance rule**, describe this generically (flat-roofed adobe-style construction with milled wood trim) rather than naming or depicting any specific named building or architect's work.
- **Sonoran hot dog cart/stand scene — a genuine, sourced "meal on the go" and "away from home" register distinct from `us.md`'s national car-centric default.** Vendors (called *dogueros*) range from long-running brick-and-mortar restaurants to family-run carts under a canopy in a gravel lot; the assembly-line construction (bun, beans, dog, tomato/onion, salsa, mustard, mayo, in that order) happens visibly at the stand. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Fast Food Club — Craving Real Sonoran Hot Dogs? [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Visit Tucson — Visit Tucson Launches Sonoran Dog Trail](https://www.visittucson.org/articles/post/visit-tucson-launches-sonoran-dog-trail-celebrating-the-citys-iconic-culinary-culture/)] **Per the trademark-avoidance rule**, stage this as a generic hot dog cart or small stand, never naming or depicting a specific real vendor's branding.

A traditional hogan (a round or hexagonal log-and-earth dwelling) is a real, documented Navajo architectural form still in ceremonial and some residential use, though a modern single-story home is at least as representative for an everyday contemporary scene. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Tucson Sentinel — 'Harmony with the desert': O'odham harvesting traditions persevere](https://www.tucsonsentinel.com/arts/report/071326_oodham_saguaros/harmony-with-desert-oodham-harvesting-traditions-persevere-despite-climate-change)]

## DISH CATALOG

### Dish: Sonoran hot dog (full authoritative entry — supersedes `us.md`'s national-index Hot dog pointer for the Sonoran variant)

- Category: Everyday — a genuine all-hours street food (lunch, dinner, and a documented late-night/after-bar snack).
- Cuisine lineage: Mexican-origin (Sonora, Mexico), specifically credited to Hermosillo — by the 1970s a popular late-night snack for students at the University of Sonora — with the bacon-wrapping technique itself first popularized at a Mexico City hot dog cart in 1956. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — consistent across multiple sources, though the exact 1956 date rests on a single detailed account] [SOURCE: [CNN — Sonoran hot dogs: How this zesty, bacon-wrapped delight was born](https://www.cnn.com/travel/sonoran-hot-dogs-tucson-arizona)]
- **Institutional history in Tucson**: El Güero Canelo, run by Daniel and Blanca Contreras since 1993, is widely credited as a founding and still-operating driver of the Sonoran hot dog's rise to prominence in Tucson, winning a James Beard Award (America's Classics) in 2018. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Daily Beast — El Guero Canelo Serves Tucson's Most Mexcellent Hot Dog](https://www.thedailybeast.com/el-guero-canelo-serves-tucsons-most-mexcellent-hot-dog/)] **Per the trademark-avoidance rule**, this is background historical context, not a literal prompt ingredient — an actual prompt should describe a generic Sonoran hot dog stand, not this or any other named business.
- Regional form variation: None within Arizona — a single, consistent Sonoran-style construction; not to be confused with the plain national hot dog or with the other regional hot dog styles already catalogued in `us.md` (Chicago, Coney, NY cart).
- Serving format: Sandwich/handheld only.
- Primary protein/composition: A large, all-beef hot dog (a "6-to-1," i.e. six dogs to a pound) wrapped in a strip of bacon and cooked in a shared trough alongside other bacon-wrapped dogs — the shed bacon grease functions like a shallow deep-fry, melting bacon flavor into the frank and leaving the bacon itself patched with streaks of lean, crisped pork. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Daily Beast, as above]]

[CONFIDENCE: LOW-MEDIUM — this file's own inference, not an independently sourced measurement]

### Dish: Chimichanga (a real, dated, genuinely contested-origin dish — treated with the same honest-disclosure standard this project applies to the gyro, the Reuben, and toasted ravioli)

[CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — the finished-dimension figures are this file's own reasonable synthesis from standard tortilla size and typical burrito-rolling convention, not an independently measured dish figure] [SOURCE: [Cuisine & Cocktails — Chimichangas (Pan Fried, Baked, or Air Fried)](https://www.cuisineandcocktails.com/chimichangas/)]

### Dish/ingredient: Tepary beans and saguaro fruit (Tohono O'odham heritage — ingredient entry, parallel to `us-new-mexico.md`'s Hatch chile treatment)

[CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Other traditional Tohono O'odham desert foods, named but not given full entries this pass (see GAP LOG): cholla cactus buds and mesquite beans, both documented as traditional wild-harvested foods alongside tepary beans and saguaro fruit. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]

Saguaro fruit itself is a small, oval, ruby-red-fleshed fruit when split open, with numerous tiny black seeds — a genuinely distinctive, vivid, checkable color when shown fresh/split, though this is a foraged/ceremonial food rarely plated as a restaurant dish.
- Common confusion: Saguaro fruit vs. prickly pear fruit (this file, above) — both are Sonoran Desert cactus fruits used similarly (fresh, syrup, jelly), but they come from different cacti with different fruit colors and shapes; this file did not find a strong basis to claim they're visually interchangeable and treats them as two related but distinct ingredients.

[CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — consistent across sources, food-blog/enthusiast tier rather than tribally-authored; flagged per this file's sourcing discipline] [SOURCE: [Tastes From The Road — Blue Corn Mush](https://www.tastesfromtheroad.com/post/blue-corn-mush-a-taste-of-the-navajo-nation)] **Note**: blue corn's sacred status and use is shared across multiple Southwestern Indigenous nations, not exclusive to the Navajo — the Hopi entry below documents blue corn's central role in Hopi piki bread as well, and `us-new-mexico.md` documents it for northern New Mexico Pueblo communities. Kneel-down bread, once unwrapped from its husk, shows a moist, dense, slightly sweet pale-blue-gray pudding-like texture — softer and moister than a dried-masa tamale, more like a corn pudding than a bread in the sliceable sense.

### Dish: Navajo taco (fry bread) — Arizona-specific presence and the informal "state dish" claim

- Category: Everyday — a common powwow, fair, and everyday-restaurant food across the Navajo Nation and, more broadly, Native American communities nationally.
- Cuisine lineage: **A genuinely important, honestly-disclosed origin distinct from a leisurely culinary tradition — the same disclosure `us-new-mexico.md` makes for the same dish.** Fry bread traces to 1864, created from flour, sugar, salt, and lard supplied by the US government as commodity rations to the Navajo people during and after the "Long Walk" — the forced ~300-mile relocation to Bosque Redondo, New Mexico. Fry bread is not an Indigenous-tradition food predating this event; most sources trace its origin story directly to the internment/relocation-driven commodity-ration system imposed on multiple displaced tribes in the mid-1800s. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH — consistently described across multiple sources, though at journalism/enthusiast tier rather than a tribally-authored primary source; this is a difficult, contested-in-tone history (celebrated food vs. reminder of hardship) that some Native writers and chefs themselves treat with ambivalence — flagged honestly rather than presented as a simple celebratory origin] [SOURCE: [Mental Floss — The Complicated History of Fry Bread](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/637618/fry-bread-controversial-history); [Smithsonian Magazine — Frybread](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/frybread-79191/)]
- Regional form variation: The **Navajo taco** — fry bread topped with beans or ground beef, lettuce, tomato, shredded cheddar, and optional green chile — is the specific composed dish; plain fry bread (unsweetened, or dusted with powdered sugar/honey as a dessert register) is a related but visually distinct sibling. **This file does not conflate Navajo fry bread with New Mexico's sopapilla** (`us-new-mexico.md`) — the two are related but structurally distinct: a sopapilla is cut from rolled dough and fries hollow/puffed, while Navajo fry bread is shaped by hand from individual dough balls pressed into a thin round and fries flat and solid, with no hollow puff, typically larger in diameter and denser/breadier in chew. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for this distinction, already established at that level in `us-new-mexico.md` and not re-derived here]
- **Arizona-specific institutional note**: the Navajo taco was voted the "State Dish of Arizona" in a 1995 informal readers' poll conducted by the Arizona Republic newspaper — a real, dated, sourced popular-vote result, but **not a legislated official state symbol** (Arizona has no legislated official state dish as of this pass) — flagged explicitly so this claim isn't overstated as government-designated fact. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH for the poll's existence and result; explicitly not an official government designation]
- Serving format: Plate/handheld — a large, flat round of fried dough, topped rather than folded or wrapped.
- Primary protein/composition: Ground beef or beans as the primary topping, with shredded lettuce, diced tomato, shredded cheddar cheese, and optional green chile.
- Serving vessel: A large plate — the fry bread itself is often described as "plate-sized," meaning it should visually approach or match the diameter of a standard entree plate rather than sitting well within its margins the way a smaller bread item would.
- Real-world size (§4.5): Fry bread rounds are commonly described as roughly 8–10 inches in diameter (comparable to or exceeding a standard 22–24cm small entree plate, and noticeably larger than New Mexico's 2-inch sopapilla squares) — flat, not domed/puffed, roughly ¼–½ inch thick. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — consistent general description across sources, not a single precisely-measured industry figure] [SOURCE: [House of Nash Eats — Navajo Tacos (Indian Fry Bread)](https://houseofnasheats.com/navajo-tacos-indian-fry-bread/)]
- Visual/plating characteristics: A large, flat, irregularly round disc of fried dough, golden-to-deep-brown, with visible large air bubbles/blisters across a matte, slightly craggy surface — flat and solid in cross-section (no hollow interior, the checkable differentiator from a sopapilla), with a dense, chewy, breadier crumb than a sopapilla's light, hollow puff. The toppings (beef/beans, lettuce, tomato, cheese) sit piled on top of the flat round rather than stuffed inside a pocket.
- Common confusion: `us-new-mexico.md`'s sopapilla — the flat-and-solid (Navajo taco) vs. hollow-and-puffed (sopapilla) distinction is the single clearest, most checkable marker, already established at HIGH confidence in `us-new-mexico.md` and directly applicable here. Not a hard-shell or soft Tex-Mex taco (`us-texas.md`) — no tortilla of any kind is used; the "shell" is fried bread dough itself.
- Confidence: HIGH for the fry-bread-vs-sopapilla distinction (inherited); MEDIUM-HIGH for the dish's composition and the 1995 poll; the origin history is treated with explicit care given its difficult, sometimes ambivalently-regarded historical context.
