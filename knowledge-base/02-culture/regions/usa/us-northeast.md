## FILE ROLE & METHOD

This is the first regional file built under the 13-file US structure decided in `us.md`. It holds the full, authoritative depth for every dish `us.md`'s national index points here for (New York-style pizza and its NJ/CT siblings, NYC halal cart gyro, NYC deli sandwiches, New England clam chowder and lobster roll), plus genuinely new dishes researched specifically for this file that had no prior index entry.

**Internal structure — three zones, one file.** The 13-file regional list (including keeping the Northeast as a single file) was decided in `us.md` and is not re-litigated here. But real internal texture exists within "Northeast" — evidenced directly by dish-level facts already locked into `us.md` before this file existed (Connecticut vs. Maine lobster roll are materially different; New Haven apizza vs. NY-style pizza are different; Boston's "grinder" vs. NYC's "hero" are different terms for the same object) — enough to warrant labeled internal zones, the same treatment Uruguay gives its coastal/interior distinction and `us-south.md` gives Lowcountry. This file uses three:

1. **NYC Metro & New Jersey** — New York City (all five boroughs), Long Island, northern/central New Jersey, the Jersey Shore, the lower Hudson Valley.
2. **Southern New England** — Connecticut and Rhode Island.
3. **Northern New England** — Massachusetts (including Boston), Vermont, New Hampshire, Maine.

(A human-reviewed question during this file's construction: whether Boston proper should get its own callout distinct from rural Vermont/New Hampshire/Maine within zone 3. **Decided: no** — Northern New England stays one zone. The urban/rural contrast is noted in prose within that zone's content below rather than given a separate subheading.)

Each zone was researched by a separate parallel pass; this file is the reconciled, merged result — duplication and cross-zone placement questions (New Haven apizza was independently drafted by both the Southern New England pass and the NYC Metro/NJ pass) were resolved by hand, not left as-is. **New Haven apizza's authoritative entry lives in the Southern New England zone** (geographically correct — New Haven is in Connecticut) — this was a human decision, made explicitly because the file's zone boundaries should stay predictable for future readers even though the dish's Italian-immigrant-pizza history has real parallels to NYC's.

**Placement rule reminder (per `country-file-schema.md` §4.3)**: every dish below that had a thin index entry in `us.md` gets its full authoritative depth here; the summary in `us.md` is not restated in full and should be treated as superseded by the corresponding entry below.

**Typical use note (added after this file's first draft, based on direct product-team feedback)**: the realistic use case for this file is expected to skew toward general, archetypal scenes — a general Northeastern coastal/seafood-shack meal, a fall outdoors scene, a classic sit-down restaurant interior, a beach/boardwalk moment — rather than hyper-local, neighborhood-specific staging (a named NYC block, a specific Beacon Hill-vs-South-End architectural distinction, a specific bodega's exact history). The neighborhood/city-level detail in this file below remains real, sourced, and useful — it's what makes each dish's own identity checkable and correctly distinguishable from a similar-looking neighbor — but it is not expected to be the primary thing a prompt draws on. **See the "Quick-reference: generic scene registers" table immediately below the zone characterizations** for the broadly-reusable staging archetypes most prompts will actually need; treat the more granular neighborhood-level content further down as depth to consult only when a brief specifically names a city or dish that calls for it.

## ZONE CHARACTERIZATIONS

### NYC Metro & New Jersey

This zone earns its own labeled emphasis on causal, not just nominal, grounds. NYC's specific 19th/20th-century immigration waves — Eastern European Jewish (deli/appetizing/knish tradition), Italian (pizza-by-the-slice, counter-service pizzeria culture), and, more recently, Muslim immigrant communities from Egypt, Bangladesh, and Afghanistan who took over the street-cart trade from the 1980s–90s onward (halal carts) — produced a dense, geographically concentrated set of genuinely NYC-specific foods and serving formats with no equivalent elsewhere in the Northeast. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Macaulay CUNY/OpenLab — More Than Chicken Over Rice](https://openlab.macaulay.cuny.edu/nyfoodatlas/2025/05/15/more-than-chicken-over-rice-the-true-story-behind-nycs-halal-carts/)]

Building density visibly changes the staging environment, not just the food: NYC apartment kitchens are legally defined and commonly built at a fraction of the size assumed by `us.md`'s national open-plan-island default — NYC code sets 80 sq ft as the minimum for a legally defined "kitchen," and a typical Manhattan one-bedroom kitchen runs only slightly above that floor, around ~90 sq ft. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — single real-estate/design-trade source for the specific figures] [SOURCE: [6sqft — New York's dirty little secret: the apartment kitchen](https://www.6sqft.com/new-yorks-dirty-little-secret-the-apartment-kitchen/)] This produces a distinct outdoor/vertical eating geography too — the brownstone stoop as a genuine, Dutch-colonial-rooted (from Dutch "stoep") social/eating perch, and the rooftop/terrace as the practical answer to "meal outdoors at home" where a private yard is the exception, not the rule.

New Jersey has its own distinct causal case: it is the documented historical epicenter of the American diner-manufacturing industry (as many as 20 prefabricated-diner manufacturers based in NJ from the 1920s–80s) and is cited as having roughly 600 diners today, the most of any state. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [NJ Monthly — Classic Diners, Offering a Glimpse Into the Past, are True Jersey Gems](https://njmonthly.com/articles/eat-drink/classic-diners-jersey-gems/); [Preservation NJ — Historic Diners of New Jersey](https://preservationnj.org/10-most/historic-diners-of-new-jersey/)]

**Caricature-avoidance note (editorial judgment)**: this zone should not default every scene to dense Manhattan apartment living — Long Island, northern/central New Jersey, and the lower Hudson Valley genuinely include conventional suburban single-family housing with yards and garages, consistent with `us.md`'s national default. A "New Jersey" or "Long Island" brief should not be force-fit into a Manhattan-apartment register just because it's inside this zone.

### Southern New England (Connecticut & Rhode Island)

Two lines of evidence support this zone. First, settlement-pattern causality: New Haven's Italian-American community is specifically Campania/Amalfi-coast-descended (Frank Pepe was born in Maiori, near Naples), while Rhode Island runs on a separately-documented immigrant thread — RI has the highest percentage of Portuguese ancestry of any US state, concentrated in Providence, Bristol, and Pawtucket, mostly Azorean/Madeiran in origin, showing up directly in RI's own coastal dishes (stuffies are specifically chouriço-flavored). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [CNN — New Haven entices with culture — and pizza](https://www.cnn.com/travel/new-haven-connecticut-best-towns-america-2026); [Foodigenous — The Porkchop Riviera](https://www.foodigenous.com/post/the-porkchop-riviera); [TugaFinds — Rhode Island Portuguese Heritage](https://www.tugafinds.com/states/rhode-island)] Second, both states share a specific coastal/small-state visual register — the small, dense Long Island Sound/Narragansett Bay coastal town — genuinely different from NYC/NJ's urban-density-and-boardwalk register and from Northern New England's larger-scale Boston-urban-plus-rural-Maine/Vermont spread. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]

**What this grouping is not claiming**: CT and RI are not food-culturally identical — Connecticut's identity is Italian-immigrant- and Long-Island-Sound-coded, Rhode Island's is Portuguese-immigrant- and Narragansett-Bay-coded. They're grouped because both are small, both are genuinely distinct from their larger neighbors on the "would swapping norms look wrong" test, and grouping them avoids two very-thin single-state sections in an already-multi-zone file. **A flagged open question, noted for a future pass**: Rhode Island's Portuguese-heritage food culture is documented by at least one source as spanning into southeastern Massachusetts rather than stopping cleanly at the state line — worth revisiting if a future pass finds this zone boundary genuinely porous.

### Northern New England (Boston/MA, Vermont, New Hampshire, Maine)

This zone clears the "would swapping norms look wrong" bar on food, architecture, and landscape simultaneously. On food: Maine-style lobster roll, New England-style clam chowder, Boston baked beans, Boston cream pie, and the Maine whoopie-pie/blueberry-pie pair have no equivalent claimed dish in either other zone. On setting: the zone spans a genuine urban-to-rural range with no equivalent inside NYC/NJ or CT/RI — dense 19th-century brick rowhouse/brownstone neighborhoods in Boston proper (Beacon Hill, the South End, Back Bay) at one end, small Vermont/New Hampshire/Maine villages built around a white clapboard church and a town green, plus Maine's working coastal lobster-shack economy, at the other. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH for the architectural contrast; HIGH for the food-distinctness half, inherited from `us.md`'s already-locked entries]

This zone's defining dishes trace to Yankee/colonial-era and Maine-coastal traditions rather than a specific 19th/20th-century immigrant wave the way NYC's pastrami-on-rye or halal-cart gyro do — an observation about which dishes anchor the zone's identity, not a claim that the zone lacks immigrant populations. Versus Connecticut/Rhode Island specifically: Maine's lobster-shack economy and Boston's urban brownstone density are not features of the CT/RI zone's own identity (Connecticut-style lobster roll, New Haven apizza); depicting a Connecticut lobster shack with Maine's paper-tray/picnic-table aesthetic, or Boston's brownstone streetscape for a Rhode Island scene, would be exactly the kind of visible error this zone split exists to prevent.

(Human-reviewed and decided: Boston stays merged into this zone rather than becoming a fourth internal callout — see FILE ROLE & METHOD above.)

## TRUSTED CONTENT — ZONE-WIDE NORMS

### NYC Metro & New Jersey

- **The delicatessen/appetizing-store split is a real, kosher-dietary-law-driven institutional distinction that matters for staging which venue a dish belongs in.** Delicatessens sell *flayshig* (meat) foods — pastrami, corned beef, knishes, hot dogs. Appetizing stores sell *milchig* (dairy) foods — lox, herring, cream cheese, bagels — kept separate because Jewish dietary law forbids mixing meat and dairy. Bagels-and-lox is historically an appetizing-store item, not a delicatessen item, even though today the two are often thought of together as "the NYC deli." [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Today.com — Al Roker explores the origins of bagels and lox](https://www.today.com/food/al-roker-explores-origins-bagels-lox-new-york-city-t241468)]
- **Counter-service, order-by-the-slice pizzeria culture has a specific, dated causal explanation, not just "pizza is popular here."** Gennaro Lombardi received the first licensed US pizzeria in 1905 (Spring Street, Manhattan). Selling individual reheated slices became commercially practical only after coal ovens were converted to smaller gas-fired ovens starting in 1934 (credited to equipment dealer Frank Mastro) — the origin of the "walk in, point at a slice, watch it reheat, eat standing or at a counter" ritual. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Flour & Coffee — The Authentic New York Pizza Slice](https://flourandcoffee.substack.com/p/authentic-new-york-pizza-slice-history-little-italy)]
- **The bodega is a genuine, large-scale NYC institution with its own documented immigrant settlement history, distinct from the deli/appetizing history above.** "Bodega" took hold as a NYC term by the 1950s as Puerto Rican migrants opened small neighborhood groceries in areas larger chains weren't serving; ownership later shifted heavily toward Dominican families. As of a 2020 estimate, roughly 13,000 bodegas operate across NYC — far more than a place to buy packaged food, functioning also as a grill/deli counter and informal social hub. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Lower East Side History Project — The History of NYC Bodegas](https://www.leshp.org/blog/the-history-of-nyc-bodegas/); [NPR — New York City bodegas and the generations who love them](https://www.npr.org/sections/codeswitch/2017/03/10/518376170/new-york-city-bodegas-and-the-generations-who-love-them)]
- **New Jersey is documented as "the diner capital of the world"** with the industrial/manufacturing causal story above; the independently-owned diner remains "relatively common" specifically in NJ, NY, New England, Delaware, and Pennsylvania — a genuine regional concentration. Diners in this corridor are frequently Greek-American-owned, along with Polish, Ukrainian, and other immigrant-family ownership depending on area. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Wikipedia: American diner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_diner); [NJ Monthly](https://njmonthly.com/articles/eat-drink/classic-diners-jersey-gems/)]

### Southern New England

- **Seafood-shack service is a genuinely distinct regional dining register, not just "casual outdoor seafood."** New England seafood shacks trace to working fishermen's operations; picnic tables (sometimes painted a distinctive cerulean blue, per one source), paper plates or paper-lined baskets, and plastic utensils are the norm, not a formality. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [LobsterAnywhere — New England's Best Lobster Shacks](https://lobsteranywhere.com/new-england-style/best-lobster-shacks-seafood/)]
- **Caricature-avoidance note**: not every CT/RI meal-away-from-home scene should default to a seafood shack — it's this zone's most distinctive differentiating register, but casual-dining chains, diners, and ordinary neighborhood restaurants remain equally real, common options here too.

### Northern New England

- **This zone inherits, rather than restates, `us.md`'s national-level norms**: the Bread Reference section, the Hoagie/Sub/Hero/Grinder naming table (Boston/New England's term is "grinder," already locked in there), and the general US housing-stock/climate framing.
- **Seafood-shack service applies here too, with a Maine-specific origin story**: Maine's lobster shacks trace to working fishermen's co-ops selling catch straight off the boat; as coastal tourism grew from the 1950s–60s onward, many added picnic tables and simple cooking setups — the picnic-table-and-paper-tray model is the defining, still-current format. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM] [SOURCE: [The Maine Beaches — Lobster Shacks](https://themainebeaches.com/lobster-shacks/)]
- **"Chowder house" is a real, historically continuous Boston/New England restaurant register, not a marketing invention.** Boston's Union Oyster House (established 1826 in a building dating to 1714, widely reported as America's oldest continuously operating restaurant) began serving clam chowder in 1834 with a recipe described as little-changed since. **Named here as background context establishing the register's authenticity, not as a literal prompt ingredient** — a staged scene should describe the generic register (an old wood-paneled New England chowder house/oyster house, nautical bric-a-brac, dark wood booths), not this specific, real, operating business. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Boston.com — Union Oyster House turns 200](https://www.boston.com/food/restaurants/2026/07/03/union-oyster-house-americas-oldest-restaurant/)]
- **Bean-supper culture**: Boston baked beans' association with Saturday-night cooking and Sunday eating (see dish entry below) is a genuine table-timing norm, not just a dish-level fact.

## VISUAL & PLATING NORMS (cross-cutting, all zones)

- **Street-cart and bodega food is served wrapped (foil, wax paper, or a thin plastic/paper bag) rather than plated**, and — per `country-file-schema.md` §7.5's anti-hand-holding rule — should be staged resting on a surface rather than held up to camera, even though these foods are authentically eaten by hand while standing or walking. **The point-of-purchase location (the cart's own counter ledge, a bodega counter) is only one option, not a hard rule** — the underlying idea is grab-and-go: someone buys it there and eats it wherever they end up, so a home tabletop, a park bench, a boardwalk railing, the beach, or a subway-platform bench are equally authentic resting surfaces. Don't default every street-cart/bodega scene to being staged at the point of sale.
- **The NY-style pizza fold** (folding the slice in half lengthwise to eat it upright without the tip drooping) is a real, distinct eating convention, already documented in `us.md`'s crust-property description — worth repeating as a zone-specific eating-custom detail since it visibly distinguishes NY-style eating from a fork-and-knife pizza culture (deep-dish, St. Louis-style, New Haven apizza).
- **NYC deli sandwiches are piled dramatically high as a genuine, documented convention, not a caricature** — meat piled several inches thick between two slices of rye is the expected, correct visual.
- **Cream/white is Northern New England's dominant chowder palette, in contrast to Manhattan-style's red/tomato register and Rhode Island's clear-broth register.** New England clam chowder reads as pale ivory/off-white, opaque, with visible potato cubes and clam pieces suspended in a thick, cream-based liquid.
- **Matte vs. glossy is the lobster roll's own internal fork, and cuts across zones**: Maine-style shows a matte, creamy mayonnaise coating with no sheen; Connecticut-style shows a warm, glossy butter coating. A glossy appearance on a Maine-style roll, or a matte creamy appearance on a Connecticut-style roll, would misrepresent one as the other.
- **Deep amber-brown/molasses-glossy, matte-pale, and poured-chocolate-glossy are three distinct Northern New England dessert/dish finishes that should not be interchanged**: Boston baked beans read as a dark, glossy, molasses-brown sauce clinging to whole beans (not a thin, pale, ketchup-forward "pork and beans" can-style look); New England boiled dinner reads as a matte, pale palette (grey-pink corned beef, pale cabbage and root vegetables, no glossy sauce); Boston cream pie's modern finish is a smooth, glossy, dark chocolate glaze poured over pale cake with a visible drip down the sides.

## ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES — zone deltas from `us.md`'s national baseline

(Each subsection states only what genuinely differs from the national baseline in `us.md` and `country-file-schema.md` §5 for that zone — it does not repeat national norms that apply unchanged.)

### NYC Metro & New Jersey

- **Housing density and building type visibly diverge from the national single-family-detached-home default.** NYC's housing stock skews toward apartments, walk-ups, and rowhouse/brownstone construction. A NYC-cast "casual lunch/dinner at home" scene should default to an apartment or rowhouse unit, not a suburban house, unless a brief specifically calls for suburban NJ/Long Island (also real and common within this same zone).
- **Kitchen size is a real, checkable, visibly different physical constraint.** Per the 80 sq ft code minimum / ~90 sq ft typical Manhattan kitchen cited above, a NYC apartment "casual lunch at home" scene should default to a narrow galley kitchen or small enclosed room with a two-to-three-person table wedged into an adjoining nook, not an island with bar stools.
- **The brownstone stoop is a real, historically rooted (Dutch colonial, 17th century) outdoor social space** — an authentic register for an informal "meal outdoors at home" or "meal on the go" scene in brownstone-dense neighborhoods (much of Brooklyn, parts of Manhattan and the Bronx). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH for the historical/social claim; the specific "eating on a stoop" behavior is a reasonable extension of well-documented general stoop-sitting rather than a separately-sourced eating-specific claim]
- **Private rooftop/terrace grilling is real but genuinely restricted by NYC Fire Code** — a sharper, more codified version of the national apartment-balcony grill restriction `us.md` already documents. Standard propane grills cannot be stored on a balcony, roof, roof deck, or rear/courtyard; natural-gas grills require licensed-plumber installation plus city approval; charcoal grills are legal on ground-level terraces/backyards but not balconies or roofs. A private-rooftop grilling scene is less authentic here than a shared/communal building rooftop deck with a jointly-owned grill, or simply no grill at all. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the code restrictions] [SOURCE: [HPD Signs — NYC Fire Code 307.5 barbecue rules](https://hpdsigns.nyc/barbecue-on-the-balconies-rooftop-and-rear-yards-fc-307-5/)]
- **Meal on the go — a sharp, worth-stating contrast with `us.md`'s car-centric national finding.** In dense NYC neighborhoods, a meal on the go is at least as authentically staged as a pedestrian: a wrapped bodega sandwich, a halal cart platter, or a cart hot dog eaten standing at the cart or walking a sidewalk. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM — a reasonable, well-supported inference from bodega/cart density and NYC's low car-ownership rate, not a directly quantified on-foot-vs.-in-car statistic]
- **The diner is a stronger, more specifically-anchored register here than nationally**, per the NJ diner-capital sourcing above — especially authentic for a New Jersey-set 1–3-person away-from-home scene.
- **The classic counter-service NY pizzeria** is a distinct physical setting from a sit-down "pizzeria restaurant": a glass display counter holding whole pies, a large hanging menu board, a narrow standing-and-leaning counter for eating a slice upright. Both registers coexist and are equally authentic for different briefs.
- **The Jersey Shore boardwalk** builds on the already-established boardwalk pizza entry: Atlantic City built America's first boardwalk in 1870; walkable, strolling-and-snacking boardwalk food culture took its familiar form in the interwar period and expanded rapidly post-WWII. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [NJ Monthly — Jersey Shore Food History](https://njmonthly.com/articles/jersey-living/shore-food-then-and-now/)]
- **Caricature-avoidance note**: an NYC-specific brief should not be over-corrected into a single "gritty urban" cliché — clean, comfortable, well-maintained apartments and brownstones are just as authentic as more worn/dense ones.

### Southern New England

- **Meal on the go — 1 person**: a specifically Rhode Island-coded version is a walk-up order at a Del's Frozen Lemonade stand — traditionally seasonal, simple stands with a service window, a genuinely different "on the go" register from the national car-centric default. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- **Away from home — 1 person**: a New Haven apizza house's whole-pie-only norm structurally discourages solo ordering compared to a by-the-slice NY-style pizzeria — worth noting as a real constraint on staging a solo apizza scene. A Rhode Island coffee-shop/diner-counter solo scene, with a glass of pale-tan coffee milk, is a well-supported local-color alternative to the national diner-counter register.
- **Away from home — 2–3 people**: a small group sharing a whole New Haven apizza pie, cut tableside into irregular pieces (the pie on a metal tray at the table's center, not individual plates), is the zone's most distinctive Connecticut-side register. A Rhode Island clam shack table — clam cakes, a bowl of RI clear-broth chowder each, a basket of fried clams or a lobster roll, picnic-table seating — is the Rhode Island-side equivalent.
- **Newport, RI** carries a real, distinctive secondary register: a working "Sailing Capital"/harbor identity layered with Gilded Age mansion tourism produces a slightly more upscale, historic-waterfront dining register than the plain clam-shack default. **Prompt-writing note**: Newport's specific mansions and its Cliff Walk are real, named, privately/nonprofit-owned properties — describe the generic category (a New England coastal harbor town with historic 19th-century architecture, sailboats on the water), not a specific named property. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM] [SOURCE: [Fora Travel — Three Days in Newport](https://www.foratravel.com/guides/TNLQSU/three-days-in-newport-rhode-island-mansions-sailing-and-seaside-charm-beth-jalazo)]
- **Not independently sourced this pass**: small-town CT/RI Main Street architectural specifics beyond generic New England travel-writing language — a real gap, not a confirmed finding (see Gap Log).

### Northern New England

- **Boston proper: dense 19th-century brick rowhouse and brownstone streetscapes, not a generic "American downtown."** Beacon Hill is dominated by Federal and Greek Revival brick rowhouses; the South End and Back Bay by Victorian-era brownstone-and-brick townhouses with raised stoops and bay-front facades — a true brownstone (brown sandstone facade) is more characteristic of the South End/Back Bay than Beacon Hill, where brick and granite dominate. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM] [SOURCE: [Urban Circle Realty — Brownstone vs Rowhouse Living in Beacon Hill](https://urbancirclerealty.com/blog/brownstone-vs-rowhouse-living-in-beacon-hill)]
- **Rural Vermont/New Hampshire/inland Maine: the saltbox colonial house and the white clapboard church-and-common village center** are this zone's own distinct vernacular — genuinely different from Boston's urban brick density. The saltbox house (two stories in front, a long low-pitched roofline sloping to one story at the rear, central chimney) dates to 1650s colonial New England. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Britannica — Saltbox](https://www.britannica.com/technology/saltbox)] Small Vermont village centers recur around a white wooden clapboard church with a steeple fronting a village green. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [SAH ARCHIPEDIA — Vermont](https://sah-archipedia.org/essays/VT-01)]
- **Maine's coastal working/tourist economy is its own distinct staging register**: a weathered wooden lobster shack directly on or near a working harbor, picnic tables (sometimes with a simple roofed but open-air shelter), a small walk-up order window, and paper-tray service.
- **New England's autumn foliage season** is a genuine, heavily-documented regional visual signature — but this is well-sourced only as a landscape/tourism phenomenon, not specifically as an established food-photography staging convention. A reasonable, low-risk seasonal choice for an outdoor scene, but treat as editorial judgment about a real landscape phenomenon, not a sourced photography rule. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM for the phenomenon; LOW for its use as a staging convention]
- **Away from home, 1 person / 2–3 people**: a seafood shack (picnic table, order window in the background) and an old New England chowder house (dark wood booths, nautical décor) are both strong, well-supported alternatives to the national casual-dining-chain-booth default.
- **No zone-specific override found** for "meal outdoors at home" or "meal on the go" beyond the general notes above — the national grill-culture and car-centric findings are not contradicted by anything found for this zone; flag for review if a future pass finds a genuine contrast (e.g., a documented rural Vermont/NH grilling difference).

Coastal New England adds an outdoor seafood-feast register (the clambake) that has no national equivalent. [EDITORIAL]

#### Celebration: New England clambake (and the backyard lobster bake)
- Type: community or family gathering (summer, often tied to the Fourth of July, family reunions and church or civic fundraisers)
- When: July to early September; the meal comes off the bake mid-to-late afternoon after hours of cooking. Intake time of day: **golden-hour** or **midday** on a beach or lawn.
- Gathering: family, neighbours or a club or church group, ~15 to 60 for a traditional beach or lawn bake; a backyard "lobster bake" in a big pot is the smaller home version for ~8 to 15 [EDITORIAL]. Venue: other (beach, shore park, a clambake company's grounds) or home outdoor. Coastal Rhode Island, Massachusetts and Maine.
- The spread: the traditional bake layers seafood and vegetables on fire-heated stones under fresh rockweed and canvas: a whole lobster per person, steamers (soft-shell clams), corn on the cob, potatoes, sometimes sausage or fish, cooked together in one steaming pile [HIGH — New England Historical Society, Wikipedia: Clambake, Northshore Magazine]. Served with melted butter in cups and the clam broth for dipping steamers; New England clam chowder or clam cakes may start the meal (see catalog: **New England clam chowder**, **Rhode Island clam cakes**). **Clambake plate** has no full catalog entry: one bright red-orange whole lobster ~30 cm long (twice the can's height), a pile of grey-shelled steamers, a half or whole ear of yellow corn and 2 to 3 small red or white potatoes, on a heavy paper plate or a tin pie plate, a cup of melted butter beside it (see CANDIDATE QUEUE). Vessels: big enamel or aluminium pots or the rockweed pile itself, wire baskets, foil trays, rolls of paper towel.
- Snapshot staging:
  - **1 setting:** a picnic table covered in brown or newspaper-style paper; one plate with the full clambake portion, butter cup, a cracker and pick. In frame: an empty shell bowl and the edge of a steamer pot with rising steam. Cues: rockweed and canvas steaming in the background, soft; blurred figures on a lawn or beach.
  - **2 settings:** two identical clambake plates side by side, one shared bowl of empty shells between them, a basket of corn cropped at the end. Cues: the long paper-covered table running out of frame; a stacked pile of paper plates and paper-towel rolls.
  - **Small group (3 to 4 settings):** a stretch of picnic table with identical plates; a big pot or a pile of lobsters and corn tipped onto the paper in the middle, partly cropped. Cues: the bake pit smoking at the frame edge; extra picnic tables behind; harbor or dune grass soft.
- Decor and cues: plastic lobster bibs, nutcrackers and picks, paper-covered tables, a weathered shingled shed or dune line behind. Clichés to avoid: a lighthouse in every frame, nautical rope-and-anchor props, a lobster in a captain's hat.
- Never stage: beer or beer coolers (very common at a real bake, never shown); Wampanoag appanaug ceremonies (historians note the Native practice is ceremonial and distinct from the settler social bake; do not present the social clambake as an Indigenous rite, and do not stage the ceremony).
- Confidence and sources: method and components [HIGH — New England Historical Society, Wikipedia: Clambake, Northshore Magazine]; headcounts [EDITORIAL].

#### Celebration: Feast of the Seven Fishes (La Vigilia)
- Type: calendar holiday (Italian-American Christmas Eve)
- When: 24 December, a long evening dinner in courses. Intake time of day: **evening**.
- Gathering: extended Italian-American family, ~10 to 25 at the home of the senior generation [EDITORIAL]. Venue: home indoor, a long dining table often extended into the living room. Strongest in NYC Metro and New Jersey, also Southern New England and (see `us-mid-atlantic.md`) Philadelphia.
- The spread: at least seven seafood dishes, an Italian-American tradition rooted in southern Italian meatless Christmas Eve customs and brought over by the early-1900s immigration wave [HIGH — National Geographic, Atlas Obscura; the "seven" number is an American codification]. Typical dishes: baccalà (salt cod, fried or in a salad or tomato stew), fried calamari with lemon and marinara, fried smelts, a chilled seafood salad with scungilli (conch), shrimp, octopus and celery, baked stuffed clams oreganata, linguine with clam sauce, shrimp scampi, stuffed escarole [MEDIUM — National Geographic plus lifestyle and restaurant sources]. Served family-style in waves, platters and big pasta bowls on the table. None of these has a catalog entry here (see CANDIDATE QUEUE); visual anchors: **fried calamari** is golden rings and tentacles in a light crisp batter piled on a platter with lemon wedges and a small bowl of red marinara; **seafood salad** is a glossy white-and-pink mix of squid rings, shrimp and pale scungilli slices with celery and parsley in olive oil and lemon, in a wide glass or ceramic bowl; **linguine with clam sauce** is pale pasta glistening with oil, garlic and parsley, with small clams in the shell on top, in a wide pasta bowl.
- Snapshot staging:
  - **1 setting:** one place with a pasta-bowl portion of linguine with clams, or a plate with a few calamari rings and a spoon of seafood salad. In frame: the calamari platter with lemon wedges and the marinara bowl, partly cropped; a bread basket. Cues: the table running out of frame crowded with more platters; Christmas-tree bokeh behind.
  - **2 settings:** two identical plates; between them the seafood-salad bowl and the calamari platter, a dish of baked clams cropped at the edge. Cues: a third platter coming in from a blurred hand; a red tablecloth and candles.
  - **Small group (3 to 4 settings):** one end of the table with identical plates, 4 to 5 seafood platters and bowls covering the visible stretch, cropped at the sides. Cues: more platters than the diners could finish; an older relative blurred at the far end; a tray of Italian Christmas cookies on a sideboard.
- Decor and cues: white or red tablecloth, the good dishes, candles, a tree in the next room, a sideboard of cookies and struffoli. Clichés to avoid: Italian-flag colours on everything, mobster or "Sopranos" styling, a red-checked trattoria cloth (that is a restaurant cliché, not a home Christmas Eve).
- Never stage: wine (central to a real Vigilia table; never shown, no wine glasses); midnight Mass, nativity or religious items beside the product.
- Confidence and sources: tradition and origin [HIGH — National Geographic, Atlas Obscura]; dish list [MEDIUM]; headcount and staging [EDITORIAL].

#### Celebration: Italian-American Sunday dinner ("Sunday gravy")
- Type: community or family gathering (weekly)
- When: Sunday, a long early-afternoon dinner (roughly 1pm to 4pm) after a sauce that has simmered since the morning. Intake time of day: **midday** or early **golden-hour**.
- Gathering: three generations of one family, ~6 to 15 at the grandparents' or parents' home [EDITORIAL]. Venue: home indoor, dining table or a big kitchen table. NYC (especially Brooklyn and Staten Island), New Jersey, and Philadelphia (shared with `us-mid-atlantic.md`); "gravy" for tomato sauce is a regional Italian-American usage in this corridor [MEDIUM — The Manual and regional food writing; recipe-blog tier for most detail].
- The spread: one big pot of tomato "gravy" cooked for hours with meatballs, Italian sausage, braciole (rolled, tied beef or pork) and pork ribs; served as a pasta course (rigatoni, ziti or spaghetti dressed with the gravy) and then the meats on a platter, plus a green salad and Italian bread [MEDIUM — The Manual; LOW for any single family's set, as recipes vary]. See catalog (`us.md`): **Spaghetti and Meatballs — plated** for the meatball and sauce look. **Braciole** has no catalog entry: a browned beef roll ~5 cm across, sliced into rounds showing a spiral of parsley, garlic and cheese filling, sitting in deep red sauce on the meat platter (see CANDIDATE QUEUE).
- Snapshot staging:
  - **1 setting:** a wide pasta bowl of rigatoni in red gravy topped with a meatball and grated cheese. In frame: the meat platter (meatballs, sausage links, braciole rounds) partly cropped and a cheese bowl with a spoon. Cues: the sauce pot on the stove soft behind; the table running out of frame.
  - **2 settings:** two identical pasta bowls; between them the big pasta serving bowl and a loaf of Italian bread on a board. Cues: a blurred grandparent ladling at the end; a second, empty chair pulled out.
  - **Small group (3 to 4 settings):** a stretch of the family table with identical bowls, the pasta bowl and meat platter in the centre, the salad bowl cropped. Cues: more chairs than people in frame; framed family photos on the wall behind (no religious items in frame); a child's high chair at the edge.
- Decor and cues: an everyday tablecloth, mismatched serving pieces, a grated-cheese bowl, a heel of Italian bread. Clichés to avoid: red-checked cloths and candle-in-a-Chianti-bottle (restaurant cliché), mafia styling, an accordion.
- Never stage: wine or a carafe on the table (common in real life, never shown).
- Confidence and sources: [MEDIUM — The Manual and regional food writing; much of the detail rests on recipe blogs, last-resort tier, flagged]; staging [EDITORIAL].

### NYC Metro & New Jersey — Pizza

#### Dish: New York-style pizza (full zone entry — authoritative depth for `us.md`'s national index entry)
- Category: Everyday
- Cuisine lineage: Italian-American (Neapolitan immigrant origin — Gennaro Lombardi, 1905, first licensed US pizzeria, Spring Street, Manhattan). [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [Flour & Coffee](https://flourandcoffee.substack.com/p/authentic-new-york-pizza-slice-history-little-italy)]
- Regional form variation: This *is* the authoritative "New York-style" variant `us.md`'s national pizza entry points to.
- Serving format: Slice (handheld, folded lengthwise to eat) or whole pie (shared, cut into 8 large triangular wedges) — both genuinely coexisting; slice-by-the-counter became commercially practical once coal ovens converted to gas starting in 1934, letting pizzerias reheat and sell single slices rather than requiring a whole-pie order. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Venue variation: **Counter-service slice shop** (glass display case of whole pies, overhead menu board, narrow standing-eating counter, fast informal transaction) vs. **sit-down pizzeria restaurant** (table service, broader Italian-American menu, whole pies shared at table) — both authentic, coexisting registers; a "quick, casual" brief defaults to counter-service, a "family dinner out" brief to sit-down. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [Flour & Coffee](https://flourandcoffee.substack.com/p/authentic-new-york-pizza-slice-history-little-italy)]
- Visual/plating characteristics: Large, thin, hand-tossed slice, crisp exterior/chewy interior, sturdy enough to hold toppings but soft enough to fold in half lengthwise; bright but not oversweet red tomato sauce; low-moisture mozzarella with a light golden-and-white mottled melt (not a uniform browned sheet); a thin, visible sheen of orange grease pooling on the surface is expected, not a flaw; occasional small blistered char spots; cut into large triangular wedges (never squared) from a full round pie — the triangular cut is the point-of-difference detail versus Jersey Shore boardwalk pizza's Sicilian-style square cut. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH]
- Confidence: HIGH overall
- Sources: [Flour & Coffee — The Authentic New York Pizza Slice](https://flourandcoffee.substack.com/p/authentic-new-york-pizza-slice-history-little-italy)

  - What dominates: on a slice, the melted cheese-over-sauce surface is ~75–80% of the top area; the bare puffy crust rim ~15–20%; grease pools and char blisters are small accents under 5%. Plain cheese is the archetype; any topping is a brief's choice, not a default. [EDITORIAL]

  - Absent on purpose: fresh basil piles, fresh-mozzarella rounds, a thick or deep-dish base, square cut, knife-and-fork place settings, sauce pooled on top.

Mottled white-and-golden melted cheese covers almost the whole slice, with small beads of glossy orange grease and a few brown blisters; a narrow puffy tan crust rim along the wide end. #### Dish: Trenton tomato pie / New Jersey tomato pie
- Category: Everyday to special-occasion (a specific city's sit-down pizzeria tradition)
- Cuisine lineage: Italian-American, Trenton, NJ-specific
- Serving format: Plate (whole pie, shared, sit-down pizzeria)
- Visual/plating characteristics: Reversed construction — mozzarella and toppings go down first, crushed tomato sauce ladled on last in visible ribbons across the top, rather than sauce-then-cheese. **This is a finished-plate visual trait, not just a hidden prep step**: the red sauce remains clearly visible draped over the white cheese layer in the baked, served pie, unlike ordinary pizza where a sauce-then-cheese order buries any sauce pattern under the cheese during baking. Keeps the crust crackly, pushes tangy tomato flavor forward over cheese. Originated in Trenton, NJ in the early 20th century; Papa's Tomato Pies (1912) and De Lorenzo's are both still-operating originators.
- Common confusion: Distinguished from ordinary NY-style or Neapolitan pizza specifically by this visible top-layer sauce, which a sauce-then-cheese pie never shows once baked.
- Confidence: HIGH
- Sources: [Wikipedia: Trenton tomato pie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trenton_tomato_pie); [NJ Monthly — Trenton Tomato Pies Are Still A Staple of the New Jersey Pizza Scene](https://njmonthly.com/articles/eat-drink/the-original/); [Central Jersey — Sauce on Top](https://centraljersey.com/entertainment/trenton-tomato-pie/)
- Composition & proportions (§4.7) — whole round pie at a sit-down table:
  - What dominates: the red sauce ribbons on top, ~50–60% of the visible surface; the white cheese showing between the ribbons ~30–40%; the thin crust rim ~10%. The sauce reads as bands laid over the cheese, not a smooth red sheet under it. [EDITORIAL]
  - Components: pie ~35–40 cm across, a thin low rim ~1.5 cm [EDITORIAL]; sauce ribbons ~2–4 cm wide, crushed-tomato texture visible; cheese as a flat pale layer beneath; cut into ~8 wedges. [EDITORIAL]
  - Arrangement: sauce ribbons laid in loose parallel stripes or a spiral across the whole pie, cheese peeking through the gaps.
  - Vessel fill/depth: fills its round metal tray edge to edge; flat, low profile.
  - Served portion vs. whole dish: the pie at table centre; one portion is 2–3 wedges on a plain plate.
  - State cues: crackly dry-edged crust, matte crushed tomato, only a faint sheen where cheese shows.
  - Absent on purpose: a uniform cheese blanket hiding the sauce, heavy toppings, basil garnish, square cut.
  - Prompt-ready line: "A whole thin round pizza pie on a metal tray, its surface striped with broad ribbons of chunky crushed red tomato sauce laid over a pale white cheese layer that peeks through between the stripes. A thin, crackly tan crust rim. Cut into eight wedges; one wedge on a plain white plate beside it, about one and a half times the can's height long. No basil, no cheese covering the sauce."

#### Dish: Jersey Shore boardwalk pizza

- Cuisine lineage: Italian-American, venue-driven rather than one fixed recipe

### NYC Metro & New Jersey — Hot dogs and street carts

#### Dish: New York cart dog ("dirty water dog")
- Category: Everyday, street-food/on-the-go
- Cuisine lineage: Native-American-tradition with a German immigrant root (German vendors sold "dachshund sausages" from carts in the Bowery in the 1860s). [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH] [SOURCE: [EXP1 — Rediscovering the New York City Hot Dog Cart](https://www.exp1.com/blog/untold-nyc-history-hot-dog-cart/)]
- Serving format: Sandwich/handheld only
- Primary protein/preparation: A frankfurter kept warm by simmering in a large steel tank of seasoned, cloudy ("dirty") water — a practice that emerged because cooking hot dogs over open flames on early wooden pushcarts was a fire risk; carts moved to fireproof metal by the early 20th century. Seasoned water can include salt, garlic, cumin, vinegar, and beef stock. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH] [SOURCE: [The Takeout — Here's What 'Dirty Water' Hot Dogs Really Are](https://www.thetakeout.com/2188337/what-nyc-dirty-water-hot-dog-explained/)]
- Side dishes/condiments: Sauerkraut, spicy brown mustard, a tomato-based onion sauce
- Serving vessel: Wrapped in a thin paper napkin or foil sleeve, handed across the cart counter — not plated
- Cart appearance: A silver/stainless steel box cart on the sidewalk, a colored umbrella overhead (yellow is traditional/most common), a visible steel tank with hot dogs floating in the seasoned water, tongs, a bun-warming compartment, squeeze bottles of condiments. **Prompt-writing note**: one specific cart-umbrella brand became a near-universal fixture citywide, but that brand name is real and trademarked — describe it generically ("a yellow cart umbrella"), not by brand. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for the cart's general appearance]
- Common confusion: Should not be confused with a grilled/griddled hot dog (pale, evenly-cooked casing without char or grill marks is the defining visual difference) or a Nathan's Famous-style stand-grilled Coney Island hot dog (a different, stand-based, not independently researched this pass — flag for follow-up if a brief needs that distinction specifically).
- Confidence: HIGH overall
- Sources: [The Takeout](https://www.thetakeout.com/2188337/what-nyc-dirty-water-hot-dog-explained/); [Kitchen Encounters](https://www.bitchinfrommelanieskitchen.com/2021/10/the-history-behind-new-york-citys-dirty-water-hot-dogs.html)
- Composition & proportions (§4.7) — one cart dog:
  - What dominates: the soft pale bun ~50% of what shows; the frank ~25–30% (visible along the top slit and at both ends); toppings ~20–25% as a narrow strip along the top. [EDITORIAL]
  - Components: frank ~15 cm long, ~2 cm wide — **a little longer than the can is tall** [EDITORIAL, standard frank size]; bun roughly the same length; sauerkraut a loose pale strip ~1 cm deep; onion sauce a thin red-brown ribbon; spicy brown mustard one zigzag line.
  - Arrangement: toppings laid lengthwise along the frank; the frank's ends often poke slightly beyond the bun.
  - Vessel fill/depth: nested in an open paper napkin or foil sleeve that covers the lower half of the bun.
  - Served portion vs. whole dish: 1–2 dogs per person; if two, side by side in separate sleeves.
  - State cues: moist, pale, evenly cooked casing with a slight wet sheen; soft steamed bun, no toasting.
  - Absent on purpose: grill marks or char, toasted bun, green relish, Chicago-style tomato wedges and pickle spears, cheese, a plate.
  - Prompt-ready line: "One plain boiled hot dog in a soft pale steamed bun, a little longer than the can is tall, nestled in an open white paper napkin on a steel cart ledge. The smooth, pale pink-brown frank has a moist sheen and no grill marks. Along its top: a loose strip of pale sauerkraut, a thin ribbon of red-brown onion sauce and a zigzag of spicy brown mustard. No relish, no cheese."

#### Dish: NYC halal cart gyro
- Category: Everyday, street-food/on-the-go
- Cuisine lineage: A genuine coexisting variant of the Greek-American gyro (per `country-file-schema.md` §4.6) — a different, non-hierarchical register, not a lesser version
- Serving format: Sandwich/handheld
- Primary protein: Chopped/griddled chicken and/or lamb, often more heavily spiced than a traditional Greek-American gyro-spit preparation
- Side dishes/condiments: A signature sauce trio — white sauce applied as the base layer directly on the meat, red/hot sauce drizzled over it, and a rarer green sauce — plus lettuce, tomato, onion, sometimes cucumber
- Serving vessel: Rolled tightly in pita "like a burrito" rather than folded open-faced, wrapped in foil — staged resting on the cart counter or a napkin, not held up to camera, per the anti-hand-holding rule
- Cart appearance: A silver metal box cart with a colored umbrella overhead (yellow is traditional/common)
- Historical/demographic context: Muslim immigrant vendors (predominantly Egypt, Bangladesh, Afghanistan) took over the street-cart trade in the late 1980s/1990s from Greek vendors, who had themselves succeeded Italian and German vendors. In 1990, 306 New Yorkers identifying as Italian/German first-generation immigrants worked as street vendors; by 2005, that number was zero, while vendors from Egypt, Bangladesh, and Afghanistan rose from 69 to 563 over the same period.
- Confidence: HIGH for the sauce trio, wrap style, cart appearance, and demographic-shift data
- Sources: [NYSF — White Sauce, Hot Sauce, Green Sauce](https://newyorkstreetfood.com/halal/nyc-halal-cart-sauce-guide/); [Wikipedia: Halal cart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halal_cart); [Macaulay CUNY/OpenLab](https://openlab.macaulay.cuny.edu/nyfoodatlas/2025/05/15/more-than-chicken-over-rice-the-true-story-behind-nycs-halal-carts/)
- Composition & proportions (§4.7) — one wrapped gyro:
  - What dominates: the foil-wrapped cylinder is most of the object (~60–70%); only the peeled-back top end shows food — there, chopped meat ~50%, white sauce ~25%, lettuce/tomato ~25%. [EDITORIAL]
  - Components: wrap ~20–25 cm long, ~6–7 cm thick — **about the can's width, and nearly twice its height** [EDITORIAL]; meat as small chopped pieces ~1–2 cm; white sauce as a thick drizzle; red sauce as a few thin streaks; shredded lettuce and 2–4 small tomato pieces at the opening.
  - Arrangement: tightly rolled, closed at the bottom, foil peeled back 5–8 cm at the top to show the filling.
  - Vessel fill/depth: lying on the cart ledge or on a napkin, no plate.
  - Served portion vs. whole dish: one wrap per person.
  - State cues: warm meat with a light oil gloss; white sauce glossy and slightly running; foil crinkled.
  - Absent on purpose: an open folded pita (Greek-diner style), tzatziki cucumber chunks as the dominant sauce, fries inside, a plate, a hand holding it.
  - Prompt-ready line: "A tightly rolled pita wrap about the can's width and nearly twice its height, wrapped in crinkled foil and resting on a steel cart ledge, the foil peeled back at the top. At the open end: small chopped pieces of browned, spiced chicken, a thick drizzle of glossy white sauce with a few thin red hot-sauce streaks, shredded lettuce and a few small tomato pieces. No plate, no fries."

#### Dish: NYC halal cart chicken-and-rice platter
- Category: Everyday, street-food/on-the-go — arguably the single most emblematic halal cart order, sold from the same carts with the same sauce trio as the gyro above

[CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]

#### Dish: New Haven apizza (full authoritative entry — supersedes the compact `us.md` index summary)
- Category: Everyday, but also a genuine food-tourism destination dish

#### Dish: Maine wild blueberry pie

#### Dish: New England boiled dinner (corned beef and cabbage)
- Category: Special-occasion to everyday (a traditional cold-weather Saturday/Sunday dinner; corned beef and cabbage specifically is now also tied to St. Patrick's Day nationally)
- Cuisine lineage: **Two lineages layered on top of each other, stated explicitly rather than resolved into one.** Older Yankee/colonial-New-England roots as a one-pot dinner built around a single salted meat, simmered with root vegetables — the specific "New England boiled dinner" name reportedly first appeared in print in the 1890s. Separately, 19th-century Irish immigrants substituted corned beef for the bacon in their home dish "bacon and cabbage," because corned beef was cheaper and more available in American cities — this is the specific origin of "corned beef and cabbage" as an Irish-American dish. Best understood as two related but distinct lineages that converged on the same core dish. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Serving format: Plate, sliced/carved from a whole simmered piece of corned beef
- Primary protein/starch/accompaniment: Corned beef brisket, simmered whole in a large pot; cabbage wedges and root vegetables (potatoes, carrots, turnips/rutabaga, parsnips, pearl onions) added partway through and boiled until tender in the same pot
- Side dishes/condiments: Commonly served with mustard, prepared horseradish, and butter on the side for the vegetables. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM]
- Serving vessel: A large platter for family-style presentation, or an individual plate
- Leftover tradition: **Red flannel hash** — dicing and pan-frying leftover corned beef, potatoes, and (distinctively) beets, which stain the hash a reddish-pink color evoking flannel fabric; traditionally fried until a browned crust forms on the bottom, then flipped. A genuinely useful staging option for a "next-day"/leftover-themed lunch scene distinct from the boiled dinner itself. [CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Visual/plating characteristics: Sliced corned beef (grey-pink, with a visible pink-tinged curing color at the edge distinct from plain roast beef's brown-grey) arranged on a platter, surrounded by pale yellow-green cabbage wedges and root vegetables, often finished with fresh chopped parsley; the overall palette is muted and matte — no glossy sauce coating. Red flannel hash, by contrast, reads as a browned, crisp-edged pan-fried hash with a visibly pink-to-magenta tint from the beets — a completely different, much more saturated-color dish from the same source ingredients.
- Common confusion: The plain "corned beef and cabbage" framing (eaten nationally on St. Patrick's Day) is often visually simpler/narrower than the full "New England boiled dinner," which traditionally includes multiple root vegetables in the same pot.
- Confidence: HIGH for the core dish and the red flannel hash leftover tradition; MEDIUM for the two-lineage origin framing and side-dish specifics
- Sources: [Yankee Magazine — In Praise of the New England Boiled Dinner](https://newengland.com/food/main-dishes/new-england-boiled-dinner/); [Yankee Magazine — Classic New England Red Flannel Hash](https://newengland.com/food/classic-new-england-red-flannel-hash/); [Food Republic — What Is New England Boiled Dinner](https://www.foodrepublic.com/1394020/what-is-new-england-boiled-dinner-one-pot-flavor/)
- Composition & proportions (§4.7) — family-style platter and one plated portion:
  - What dominates: vegetables ~55–60% of the platter (cabbage the largest single element), sliced corned beef ~30–35%, parsley and mustard minor accents. [EDITORIAL]
  - Components:

    | Component | Real size | Count (platter / one plate) | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Corned beef slices | ~10–15 cm long, ~5–8 mm thick [EDITORIAL] | ~8–12 / 2–3 | Grey-pink, pink-tinged edge, fat rim | Fanned in overlapping slices, centre or one side |
    | Cabbage wedges | ~8–10 cm, core attached | 4–6 / 1 | Pale yellow-green, soft, translucent edges | Around the beef |
    | Potatoes | Small whole ~5 cm or halves | 6–8 / 2 | Pale, matte | Around the edge |

    | Turnip/rutabaga, parsnip | Chunks ~4–5 cm | 4–6 / 1–2 | Pale yellow-cream | Tucked between |

  - Arrangement: beef fanned at the centre, vegetables ringed around it; on a plate, beef on one side, vegetables on the other.
  - Vessel fill/depth: platter ~40 cm, covered to the rim; plate ~27 cm, about two-thirds covered.
