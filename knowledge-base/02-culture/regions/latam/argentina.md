# Argentina

## FILE ROLE & METHOD

This file is Argentina's country file: a single national staging brief
with seven labelled internal zones. One TCCC hero beverage per scene
(chosen per brief — see HERO PRODUCT SLOT), staged against real Argentine
dishes, vessels and settings, with the full beverage landscape documented
as context even where a beverage (alcohol, mate) is never itself staged.

**Scope** (same default as the Spain/Mexico files, not a new decision):
almuerzo (midday meal), cena (evening meal) and snacks are in scope.
**Merienda** (the late-afternoon snack of mate or coffee with facturas,
alfajores or tortas fritas) is treated as a snack occasion and is **in
scope** — flagged for the reviewer because it is closer to a breakfast-
style table than any other in-scope occasion (see GAP LOG). Desayuno is
out of scope except via the opt-in Morning Module. No beverage other than
the hero TCCC product is staged; others are documented in ICONIC
BEVERAGES as context only.

**Relationship to `uruguay.md`.** Asado, chimichurri, milanesa (and
napolitana), choripán, pizza with fainá, ñoquis del 29, torta frita,
pascualina and mate are **shared Río de la Plata heritage**. This file
does not re-argue attribution: where `uruguay.md` already says a dish is
shared and cannot be split by nationality (milanesa napolitana, pizza +
fainá), this file agrees and only adds what is Argentine-specific (cuts,
grate shape, porteño pizza thickness, regional empanadas). Where the two
files touch the same fact, the Uruguay wording is cited, not restated.

### Structural decision: one file, seven zones (recommendation — reviewer has final say)

The `country-file-schema.md` §1.1 swap test, applied with this pass's
evidence:

- **A strong national core travels everywhere**: asado on a parrilla,
  choripán, milanesa, empanadas, pizza, pasta on Sunday and ñoquis on the
  29th, the merienda with facturas, locro and pastelitos on 25 May.
  [HIGH — each is documented as national in the sources in its entry]
- **Some dishes are genuinely form-changing by region** (§4.2): the
  **empanada** (small, juicy, oven-baked salteña with potato; fried
  tucumana of hand-cut matambre; large cordobesa with raisins and a
  sweet-savoury note; mendocina with olive and egg [MEDIUM-HIGH]); the
  **tamal and humita** (NOA, round and small in Salta/Jujuy, rectangular
  in Tucumán [MEDIUM]); the **roast itself** (whole lamb on an iron cross
  in Patagonia vs. beef on a grate in the Pampas [HIGH]).
- **Environment does not travel**: Buenos Aires's European-style
  apartment blocks, the flat pampa, Córdoba's sierras, the Andean NOA
  with adobe and cardón cactus, the humid red-earth Northeast, Mendoza's
  vineyards under the Andes and Patagonian steppe and lakes look nothing
  alike.

**Recommendation: one national file, seven zones**, handled as dish-
variant and environment deltas — the same call made for Mexico, Spain,
Germany and South Africa. Official/common regionalisations (NOA, NEA,
Cuyo, Pampeana, Patagonia) are real and widely used in Argentine sources
[HIGH — Spanish-language Wikipedia *Gastronomía de Argentina* and
*Regions of Argentina* (via search), argentina.gob.ar "Cocina Abierta"];
this file splits Pampeana into three staging zones because Buenos Aires
city, the rural pampa and Córdoba look and eat differently enough to
matter on camera [EDITORIAL].

**Flagged spinout candidate (not triggered)**: the **Northwest (NOA,
zone 4)** — Andean-rooted cuisine (locro, humitas, tamales, salteñas
baked in clay ovens, llama and quinoa in the Puna), Andean architecture
(adobe, cardón-wood, whitewashed colonial churches) and a distinct
highland light. If briefs set there grow past roughly a quarter of
Argentina usage, split to `argentina-noa.md` per `country-file-schema.md`
§2.2. **Recommendation for the reviewer, not a decision** (§7).

### Zones (one scheme, used throughout)

| # | Zone | Covers | Visual register |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Buenos Aires (AMBA) | Ciudad de Buenos Aires + the Greater Buenos Aires conurbano, La Plata | Dense European-style apartment blocks with balconies, PH houses and casas chorizo, cafés and bodegones on corners, plane trees on sidewalks, the Río de la Plata's flat grey-brown horizon |
| 2 | Pampa & southern Litoral | Interior Buenos Aires province, La Pampa, southern Santa Fe (Rosario), southern Entre Ríos | Flat grassland to the horizon, estancias with eucalyptus windbreaks and windmills, small towns with a plaza and a church, river towns on the Paraná |
| 3 | Córdoba & centre | Córdoba province (city, sierras) | A big university city with colonial Jesuit core and modern towers; the Sierras' stone, streams and pine; the country's fernet capital (never staged) |
| 4 | Northwest (NOA) | Jujuy, Salta, Tucumán, Catamarca, La Rioja, Santiago del Estero | Andean valleys and quebradas in red and ochre rock, adobe houses, cardón cactus, whitewashed colonial churches, clay ovens (hornos de barro), peñas |
| 5 | Northeast (NEA) & Litoral norte | Misiones, Corrientes, Chaco, Formosa, northern Santa Fe and Entre Ríos | Subtropical, humid, green; red earth (tierra colorada), yerba plantations, rivers and islands, Guaraní influence |
| 6 | Cuyo | Mendoza, San Juan, San Luis | Irrigated vineyards and poplar rows against the Andes, adobe and brick fincas, dry bright light, irrigation ditches (acequias) lining city streets |
| 7 | Patagonia | Neuquén, Río Negro, Chubut, Santa Cruz, Tierra del Fuego | Windy steppe, lakes and forests at Bariloche/San Martín de los Andes, alpine-style wood-and-stone chalets, Atlantic coast towns, sheep estancias |

Zone boundaries are a staging convenience, not a claim about identity;
Santa Fe and Entre Ríos straddle zones 2 and 5, and San Luis is sometimes
grouped with the centre [EDITORIAL].

### Default when no zone is named

Fall back to **zone 1 (Buenos Aires) at the everyday register**: a
Buenos Aires apartment or PH, a neighbourhood parrilla, bodegón or
pizzería. Buenos Aires is the country's largest metro by far and the
origin of the most portable urban formats (porteño pizza, milanesa
napolitana, the bodegón). **For an asado brief with no zone, use a home
patio/quincho in the conurbano or zone 2**, not a CABA flat balcony.
[EDITORIAL fallback — not a sourced "most typical Argentina" claim]

## METHOD NOTE (read first)

**Build method, this pass.** Drafted directly from model knowledge and
checked with 35 WebSearch queries in one session (the session's shared
search budget ran out on the 36th — a query about TCCC's reported
alcoholic ready-to-drink launch in Argentina, which is therefore unread;
see GAP LOG). Priority went to the claims a scene visibly depends on:
pack formats and can size, meal timing, housing type, cut and piece
sizes, regional empanadas, festivals with 2026/2027 dates. Tags mean:

- **[HIGH] / [MEDIUM] / [LOW]** — schema §6 tags earned this pass (HIGH =
  2+ independent corroborating sources; MEDIUM = 1 credible source).
- **[MEDIUM — not independently re-checked this pass]** /
  **[LOW-MEDIUM — not independently re-checked]** — model knowledge that
  is uncontested general culinary or cultural knowledge but was not
  individually searched.
- **[EDITORIAL]** — a judgment call (defaults, shares, counts,
  caricature-avoidance guidance, zone boundaries), never a factual claim.
- **[HIGH — first-party test]** — this project's own image-generation
  findings (`coca-cola-guidelines.md` §1, `country-file-schema.md` §7.5).
  **No Argentina-specific image tests have been run yet.**

**Writing principle.** Describe what the camera sees: surface, sheen,
crust, char, cut face, crumb, and real-world size against the can.
Background notes only where they prevent a visual error.

**File-wide rules for every scene built from this file:**

1. **Text as atmosphere.** Menu boards, chalkboards, **fileteado porteño**
   (the ornate painted lettering on Buenos Aires shop signs and bodegón
   walls), football posters, price cards, packaging and cooler lids may
   appear only as heavily blurred, unreadable colour. Never quote words
   or prices. Any readable word, number or brand mark means reject or
   retouch. Blur instructions are known to fail. [HIGH — first-party test;
   `country-file-schema.md` §7.5] Fileteado is a strong prior for "Buenos
   Aires" — expect to fight it.
2. **The hero product is a TCCC beverage chosen per brief.** Fill the
   HERO PRODUCT SLOT with the exact product, variant and format. Branding
   is composited in post, never trusted from the generation
   (`coca-cola-guidelines.md` §1–2). If the brief names no product, ask.
3. **No alcohol in any scene, ever, and never a TCCC product as a mixer with alcohol (non-alcoholic TCCC-brand mixes are allowed, schema §5.5).**
   Argentina has the single most famous Coca-Cola mixed drink in Latin
   America — **fernet con Coca** — documented in ICONIC BEVERAGES as
   context, **never staged, never implied** (no tall glass of dark cola
   with a tan foam head at an asado or party, no dark spirits bottle near
   the product). Wine and beer are equally excluded from asado tables.
4. **No drinks in frame other than the hero product**, unless the brief
   explicitly allows a named non-alcoholic companion. **This includes
   mate** (gourd, bombilla, thermos) and the glass **soda siphon** on a
   bodegón table — both are strong priors for "Argentina" and both must
   be negated explicitly. (Mate is a shared beverage/ritual, not a dish —
   Fernando, 2026-10-01, schema §5.6; `uruguay.md` now matches.)
5. **Nothing held in a hand.** Stage food and product resting on a
   surface, even for hand-eaten choripán, empanadas and sandwiches
   (`country-file-schema.md` §7.5).
6. **Breakfast is out of scope**, except via the opt-in Morning Module;
   merienda is in scope (see above).
7. **No religious or dietary hard rule shapes the everyday table.**
   Argentina's table is not governed by halal, pork avoidance or a fasting
   month; pork, beef and offal all appear freely. The one custom worth
   knowing is **Lent/Semana Santa** fish and humitas (see FESTIVALS) —
   observed by some families, not a staging constraint. A brief for a
   Jewish or Muslim Argentine household (Buenos Aires has large Jewish
   and Arab-descended communities) should be treated as its own brief.
   [MEDIUM — not independently re-checked this pass]

### HERO PRODUCT SLOT

Every scene carries one TCCC hero product, chosen by the brief. This file
uses `south-africa.md`'s generalised slot; since 2026-09-27 that is the
project-wide rule for every country file (the brief dictates the SKU,
never the region — `country-file-schema.md` §5.4, `DECISIONS.md`).

**Template:**
> [HERO PRODUCT]: {brand and variant exactly as named on pack}, in
> {format and size}, {dominant pack colour and material cue},
> {negated lookalikes}. {Position: standing upright on the surface,
> label facing camera or turned slightly}. Pack text will be composited
> in post.

**Rules:**
- **Name the variant; negate the closest lookalike** (Original vs. Sin
  Azúcar vs. Light; Sprite vs. a generic lemon soda; Schweppes tonic vs.
  a gin-and-tonic cue). An unspecified "Coca-Cola can" rendered as the
  wrong variant in 2 of 3 generations [HIGH — first-party test,
  `coca-cola-guidelines.md` §1].
- **Argentina is a 354 mL-can market, not a 330 mL one.** Argentine
  retail (Coca-Cola's own Argentine online store, Jumbo, Mercado Libre,
  several distributors) sells Coca-Cola Original and the zero-sugar
  variant in **354 mL cans**, sold singly and in six-packs. 354 mL is the
  North American 12 fl oz can (355 mL rounded down on the label), so
  **use the North American can dimensions (~123 mm tall, ~66 mm
  diameter)** — the `coca-cola-guidelines.md` §4.3 "non-US market ⇒
  330 mL" default does **not** apply here (same finding as Mexico).
  [CONFIDENCE: HIGH for 354 mL as the Argentine standard can — tienda.coca-cola.com.ar,
  Jumbo and Mercado Libre listings agree (via search); MEDIUM for
  applying the US dimensions — no Argentine can height was found]
- **The brief always names the SKU — never the region or this file.** If
  a brief names no product, ask for one rather than inferring it. The
  list below is reference for whoever writes the brief, not a default:
  - parrilla restaurant, bodegón, pizzería, café: a **350 cc returnable

  - on the go, kiosco, office, cancha: a 500 mL or 600 mL PET or a can

    the midground — **1.25 L returnable glass**, **2.25 L PET** or
    **2.5 L / 3 L PET** — with one filled glass per place setting
    (`coca-cola-guidelines.md` §4.4)
  [MEDIUM — the formats are confirmed current (see SCALE REFERENCE);

- **Use the chosen format's real dimensions as the scale anchor** (see

- **One hero product per scene** unless the brief asks for several.

### ICONIC BEVERAGES (documented context — staging rules follow)

This section documents the real Argentine drinks landscape, including
alcohol and mate, and restricts only what is staged, never what is
recorded — the same design `south-africa.md` and `mexico.md` use.

**TCCC Argentina portfolio — partly verified.** Coca-Cola (Original,
Sin Azúcar, Light), Sprite, Fanta, Schweppes; Cepita Del Valle (juices),
Aquarius (flavoured water), Bonaqua, Benedictino and Smartwater (water),
Powerade, and Ades (soy-based). [MEDIUM-HIGH — Coca-Cola Argentina's own
brands page and press coverage agree (via search); the list is not read
in full]

**Non-alcoholic context (not staged unless the brief allows a named
companion):**
- **Mate** — the national infusion: a gourd (calabaza, wood, steel or
  silicone), a metal bombilla, a thermos, yerba packed near the rim, one
  person (cebador) pouring and passing round. Argentines consumed about
  **6.1 kg of yerba per person in 2023**, and consumption grew ~12% in
  January–July 2025. [HIGH — INYM data via Infobae, Perfil, Bolsa de
  Comercio de Rosario (via search)] **Cross-reference**: `uruguay.md`
  notes that Uruguay's per-capita consumption is higher than Argentina's;
  nothing here contradicts that — mate is shared, not "Argentine". Mate
  is carried everywhere (parks, offices, car trips, the beach) and is the
  default merienda drink. **Rule 4: never in frame** unless the brief
  names it; it is the single most likely uninvited prop in an Argentine
  scene.
- **Tereré** — cold mate with water or juice, a hot-weather custom of
  the Northeast (zone 5) shared with Paraguay. [MEDIUM — not
  independently re-checked this pass]
- **Café con leche, cortado, submarino** (a bar of chocolate melted into
  hot milk), **tea** — café and merienda drinks. [MEDIUM — not
  independently re-checked this pass]
- **Soda de sifón** — carbonated water from a glass or plastic siphon
  bottle, a fixture on bodegón tables and a classic wine-cutter.
  [HIGH for the siphon as a bodegón fixture — canal26 and Pipol bodegón
  guides (via search)] Rule 4: remove it.

**Alcohol context (never staged):**
- **Wine** — Malbec and Mendoza/Cuyo vineyards; wine is the traditional
  asado and Sunday-lunch drink. [MEDIUM — not independently re-checked
  this pass, uncontested] Never on the table in a staged scene.
- **Beer** — the everyday drink at asados, football and bars with
  friends. [MEDIUM — not re-checked]
- **Fernet con Coca** — **the famous pairing, documented here and only
  here.** A two-ingredient mixed drink of **Fernet-Branca (an Italian
  bitter herbal liqueur) and Coca-Cola** over ice, described as the most
  consumed mixed drink in Argentina. [HIGH — English Wikipedia *Fernet con
  coca*, VinePair, Visit Argentina (argentina.travel) (via search)]
  - **Origin and geography**: the mix spread from **Córdoba** in the
    1970s–80s, often credited to the city's large student population;
    Córdoba is estimated to account for roughly **30–40% of national
    fernet consumption**, and the city has announced a monument to it.
    Fratelli Branca's own advertising pushed the combination nationally
    from the late 1980s/early 1990s. [HIGH for the Córdoba association;
    MEDIUM for the 30–40% share — one commercial-press estimate]
  - **The "70/30"**: Branca's recommendation is ~30% fernet to ~70% cola
    over ice with a thick foam head (~2 cm); Córdoba home pours are said
    to run stronger (closer to 50/50) and Buenos Aires weaker. [MEDIUM —
    El Cronista, Kindred Cocktails, argentineasado.com agree on 70/30;
    the regional ratios are one source]
  - **TCCC's own relationship to the pairing — flag for the reviewer.**
    Perfil reports that Coca-Cola Argentina and Fernet-Branca **did a
    joint real-time social campaign (#FernetconCoca) on Día del Amigo**,
    via Mercado McCann, with Coca-Cola's communications director quoted
    on bringing together "two brands united in people's imagination".
    [MEDIUM — one press report (via search); date not read] Separately,
    an El Cronista headline reports that **Coca-Cola is entering the
    alcoholic-drinks business in Argentina with two ready-to-drink
    cocktails**; the article was not read (search budget exhausted).
    Neither changes the staging rule. **Never stage fernet, a fernet-
    and-cola glass (tall glass, near-black liquid, tan foam cap), a
    Fernet-Branca-style dark bottle, or any TCCC-branded alcoholic RTD.**
  - **Model-drift warning [EDITORIAL]**: in Córdoba (zone 3), party,
    football, asado-evening or "Argentine friends" scenes an image model
    may add a tall glass or plastic cup of dark cola with a creamy tan
    foam head, or a two-litre bottle cut in half as a jug ("fernet en
    bidón/en jarra" is a real student/party habit [LOW-MEDIUM — not
    re-checked]). Negate explicitly: "no foam-topped dark drinks, no cut
    plastic bottles, no spirits bottles".
- **Coca-Cola with wine ("calimocho"/"jesuita")** — known in Argentina
  but far less iconic than fernet con Coca. [LOW — not re-checked]
  Never staged.

**Meal clock and light** — the most load-bearing timing facts in this file.

or 23:00"; merienda "between 16:00 and 18:00" as the bridge between lunch
and a late dinner, explained by work and school hours running to 18:00
and people getting home around 20:00. [CONFIDENCE: HIGH — Billiken,
Remitly's Argentina meal-time guide, Baires Magazine and

**Light.** Buenos Aires sunset ranges from **17:50 (earliest, ~12 June)
to 20:11 (latest, ~6 January)**. [HIGH — sunrise/sunset tables (via
search)] So a **cena at 21:30 is after dark in every season**: stage it
under warm interior light, never golden-hour sun. A summer Sunday asado
at 13:00–15:00 is hard, high midday light with shade under a roofed
patio or trees; a winter lunch has low, pale, slanting light.
Argentina is in the **Southern Hemisphere**: Christmas and New Year are
midsummer (30 °C+ in Buenos Aires), July is winter. [HIGH]

**§5.2 cross-country contrasts:**
- **Mexico eats its main meal at 14:00–16:00 and a light cena**;
  Argentina's weekday **cena is the main family meal and comes at
  21:00–22:00** — closer to Spain's late dinner and to Uruguay's 21:30+
  (`uruguay.md`) than to Mexico. [MEDIUM synthesis of HIGH inputs]
- **The merienda is a named, daily meal**, not an optional snack: it is
  what makes a 22:00 dinner workable. [HIGH]
- **Street density is far lower than Mexico's.** Argentine eating out is
  sit-down (parrilla, bodegón, pizzería, café) or takeaway (rotisería,
  empanadas by the dozen, delivery); street food concentrates at
  riverside promenades, stadiums, festivals and roadside stands rather
  than on every corner. [MEDIUM — consistent with `uruguay.md`'s
  "grab-and-go barely exists" for the shared Río de la Plata register;
  not measured]

**Table norms:**
- **Bread basket (panera) on the table** at almost every meal — pan
  francés/flauta slices or small rolls (mignón), placed in a cloth-lined
  basket, no side plates. [MEDIUM-HIGH — `uruguay.md` cites La Nación on
  Buenos Aires paneras; the Río de la Plata norm is shared]
- **Chimichurri and salsa criolla** in small bowls, spooned by the diner
  onto grilled meat — not pre-applied. Chimichurri: parsley, oregano,
  garlic, chile flakes, oil and vinegar, loose and herb-flecked; salsa
  criolla: small-diced tomato, onion and bell pepper in oil and vinegar.
  [MEDIUM-HIGH — `uruguay.md` sources for chimichurri; salsa criolla MEDIUM,
  not re-checked this pass]
- **Cutlery**: Continental (fork left, knife right), serrated steak
  knives at a parrilla; sandwiches, empanadas, choripán and pizza are
  eaten by hand. [MEDIUM — shared with `uruguay.md`] Rule 5 still applies.
- **Sobremesa** — lingering after lunch or dinner is normal. [MEDIUM —
  not independently re-checked this pass]
- **The asador** — one person (traditionally a man, increasingly not)
  runs the grill for hours; guests pick at chorizo and achuras as a

### VISUAL & PLATING NORMS

- **Doneness: jugoso / a punto.** A pink, juicy centre with a browned
  crust is the default; a bife de chorizo "should show three colours —
  browned outside, grey inner edge, red juicy centre". Asado de tira and
  vacío are cooked longer and read browner, with rendered, crisped fat.
  [HIGH for the jugoso preference — shared with `uruguay.md`'s INAC
  source; three-colour bife MEDIUM (hacerasado.com.ar)]
- **Grill marks: faint parallel bars, never crosshatch.** Argentine
  parrillas typically use **V-profile grate bars that channel fat to a
  side gutter** instead of onto the coals, with wood burned in a side
  **brasero** and embers raked underneath — slow, clean heat without
  flare-ups. The result is an even golden-brown surface with, at most,
  soft parallel bands, not bold black crosshatch. [HIGH for V-grates and
  brasero as the Argentine parrilla pattern — two builder/guide sources
  (via search); `uruguay.md` contrasts Uruguay's round rods with
  Argentina's V-grooved grates, consistent with this] **Do not import
  Uruguay's "mottled round-rod" note wholesale** — both countries avoid
  crosshatch; the grate shape differs.
- **Plating is generous and informal.** Asado comes whole or in large
  pieces on a board; a parrilla plate is one big cut with little or no
  garnish; bodegón milanesas overhang oval platters. Sauce swooshes,
  micro-herbs and stacked towers read as fine dining, not everyday
  Argentina. [MEDIUM — consistent with the bodegón and asado sources]
- **Palette**: mahogany-brown grilled beef and translucent golden fat;
  the herb-green-and-red of chimichurri; salsa criolla's red, white and
  green dice; the pale golden crumb of pan francés; ivory mozzarella
  browned in patches; dulce de leche's caramel brown; quince paste's
  deep amber-red. [EDITORIAL synthesis]
- **Colour grading**: neutral, true to zone — clear dry Andean light in
  the NOA and Cuyo, soft grey-humid light on the Río de la Plata, crisp
  cold light in Patagonia. **Avoid** both a Mexico-style warm/yellow
  filter and a desaturated sepia "old Buenos Aires tango" cast. [EDITORIAL]

  Buenos Aires.** The 2022 National Census counts **76.7% of occupied
  private dwellings as houses (casas), 19.6% as apartments (departamentos)
  and 3.7% other types** (ranchos, casillas, rented rooms, family hotels

  censo.gob.ar (via search)] [SOURCE: [Censo 2022 — el 76,7% de las

  (most CABA households live in departamentos). [MEDIUM — widely known;
  the CABA-specific census share was not searched this pass]
  **§5.2 contrast**: nationally close to Mexico's 73% single houses and
  the opposite of Spain's majority-flat pattern; but a zone 1 CABA scene

  scene to a house. [EDITORIAL on the staging default]
- **House and flat types to stage** [MEDIUM — not independently
  re-checked this pass]:
  - **CABA apartment**: 1950s–70s concrete blocks or older French/Italian-
    style buildings with high ceilings, parquet floors, tall wooden

    secondary register.
- **Gen Z lens (§5.3)**: **~37–38% of 25–34-year-olds still live with
  their parents**, and the average leaving-home age is now about **27.5

  Urbano analysis of INDEC's EPH, reported by Infobae, La Gaceta, Perfil

  - **Gaucho and tango costume Argentina**: bombachas, boinas, ponchos,

    peña, Caminito) and only for an explicit brief.
  - **"Buenos Aires is Paris"**: grand Belle Époque interiors as the
    default home. Most homes are ordinary brick houses and mid-century
    flats.
  - **Mate everywhere**: authentic, but rule 4 excludes it; do not let it
    creep in as décor.
  - **Football shrines**: shirts, scarves and flags on every wall.
  - **Crisis framing**: shuttered shops, graffiti, protest imagery as the
    "real Argentina".
  - The ordinary baseline is a tidy, lived-in flat or brick house with a
    patio, or a busy neighbourhood parrilla, pizzería or café.

## CROSS-CUTTING REGISTER: ASADO & PARRILLA

- **The rig**: brick or steel parrilla at waist height, a **side brasero**
  (a steel basket or chamber where wood — often quebracho or local
  hardwood — or charcoal burns down), and **V-profile grate bars** feeding
  a fat gutter; embers are raked under the grate in the amount each cut
  needs. [HIGH — builder and guide sources (via search)]
- **The order**: thick cuts (vacío, wide tira) go on first for 1–1.5 h;
  **chinchulines** almost at the same time (~50 min); then mollejas and
  thinner cuts (lomo, colita de cuadril, thin tira); **chorizos** ~15 min
  after those (30–40 min); **morcillas** last (~15 min, just heated).
  Large cuts at the high back of the grate, chorizos in the middle,
  achuras at the front/low end. [HIGH — BAE Negocios, Crónica, Infoagro
  agree (via search)]
- **What guests see first**: chorizo and morcilla, often as **choripán**,
  and achuras, eaten standing near the grill with bread — a *picada*
  before the sit-down cuts. [MEDIUM — consistent with the order above;
  the standing-first-course custom not separately sourced]
- **Staging**: the grill with meat on it can be the background; the
  foreground is the table or a board of carved meat. Smoke light, not
  billowing. No flames licking the meat (that reads as a gas BBQ).
  [EDITORIAL]

## CELEBRATIONS & LARGE GATHERINGS

### How large gatherings work here

- **Who gathers.** Family and friends in roughly equal weight: the
  weekend asado is as often a friends' event as a family one. In a 2022
  survey reported by Infobae, about 85% of respondents host asados for
  family and friends (52.9% habitually), and the most common group size
  was **6–10 people (32.9%)**, followed by **more than 15 (27.8%)**,
  11–15 (20.4%) and under 5 (17.4%). [MEDIUM — one survey, reported by
  Infobae] **Economic context**: a 2024 survey reported by Infobae found
  eight in ten Argentines had cut back on or stopped asados for cost
  reasons. [MEDIUM — one survey] Stage gatherings as ordinary and
  modest, not lavish [EDITORIAL].
- **Life events are mid-sized to large.** Wedding budgets are quoted for
  about 100–150 guests, with 70–120 described as a mid-sized wedding and
  120–250 as large (El Cronista, Ámbito, wedding-budget guides). Fiestas
  de 15 are planned on the same catering model. [LOW-MEDIUM — no single
  survey average was found; these are budget-guide brackets]
- **Where (intake venues).** Asados, birthdays and Christmas: **home
  outdoor** (patio, quincho, garden, terrace) or **home indoor** in
  winter and in CABA flats. Weddings and fiestas de 15: a rented
  **salón** or quinta (country house) with catering ("other"). Children's
  birthdays: home, or a rented **salón de fiestas infantiles** or play
  centre ("other"). Restaurant private rooms host smaller milestones.
  [EDITORIAL; MEDIUM for salones and catering being the standard — vendor
  and press sources]
- **Table form.** At home: one **long table**, often two tables end to
  end, or the quincho's built-in long table with benches; plastic or
  folding chairs added. At salones: round tables of 10, plus a head table
  or the honoured person's table. [EDITORIAL]
- **Who serves and how.** At an asado, **the asador** (traditionally a
  man of the house, increasingly anyone) carves on a board and hands
  pieces down the table; sides sit in bowls along the table. At salones,
  waiters (mozos) serve a sequence: recepción (standing finger food),
  entrada, plato principal, postre, then a **mesa dulce** (dessert
  buffet), the cake and a late-night **fin de fiesta** snack. [MEDIUM —
  Ámbito and catering vendors list this sequence]
- **Plates and cutlery.** Home asado: everyday ceramic plates and steak
  knives, often mismatched; disposable plates and cups appear at big
  birthdays. Salones: white china, full cutlery, cloth napkins.
  [EDITORIAL]
- **Snapshot-staging default for Argentina [EDITORIAL].** The three most
  authentic crowd cues: (1) **a wooden board of carved meat bigger than
  the visible diners could finish**, with several bread baskets and
  salad bowls running down the table; (2) **the parrilla soft in the
  background** with more meat still on the grate; (3) **the long table
  running out of frame** with mismatched chairs. Use two of the three.
  For salón events, swap (2) for round tables with white cloths soft
  behind.
- **Product format.** When the brief allows a multi-serve bottle, the
  implied gathering justifies a 2.25 L or larger bottle in the midground
  of the visible stretch (schema §5.7). Remove every wine glass, beer
  bottle, fernet glass, cut plastic bottle used as a jug, the soda siphon
  and the mate kit (file-wide rules 3–4); at a real Argentine party all
  of these are present, so negate them explicitly.

#### Celebration: Weekend family or friends' asado (asado del domingo)
- Type: community or family gathering
- When: Sunday (or Saturday) midday, eating from ~13:30, sobremesa until
  17:00 or later; intake time: **midday** (also **golden-hour** for
  summer evening asados).
- Gathering: 6–15 typically, often more; **home outdoor** (patio,
  quincho, garden), in winter the quincho or indoors. [MEDIUM — Infobae
  survey above]
- The spread: the full home asado (see catalog: Asado — the home
  parrillada) with choripán and achuras first (see catalog: Choripán;
  Achuras), provoleta (see catalog: Provoleta), then tira and vacío
  carved on a board; bread baskets, chimichurri and salsa criolla bowls,
  ensalada mixta and ensalada rusa. 5–8 shared vessels on a long table.
  The Sunday alternative in many families is **pasta** (see catalog:
  Pasta del domingo — ravioles con tuco). See also CROSS-CUTTING REGISTER:
  ASADO & PARRILLA for the order.
- Snapshot staging:
  - **1 setting**: one plate with a strip of asado de tira and a piece of
    vacío, a spoon of salad; the carving board cropped at the edge, a
    bread basket and a chimichurri bowl.
  - **2 settings**: two identical plates facing each other; between them
    the board of carved meat (partly cropped), a salad bowl and bread.
  - **Small group (3–4)**: identical plates along one side; boards,
    salads and bread repeating down the table and out of frame.
  - **Crowd cues**: the parrilla smoking softly behind with meat still
    on it; extra chairs; a blurred figure at the grill.
- Decor and cues: checked or plain cloth, or bare wood; mismatched
  plates; a quincho's brick walls; summer shade or winter sun. Avoid
  gaucho costume, football shirts on everyone.

### Watch parties

Football is the viewing occasion: the Selección above all, then the
Superclásico (Boca–River) and the league. The meal sits at home: 66% of
Argentines planned to watch the 2026 World Cup with family and 32% with
friends, and picada and asado purchases spike on match days [MEDIUM —
canal26, El Comodorense, La Capital]. Signature foods: the **picada**
board, **sándwiches de miga**, the patio **asado**, and **choripán** off
the carrito outside the cancha.

1. **Buenos Aires (AMBA)** — Apartments and PHs, cafés, bodegones,
   pizzerías, the Costanera. Porteño pizza and fainá, fugazzeta,

   plazas. The asado at its most classic, asado con cuero at country
   festivals, pasta and Italian-rooted cooking in Rosario and the
   gringa towns, river fish on the Paraná.
   → catalog: Asado; Matambre a la pizza; Pasta del domingo; River fish.
3. **Córdoba** — Colonial core and sierras. **Lomito cordobés**,

   → catalog: Lomito; Empanadas (cordobesa).
4. **Northwest (NOA)** — Adobe, quebradas, hornos de barro. **Empanadas
   salteñas and tucumanas**, **locro**, **humitas en chala**, **tamales**,
   llama and quinoa in the Puna. **Spinout candidate.**
   → catalog: Empanadas; Locro; Humita; Tamales.
5. **Northeast (NEA)** — Red earth, rivers, Guaraní influence. **Chipá**,

   → catalog: Chipá; River fish.
6. **Cuyo** — Vineyards, poplars, acequias, the Andes. **Empanada

   → catalog: Empanadas (mendocina).
7. **Patagonia** — Steppe, lakes, chalets, sheep estancias. **Cordero
   patagónico al asador**, trout and centolla (king crab) in the south

   for lamb and trout/centolla — argentina.gob.ar, Wikipedia ES (via

   → catalog: Cordero patagónico.

### A. Asado and the parrilla

#### Asado — the home parrillada (national; zone 2 register as default)

- **Category**: Weekend/special ritual at home; everyday menu item at
  parrilla restaurants.
- **Lineage**: Criollo/gaucho cattle culture of the pampas; shared Río
  de la Plata heritage with Uruguay (`uruguay.md` Asado) and distinct
  from Brazilian skewered churrasco.
- **Variants (§4.6), offered as choices**: (a) **home asado on a brick
  parrilla** (default); (b) **parrillada at a restaurant** on a
  table-top parrillita; (c) **asado con cuero** — a large section of
  beef cooked hide-on over a fire pit at country festivals (zone 2,
  Día de la Tradición) [LOW-MEDIUM — not re-checked]; (d) **cordero al
  asador** (zone 7, own entry). **Default when unspecified**: (a), at
  midday on a covered patio. [EDITORIAL]
- **Cuts and order**: **asado de tira** (cross-cut short-rib strips),
  **vacío** (flank, cooked fat side down first), matambre, colita de
  cuadril, entraña (skirt), bife de chorizo (sirloin strip); **chorizo**,
  **morcilla**, **chinchulines** (small intestine), **mollejas**
  (sweetbreads), riñones. [HIGH for cuts and grilling order — BAE
  Negocios, Crónica, Infoagro] **Cross-reference**: `uruguay.md` reports
  (from one blog, MEDIUM) that Argentina leans to bife de chorizo and
  Uruguay to tira; at a **home** asado in Argentina tira and vacío are
  the backbone — bife de chorizo is the **restaurant** single-diner cut.
  Not a contradiction, a venue difference. [EDITORIAL]
- **Accompaniments**: bread basket (pan francés), chimichurri, salsa
  criolla, **ensalada mixta** (lettuce, tomato, onion), **ensalada rusa**
  or potato salad, provoleta; grilled peppers or whole onions/sweet
  potatoes in the embers. [MEDIUM]
- **Serving vessel**: the asador carves on a **wooden board**; guests
  take pieces onto their **own 26–28 cm plates**; chimichurri and salsa
  criolla in small bowls along the table.
- **Utensils**: steak knife and fork on each plate; choripán and
  achuras eaten by hand standing up before the sit-down cuts.
- **Visual/plating**: see VISUAL & PLATING NORMS and TEXTURE LEXICON —
  golden-to-mahogany crust, faint parallel bars at most, rendered fat
  translucent and crisp, pink juicy centre on vacío and bife, tira
  browner. Meat piled in large pieces on the board, not portioned.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — a carving board for ~6 people
  at the table, plus one guest's plate.
  - **What dominates**: **large, whole pieces of browned beef**. On the
    board: tira strips and a sliced vacío ~70% of the surface; chorizo
    and morcilla pieces ~20%; wood showing ~10%. On a guest's plate: one
    or two pieces of meat ~60%, salad ~30%, a spoon of chimichurri on the
    meat ~5%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count (board / one plate) | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Asado de tira | Strips **4–8 cm wide**, cut into lengths of ~15–20 cm; bone cross-sections ~2–3 cm ovals, 3–4 per length [HIGH for width classes; length EDITORIAL] | 4–6 lengths / 1 | Deep brown top, crisp golden fat cap, row of pale oval bones along one edge, pink-brown meat between bones | Laid flat side by side along one half of the board |
    | Vacío | Whole piece ~30 × 20 cm, 3–5 cm thick; sliced across the grain into **1.5–2 cm** slices [LOW-MEDIUM] | 1 half-sliced piece / 1–2 slices | Crackly golden fat-and-membrane side, glossy juicy pink cut faces | The other half of the board, slices fanned off the uncut end |
    | Chorizo | ~12–15 cm × 3 cm, cut in halves or thirds [LOW-MEDIUM] | 3–4 pieces / 0–1 | Taut, blistered, browned skin, a split showing coarse pink-brown filling | Heaped at one corner |
    | Morcilla | Similar size, softer; cut in chunks | 2–3 / 0–1 | Near-black, skin split, dark crumbly-creamy interior | Beside the chorizo |
    | Chimichurri | 2–3 tbsp per bowl | 1–2 bowls / a spoonful | Loose, oily, parsley green with red chile flecks | In ~8–10 cm bowls off the board; a spoonful on the plate's meat |
    | Salsa criolla | ~0.5 cm dice | 1 bowl / a spoonful | Red, white and green dice glistening in oil and vinegar | In a bowl beside the chimichurri |
    | Ensalada mixta | Torn leaves, tomato wedges, onion rings | 1 bowl / a small heap | Bright green and red, dressed with oil | Large bowl mid-table; on the plate opposite the meat |
    | Bread | Pan francés slices ~2 cm | 1 basket / 1–2 slices | Crisp pale-golden crust, open white crumb | Basket on the table; a slice on the plate rim |

  - **Arrangement**: the board holds three groups (tira, vacío,
    embutidos), not an even scatter; the table has bowls in a line down
    the centre, plates at each seat. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Vessel fill**: the board is **crowded to within a few cm of its
    edges**; a guest's plate is about half covered. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Served portion vs. whole**: one plate = **one tira length + one or
    two vacío slices**, or a piece of chorizo; salad beside; chimichurri
    spooned onto the meat, not pooled. [EDITORIAL]
  - **State cues**: fat still glistening, a light wisp of steam off the
    vacío slices, juices pooling faintly pink on the board; no smoke on
    the table. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Absent on purpose**: BBQ sauce, skewers, corn on the cob, burgers,
    crosshatch marks, blackened meat, wine glasses, beer, fernet, the
    mate kit, a soda siphon.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A worn wooden carving board crowded with
    large pieces of grilled beef: several narrow strips of cross-cut
    short ribs, each about the can's width, golden-brown with crisp fat
    and a row of pale oval bone ends; half a flank steak sliced into
    thick pink juicy slices; a few chunks of browned sausage and dark
    blood sausage. Beside it, a small bowl of loose herb-green
    chimichurri and a bowl of diced tomato and onion. No grill-stripe
    crosshatch, no sauce, no wine."
- **Model failure**: US barbecue (sauced ribs, brisket bark, smoke
  ring); Brazilian rodízio skewers; blackened steaks with crosshatch;
  a single plated steak with a sauce swoosh.
- **Confidence**: HIGH for cuts, order, V-grate and doneness; MEDIUM for
  sides; EDITORIAL for counts and shares.
- **Sources**: [BAE Negocios — orden de la parrilla](https://www.baenegocios.com/sociedad/Asado-Cual-es-el-orden-para-colocar-carnes-chorizos-morcillas-y-achuras-en-la-parrilla-20250403-0038.html);
  [Crónica — orden del asado](https://www.cronica.com.ar/cocina/Asado-En-que-orden-colocar-carnes-chorizos-morcillas-y-achuras-en-la-parrilla-20211028-0032.html);
  [Wikipedia ES — Asado de tira](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asado_de_tira) (via search);
  [Recetas Nestlé — asado de tira](https://www.recetasnestle.com.ar/escuela-sabor/coccion/todo-sobre-asado-tira);
  [Mercado Libre blog — parrillas argentinas](https://www.mercadolibre.com.mx/blog/guia-caracteristicas-y-ventajasparrillas-argentinas);
  `uruguay.md` Asado.

#### Choripán (national; street, stadium, asado starter)

- **Category**: Everyday street food; asado first course.
- **Lineage**: Argentine invention, also eaten in Uruguay, Chile and
  Brazil (`uruguay.md` Chorizo y morcilla entry agrees).
- **Variants (§4.6)**: whole chorizo in the bread, or **mariposa**
  (chorizo split lengthwise and opened flat, "butterfly"); with
  chimichurri (default), salsa criolla, or both. **Default when
  unspecified**: mariposa with chimichurri. [EDITORIAL]
- **Bread**: **pan francés** — an individual white baguette **15–20 cm**
  long, crisp thin crust and soft open crumb, split lengthwise and often
  toasted on the grill; not a soft hot-dog bun. [MEDIUM —
  choripan.com.ar; `uruguay.md` uses the same bread source]
- **Vessel**: a paper napkin on a cart's steel counter, or a plate at an
  asado.
- **Texture & finish**: blistered, browned, faintly glossy chorizo skin;
  in mariposa, the cut face flat-grilled with a golden-brown crust and a
  coarse pink-brown grain; chimichurri soaking into the crumb in green
  streaks.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one sandwich.
  - **What dominates**: **bread and sausage roughly equal** in the side
    view; from above, the opened chorizo covers ~70% of the bread's cut
    face; chimichurri a loose streak along the top (~10%). [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Pan francés | 15–20 cm × 6–7 cm, split | 1 | Pale-golden crisp crust, a few grill marks on the cut face | The base, hinged open |
    | Chorizo (mariposa) | ~12–15 cm long, opened to ~5–6 cm wide, ~1.5 cm thick | 1 | Cut face browned and slightly crusty, edges curling, skin blistered | Laid flat inside, ends reaching near the bread's ends |
    | Chimichurri | ~1–2 tbsp | — | Oily green-red herb flecks | Spooned along the chorizo, dripping onto the crumb |
    | Salsa criolla (optional) | ~0.5 cm dice | ~1 tbsp | Red-white-green dice | On top of the chimichurri |

  - **Arrangement**: the sandwich rests closed or half-open on a paper
    napkin, the chorizo ends visible at both ends. [EDITORIAL]
  - **State cues**: steam, a sheen of fat on the chorizo, bread toasted.
  - **Absent on purpose**: ketchup, mustard stripes, soft hot-dog bun,
    cheese, lettuce.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A crusty individual white baguette about one
    and a half times the can's height, split lengthwise and toasted,
    holding a grilled pork sausage cut open flat along its length, the
    browned cut face slightly crusty, a loose streak of oily green herb
    sauce with red chile flecks soaking into the bread. On a paper napkin
    on a steel counter. Not a hot dog, no ketchup."
- **Model failure**: a US hot dog; a Spanish cured red chorizo sliced;
  a sub roll loaded with toppings; confusion with Uruguay's chivito
  (a steak sandwich — see `uruguay.md`).
- **Confidence**: HIGH for form (Wikipedia *Choripán*, choripan.com.ar,
  Laylita); EDITORIAL for shares.
- **Sources**: [Wikipedia — Choripán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chorip%C3%A1n) (via search);
  [choripan.com.ar — el mejor pan para choripán](https://choripan.com.ar/guias/el-mejor-pan-para-choripan/).

#### Bife de chorizo (parrilla restaurant, single diner)

- **Category**: Everyday restaurant plate; home asado cut.
- **Lineage**: Argentine name for the sirloin strip (striploin); **not
  a sausage** despite "chorizo" in the name — a frequent outsider
  confusion. [HIGH — uncontested; 69 Gauchos history page (via search)]
- **Form**: a thick, boneless steak with a fat strip along one edge,
  **2–5 cm thick**, ~350–450 g; a Buenos Aires neighbourhood-parrilla
  portion documented at ~2 cm and ~400 g. Served **jugoso** or a punto.
  [MEDIUM-HIGH — hacerasado.com.ar, El Cronista/Capitán Parrilla (via
  search)]
- **Sides**: papas fritas, puré, ensalada mixta, or a fried egg on top
  ("a caballo") at a bodegón; chimichurri on the table. [MEDIUM]
- **Vessel**: a 26–28 cm plain white plate or a wooden board; serrated
  steak knife.
- **Texture & finish**: browned crust with faint bar marks, the fat
  strip crisp-golden; **the cut face shows three bands — brown crust,
  thin grey band, wide red-pink juicy centre**. [MEDIUM]
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one plate.
  - **What dominates**: **the steak, ~50–60% of the plate**; fries or
    salad ~30%; plate rim visible ~10–20%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Bife de chorizo | ~16–18 × 10–12 cm footprint (LOW), **2–4 cm thick — about half the can's width** | 1 | Mahogany crust, fat strip golden and crisp along one long edge | Centre-left, the fat edge toward the rim |
    | Cut section (optional) | One cut 2–3 cm in from an end | 1 | Three-band cross-section, a thin pool of pink juice | Turned toward camera |
    | Papas fritas | Thick-cut sticks ~1 × 1 × 7–9 cm | ~15–20 | Golden, matte, a few darker tips | Loose heap on the right third |
    | Chimichurri | ~1 tbsp | — | Herb-green oily spoonful | On the steak or in a bowl beside |

  - **Served portion**: one steak per diner, no sharing; salad in its
    own bowl if ordered.
  - **State cues**: juice beading on the cut face, fries hot and dry, no
    sauce.
  - **Absent on purpose**: peppercorn or béarnaise sauce, garnish sprigs,
    crosshatch, blue-rare purple centre, a bone (that is an ojo de bife
    or T-bone).
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "On a plain white plate, one thick boneless
    grilled steak about as thick as half the can is wide, deep brown
    crust with a crisp golden strip of fat along one edge, cut once near
    the end to show a thin grey band around a wide juicy pink-red centre;
    a loose heap of thick golden fries beside it and a spoonful of green
    herb sauce. No other sauce, no garnish."
- **Model failure**: a sausage; a thin minute steak; a T-bone.
- **Confidence**: MEDIUM-HIGH for form and thickness.
- **Sources**: [hacerasado.com.ar — bife de chorizo](https://hacerasado.com.ar/bife-de-chorizo/);
  [El Cronista — bife de chorizo completo más grande](https://www.cronista.com/clase/gourmet/el-bife-de-chorizo-completo-mas-grande-de-buenos-aires-cuanto-cuesta-comer-en-el-mejor-bodegon-de-la-ciudad/).

#### Provoleta (asado starter; national)

- **Category**: Asado and parrilla starter.
- **Lineage**: Argentine; a spun-curd cheese in the provolone family,
  registered as "queso provolone hilado argentino" in the Código
  Alimentario in 1955. [MEDIUM — Wikipedia *Provoleta*, El Esquiu (via
  search)]
- **Form**: a disc cut **1.5–3 cm thick** from a long cylinder, grilled
  directly on the grate or in a small cast-iron/aluminium dish until the
  outside crusts and the inside melts; finished with **dried oregano,
  chile flakes (ají molido) and olive oil**. [MEDIUM-HIGH — La Nación
  Foodit, Christian Petersen/Canal Cocina (via search)]
- **Vessel**: a small round cast-iron skillet or provoletera ~14–16 cm
  [LOW], or straight onto a plate; bread alongside.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one provoleta.
  - **What dominates**: **the golden crusted cheese disc fills ~80% of
    the small pan**; oregano and chile a light scatter over ~60% of the
    top; oil a thin sheen. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Provoleta disc | ~10–12 cm across (LOW), 1.5–3 cm thick — **wider than the can, thinner than the can is wide** | 1 | Top and edges golden-brown with a crisp, slightly blistered crust; sides slumping; a molten ivory pull where cut | Centred in the pan |
    | Oregano | Dried flakes | a pinch | Dull green specks | Scattered on top |
    | Ají molido | Flakes ~2–3 mm | a pinch | Red flecks | Scattered on top |
    | Olive oil | — | a drizzle | Thin golden sheen pooling at the edge | Top and pan base |

  - **State cues**: bubbling at the edge, a wedge lifted to show a long
    stretchy string of cheese — **resting on the pan, not on a fork in
    hand**. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Absent on purpose**: tomato, basil, honey, breadcrumbs, a
    Mexican queso fundido look (loose pool of cheese with chorizo).
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A small round cast-iron pan holding a thick
    disc of grilled white cheese a little wider than the can, the top
    crusted golden-brown and blistered, edges slumping and bubbling,
    sprinkled with dried oregano and red chile flakes and a thin sheen
    of olive oil; one wedge pulled slightly away to show molten ivory
    strands."
- **Model failure**: queso fundido; halloumi slices; a pizza.
- **Confidence**: MEDIUM-HIGH.
- **Sources**: [La Nación Foodit — provoleta](https://foodit.lanacion.com.ar/trucos/hasta-los-vegetarianos-aplauden-asi-se-hace-la-mejor-provoleta-del-mundo-a-la-parrilla-nid29112024/);
  [Canal Cocina — Christian Petersen provoleta](https://canalcocina.es/receta/provoleta-de-queso-provolone-a-la-parrilla).

#### Achuras — chinchulines, mollejas, morcilla (asado and parrillada)

- **Category**: Asado first course; restaurant parrillada.
- **Lineage**: Criollo; shared with Uruguay (`uruguay.md` notes offal
  such as chotos as more Uruguayan than Brazilian).
- **Form**: **chinchulines** — beef small intestine grilled in long
  coils until crisp and golden, cut into short lengths; **mollejas** —
  sweetbreads grilled whole until golden and crisp outside, creamy
  inside, served with lemon; **morcilla** — soft blood sausage, often
  the sweet morcilla dulce in the Río de la Plata (`uruguay.md`).
  [HIGH for their place in the asado order — BAE Negocios, Crónica;
  look MEDIUM — not individually re-checked]
- **Vessel**: the parrillita de mesa, or the carving board.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — a parrillita for two.
  - **What dominates**: **mixed pieces covering the whole small grate**:
    chinchulines ~25%, molleja ~15%, chorizo/morcilla ~25%, a tira and a
    vacío slice ~35%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Chinchulines | Cut in lengths ~6–10 cm, tube ~2 cm thick | 4–6 | Deep golden, crisp, corrugated/braided-looking surface, slightly glossy | Heaped at one end of the grate |
    | Molleja | Lumps ~5–8 cm | 1–2 | Golden-crusted, irregular, knobbly; creamy pale inside if cut | Beside the chinchulines, a lemon wedge nearby |
    | Morcilla | ~10 cm length | 1 | Near-black, split, soft | Middle |
    | Chorizo | ~12–15 cm | 1 | Browned, blistered | Middle |
    | Tira / vacío | as Asado | 1 each | as Asado | Far end |

  - **State cues**: faint glow of embers under the grate, fat sizzling
    sheen; no flame.
  - **Absent on purpose**: skewers, sauces, raw-looking offal, a
    wine bottle.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A small iron table grill on legs over a tray
    of glowing embers, covered with mixed grilled meats: short lengths
    of crisp golden coiled beef intestine, a knobbly golden sweetbread
    with a lemon wedge, a split dark blood sausage and a browned pork
    sausage, a strip of cross-cut short rib; each piece shorter than the
    can is tall."
- **Model failure**: sausages only; Korean BBQ; unappetising raw offal.
- **Confidence**: MEDIUM.
- **Sources**: as Asado.

#### Matambre a la pizza (parrilla and home)

- **Category**: Everyday asado and parrilla dish.
- **Lineage**: Argentine: the thin matambre (flank/rose meat) cut,
  boiled or grilled tender, then topped like a pizza.
- **Form**: a whole matambre (**1.8–2.5 kg**, a thin broad sheet),
  often first simmered in milk and water with bay and garlic, then
  browned on the grill, flipped and covered with tomato sauce and
  mozzarella (and oregano, sometimes ham, egg or olives) until the
  cheese melts. [MEDIUM-HIGH — Infobae, La Nación Foodit, Cocineros
  Argentinos, Paulina Cocina (via search)]
- **Vessel**: the grate, then a wooden board; cut into squares.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — a whole matambre on a board.
  - **What dominates**: **melted mozzarella ~60% of the visible top**,
    tomato sauce ~25% showing through and at the edges, browned meat
    edge ~15%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Matambre | Sheet ~40 × 30 cm (LOW), ~1.5–2 cm thick — **thinner than a third of the can's width** | 1 | Browned underside, meat edge showing as a thin brown band | Base |
    | Tomato sauce | Thin layer | — | Bright brick red | Spread over the whole top, visible at the rim |
    | Mozzarella | Melted layer ~0.5–1 cm | — | Ivory, glossy, browned in spots | Over most of the top, stopping 1–2 cm short of the edge |
    | Oregano, olives (optional) | Olives ~2 cm | 4–8 olives | Green, glossy | Scattered |
    | Squares cut | ~8–10 cm | 12–16 | Clean cuts showing a thin meat base | Across the board |

  - **Served portion**: 1–2 squares on a plate.
  - **State cues**: cheese bubbling, a little fat pooling, steam.
  - **Absent on purpose**: pizza dough, pepperoni, basil.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A thin, broad sheet of grilled beef on a
    wooden board, topped like a pizza with brick-red tomato sauce and
    glossy melted white mozzarella browned in spots, a few green olives
    and dried oregano; cut into squares each a little wider than the
    can is tall, the cut edges showing a thin brown meat base under the
    cheese. No bread dough."
- **Model failure**: an actual pizza; a rolled matambre (that is the
  Christmas arrollado — see Vitel toné & Christmas).
- **Confidence**: MEDIUM-HIGH for form; sheet size LOW.
- **Sources**: [Infobae — matambre a la pizza](https://www.infobae.com/tendencias/2024/12/17/receta-de-matambre-a-la-pizza-a-la-parrilla-rapida-y-facil/);
  [La Nación Foodit — matambre a la pizza](https://foodit.lanacion.com.ar/trucos/matambre-a-la-pizza-como-tiernizar-y-preparar-el-hit-de-la-parrilla-argentina-nid29112024/).

### B. National core — home, bodegón, pizzería, rotisería

#### Milanesa — plated, and a la napolitana (national; zone 1 bodegón register)

- **Category**: Everyday — the most common home cena and a bodegón
  flagship.
- **Lineage**: Italian-immigrant cotoletta adapted in the Río de la
  Plata. **Milanesa a la napolitana is a Buenos Aires creation, not
  Neapolitan**: traced to the Nápoli restaurant near Luna Park in the
  late 1940s ("milanesa a la Nápoli"), with a competing family claim for
  José Nápoli's bar. [HIGH that it is a Buenos Aires dish — Wikipedia ES,
  Buenos Aires city tourism, serargentino (via search); origin story
  MEDIUM] **Cross-reference**: `uruguay.md` treats it as shared Río de la
  Plata heritage with no provable national visual difference — this file
  agrees and does not try to split it.
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **beef** (nalga/cuadrada) or **chicken** breaded
  cutlet; **plain with lemon**, **napolitana** (tomato sauce, ham,
  mozzarella, oven-gratinated), **a caballo** (fried eggs on top); with
  **papas fritas**, **puré** or salad. **Default when unspecified**:
  beef milanesa napolitana with papas fritas at a bodegón; at home, a
  plain beef milanesa with puré. [EDITORIAL]
- **Vessel**: bodegón — an **oval tin or stainless platter** the
  milanesa overhangs ("barely fits on the table"); home — a 26–28 cm
  plate. [HIGH for the bodegón platter and giant portions — canal26,
  Pipol, Cuisine & Vins (via search)]
- **Texture & finish**: very thin meat (well under 1 cm) under a craggy,
  even golden-brown crumb; napolitana top: a thin red sauce layer, pink
  ham, mozzarella melted and **browned/bubbled in patches**, crumb edge
  visible all round (shared description in `uruguay.md`).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — bodegón napolitana with fries,
  one platter for 1–2 people.
  - **What dominates**: **the milanesa, ~65–75% of the platter**, so
    large it reaches or overhangs the rim at one or both ends; fries
    ~25% in a heap at one end or alongside. On the milanesa, cheese
    covers ~80% of the top, leaving a **1–2 cm golden crumb border**.
    [EDITORIAL; "overhangs the platter" HIGH per bodegón sources]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Breaded cutlet | ~30–40 cm long × 18–25 cm at a bodegón (LOW); home ~20–25 cm; **~0.5–0.8 cm thick including crumb — thinner than the can's rim is wide** | 1 | Irregular oval, craggy golden crumb, darker at the edges | Fills the platter, one end over the rim |
    | Tomato sauce | Thin layer ~2–3 mm | — | Brick red, a little glossy | Spread across the top under the ham |
    | Ham (jamón cocido) | Thin slices | 3–4 | Pale pink, edges slightly curled | Over the sauce, partly showing |
    | Mozzarella | Melted layer ~5 mm | — | Ivory with golden-brown blistered patches | Over the ham, stopping short of the crumb edge |
    | Oregano / tomato slices (optional) | Slices ~6–7 cm | 0–4 | Red rounds, oregano specks | On the cheese |
    | Papas fritas | Sticks ~1 × 1 × 7–9 cm | a large heap (~40–60) | Golden, matte | At one end or alongside |
    | Lemon (plain milanesa only) | Wedge | 1–2 | Pale yellow | Plate rim |

#### Pizza porteña — muzzarella al molde, with fainá (zone 1 authoritative)

- **Category**: Everyday — pizzería counter, Friday night at home,
  delivery.
- **Lineage**: Italian-immigrant (Genoese and Neapolitan) pizza turned
  into a distinct Buenos Aires style. **Cross-reference**: `uruguay.md`
  documents Uruguay's **pizza a la piedra** (thin, stone-baked) with
  fainá "a caballo" as shared heritage; the porteño default is the
  **thick pan style**, with a la piedra as a co-equal option.
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **al molde** (thick pan, spongy), **media masa**
  (medium), **a la piedra** (thin, stone); toppings: **muzzarella**
  (default), napolitana (tomato slices, garlic), fugazzeta (own entry),
  jamón y morrones, calabresa. **Default when unspecified**: muzzarella
  al molde. [HIGH for the three bases — Wikipedia ES *Pizza en
  Argentina*, canal26, Designbeep (via search)]
- **Defining trait**: **"abundant mozzarella to the edges, so it browns
  and gratinates"** over a thin smear of sauce; a few green olives; the
  cheese is the protagonist. [HIGH]

  slice laid **on top of** a pizza slice ("a caballo"). [HIGH]
- **Vessel**: a round **aluminium pan/tray** ~30–35 cm; slices on small
  plates or a sheet of paper at a standing counter. [LOW-MEDIUM for size]
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one whole pie and one served
  slice with fainá.
  - **What dominates**: **cheese, ~85–90% of the top**; a narrow
    raised crust rim ~1–1.5 cm; sauce visible only as orange-red seep
    at the cheese's edge; 4–8 green olives. Side view: crust ~2–3 cm
    tall, cheese layer ~0.7–1 cm. [EDITORIAL; "cheese protagonist" HIGH]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count (pie / slice) | Look | Where it sits |

  - **Served portion**: one wedge (one-eighth), crust point toward camera;

  enriched with Spanish-era meats. [HIGH — La Nación, Tucumán Turismo,
  Excelencias Gourmet (via search)]
- **Form**: **white hominy corn (maíz blanco pisado), white beans,
  squash (zapallo) cooked until it breaks down and thickens the pot**,
  beef, pork (skin, trotters, belly), **chorizo colorado**; paprika and
  cumin; about 3 hours in iron or clay pots. **Topped at the table with
  a spoon of red fat sauce** (paprika-chile fat with spring onion,
  "quiquirimichi" in some regions). [HIGH for the ingredients; the
  topping sauce MEDIUM — not independently re-checked this pass]
- **Vessel**: a glazed clay bowl (cazuela/cuenco) ~14–18 cm, or a deep
  plate; a big pot in the background at a peña.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one bowl.
  - **What dominates**: **a thick, pale-orange porridge-like stew** of
    corn and squash, ~70% of the surface; meat pieces ~20%; the red fat
    topping and green onion ~10%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Spring onion | Rings ~0.5 cm | a pinch | Bright green | On the red sauce |

  - **State cues**: steaming, thick enough that a spoon stands briefly.
  - **Absent on purpose**: clear broth, tomato-red stew, sour cream,
    avocado, Mexican pozole garnish (radish, lettuce, tostadas).
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A glazed brown clay bowl filled to near the
