# Brazil

## FILE ROLE & METHOD

This file is Brazil's country file: a single national staging brief with
seven labeled internal zones. One TCCC hero beverage per scene (named by
the brief — see HERO PRODUCT SLOT), staged against real Brazilian dishes,
vessels and settings, with the wider drinks landscape documented as
context even where a drink (alcohol) is never staged.

**Scope** (the same default as the Mexico, Spain, Germany and South Africa
files, not a new decision): **almoço** (lunch, the main meal), **jantar /
janta** (evening meal) and **lanches** (snacks, street food, the afternoon
café da tarde, party food) are in scope. **Café da manhã** (breakfast) is
out of scope except through the opt-in Morning Module; the brief asked for
breakfast entries in the catalog, so they are written in full but flagged
as morning-only. No beverage other than the hero TCCC product is staged.

### Structural decision: one file, seven zones (recommendation — reviewer has final say)

The `country-file-schema.md` §1.1 swap test, applied with the evidence
gathered this pass:

- **A strong national core travels almost everywhere.** Rice and beans
  eaten daily, the **prato feito** (PF), the **comida por quilo**
  self-service restaurant, feijoada on Wednesdays and Saturdays, the home
  **churrasco**, the lanchonete counter of **coxinhas** and salgados,
  **pão de queijo**, **pastel**, **brigadeiro** at every birthday.
  [CONFIDENCE: HIGH that arroz-com-feijão is the national daily base —
  IBGE's POF 2017–2018 release is headlined "brasileiro ainda mantém dieta
  à base de arroz e feijão" (via search); HIGH for por quilo being
  nationwide — pt.wikipedia "Comida por quilo" and Abrasel (via search)]
- **Several things are genuinely form-changing by region** (§4.2) and look
  wrong transplanted:
  - **The bean itself.** Brown-beige **carioca** beans dominate in São
    Paulo and Minas; **black beans** dominate in Rio de Janeiro and the
    three southern states. Nationally ~56% of common-bean consumption is
    carioca vs ~21% black. [HIGH — Embrapa consumption page and Instituto
    Gourmet (via search) agree] A black-bean PF in a São Paulo scene, or
    a carioca-bean feijoada in Rio, is a visible error.
  - **Açaí.** In Pará it is an unsweetened, thick purple pulp eaten at
    lunch with fried fish and manioc flour; in the Southeast it is a
    frozen sweet bowl with banana and granola. Locals in Pará treat the
    sweet version as a different thing. [HIGH — Band, Pará+, O Liberal
    (via search)]
  - **Moqueca.** Baiana (dendê palm oil and coconut milk, deep orange)
    vs capixaba (no dendê, no coconut; annatto and tomato; black clay pot
    from Goiabeiras, Vitória). [HIGH — Prática, Estado de Minas, Unilever
    Food Solutions (via search)]
  - **The Bahian dendê kitchen** (acarajé, vatapá, caruru) and the
    **Amazonian manioc kitchen** (tucupi, jambu, tacacá) do not exist as
    everyday food elsewhere.
- **Environment does not travel**: São Paulo's dense grey high-rise
  sprawl, Rio's hills-and-beach city, Minas's colonial hill towns and
  wood stoves, the South's European-immigrant towns and cold winters,
  Salvador's colonial Pelourinho and Atlantic heat, the dry Northeastern
  sertão, and Belém's river-port humidity look nothing alike.

**Recommendation: one national file, seven zones, handled as dish-variant
and environment deltas** — the same call made for Mexico (seven zones) and
Spain (six). The form-changing dishes are real but concentrated in a
manageable set of entries.

**Flagged for the reviewer (not decided here):**
1. **Brazil is the one LATAM market where a US-style national-index +
   regional-files split is a serious option** — it is continental in
   size and the swap test fails in more places than it did for Mexico.
   This pass recommends one file only because the core (PF, por quilo,
   churrasco, feijoada, salgados) is so strongly shared.
2. **Spinout candidates**: **zone 5 (Bahia / Recôncavo)** — a distinct
   Afro-Brazilian dendê cuisine with religious links that need careful
   framing; and **zone 7 (North / Amazon)** — a distinct manioc-and-river
   cuisine, its own climate and architecture. If briefs set there grow,
   split to `brazil-bahia.md` / `brazil-amazon.md` per
   `country-file-schema.md` §2.2. **A recommendation for Fernando, not a
   decision** (§7).

### Zones (one scheme, used throughout)

| # | Zone | Covers | Visual register |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | São Paulo (Sampa + interior paulista) | São Paulo city and metro, the state interior, Santos | Dense grey-and-beige high-rises to the horizon, padarias on every corner, feiras livres on street mornings, Italian and Japanese-rooted food, cooler, often overcast light |
| 2 | Rio de Janeiro & Espírito Santo | Rio city and state; Espírito Santo | Granite hills and forest behind the city, beach promenades, botecos with tiled walls, black-and-white wave-pattern pavements, humid bright light; Espírito Santo's clay-pot moqueca |
| 3 | Minas Gerais & the Centre-West | Minas Gerais; Goiás, Distrito Federal, Mato Grosso (do Sul) as a thin extension | Hill country, colonial towns with whitewashed walls and blue or ochre window frames, wood-burning stoves (fogão a lenha), cheese and pork; open cerrado and cattle country further west |
| 4 | South (gaúcho and immigrant South) | Rio Grande do Sul, Santa Catarina, Paraná | Pampas and ranches, German and Italian immigrant towns (half-timber, stone, vineyards in the Serra Gaúcha), real winters, chimarrão gourds, churrasco as ritual |
| 5 | Bahia (Salvador and the Recôncavo) | Bahia's coast and Recôncavo | Colonial Pelourinho façades in saturated colours, Atlantic beaches, baianas' white dress and tabuleiros, dendê orange everywhere on the plate |
| 6 | Northeast (beyond Bahia) | Pernambuco, Ceará, Paraíba, Rio Grande do Norte, Alagoas, Sergipe, Maranhão, Piauí; coast and sertão | Bright coastal cities and reef beaches; dry, cactus-and-scrub sertão inland; carne de sol, queijo coalho, cuscuz, tapioca; São João festivals |
| 7 | North / Amazon | Pará, Amazonas and the other northern states | River ports, wooden boats, open markets on the water (Ver-o-Peso, Belém), heavy humid heat and afternoon rain, manioc, river fish, açaí, tucupi |

Zone boundaries are a staging convenience, not a claim about cultural
identity. Espírito Santo is grouped with Rio for staging and could be
argued as its own region; the Centre-West is attached to Minas only
because it was not researched this pass [EDITORIAL].

### Default when no zone is named

Fall back to **zone 1 (São Paulo) at the everyday register**: a São Paulo
home kitchen or a neighbourhood lanchonete, padaria or por quilo
restaurant at lunchtime, with **carioca** beans. São Paulo is Brazil's
largest metro and the place most national formats (PF, por quilo,
pastel de feira) are most densely present. [EDITORIAL fallback — not a
sourced "most typical Brazil" claim]

## METHOD NOTE (read first)

**Build method, this pass.** Drafted directly from model knowledge and
checked with about 40 WebSearch queries in one session, prioritising what
a scene visibly depends on: pack formats, housing type, meal timing, bean
geography, and the signature dishes' ingredients and sizes. The session's
search budget ran out before the last few dishes could be checked. Tags:

- **[HIGH] / [MEDIUM] / [LOW]** — schema §6 tags earned this pass (HIGH =
  2+ independent corroborating sources; MEDIUM = 1 credible source).
- **[MEDIUM — not independently re-checked this pass]** / **[LOW-MEDIUM —
  not independently re-checked]** — model knowledge that is uncontested
  general culinary or cultural knowledge but was not searched. Plausible,
  not verified.
- **[EDITORIAL]** — a judgment call (defaults, shares, counts, caricature
  guidance, zone boundaries), never a factual claim.
- **[HIGH — first-party test]** — this project's own image-generation
  findings (`coca-cola-guidelines.md` §1, `country-file-schema.md` §7.5).
  **No Brazil-specific image tests have been run yet.**

**Writing principle.** Describe what the camera sees: surface, sheen,
crumb, char, cut face, how the beans sit against the rice, and real-world
size relative to the can. Portuguese dish names are given so a brief
writer can find the right entry, but **the prompt-ready lines use plain
sensory English** — image models may not know "farofa" or "couve" and a
culturally specific term is not guaranteed to render (§7.5).

**File-wide rules for every scene built from this file:**

1. **No legible text anywhere.** Padaria and lanchonete menu boards, price
   cards on por quilo counters, the digital scale's display, feira stall
   banners, packaging (farofa packets, biscoito bags), shop fronts,
   football shirts and posters may appear only as blurred, unreadable
   colour. Never quote example words or prices. Any readable word, number
   or brand mark means reject or retouch. [HIGH — first-party test;
   `country-file-schema.md` §7.5] **Brazil-specific risk: Portuguese vs
   Spanish.** Image models often put Spanish words on "Latin American"
   signage; a readable Spanish word in a Brazilian scene is a double
   error. Excluding all text avoids it.
2. **The hero product is the TCCC beverage the brief names** — never a
   regional default. Branding is composited in post, never trusted from
   the generation (`coca-cola-guidelines.md` §1–2).
3. **No alcohol in any scene, ever, and never a TCCC product as a mixer.**
   Brazil has a named cachaça-and-Coca-Cola drink (the Samba em Berlim)
   and beer is the strongest prior for a churrasco, boteco or beach scene
   — see ICONIC BEVERAGES; exclude explicitly.
4. **No drinks in frame other than the hero product**, unless the brief
   names a non-alcoholic companion. Brazilian food scenes carry strong
   priors toward beer bottles in insulated sleeves, caipirinhas, fresh
   juice (suco), coconut water in the husk, caldo de cana at the feira,
   coffee in a small glass, chimarrão gourds and mate galões on the beach.
   Name and negate the ones the scene invites.
5. **Nothing held in a hand.** Food and product rest on a surface, even
   for hand-eaten pastéis, coxinhas, acarajé and churrasco on a skewer
   (`country-file-schema.md` §7.5).
6. **Breakfast only through the Morning Module.**
7. **No hard national dietary rule shapes the Brazilian table.** Pork,
   beef and chicken are all everyday; no religious majority forbids a
   meat. Two soft norms are real: **fish on Fridays and in Lent/Holy
   Week** in Catholic households and restaurants (see the São Paulo
   weekly plate calendar), and vegetarian or Adventist households as a
   minority. **Hard staging rule instead: religious food.** Acarajé,
   caruru and several Bahian dishes are also offerings in Candomblé; never
   stage a hero product with an offering, at a terreiro, in a ritual, or
   with a baiana styled as a religious figure (see the acarajé entry).
   [EDITORIAL rule; the religious link is MEDIUM — not independently
   re-checked this pass]

| Generic scene type | What to draw on |
|---|---|
| **Lanchonete / padaria counter** | A long steel-and-granite counter with round stools fixed to the floor; a heated glass display case (estufa) of golden salgados — coxinhas, kibes, esfihas, pão de queijo; a paper-napkin dispenser; tiled wall; small plates. The default everyday snack register. |
| **Restaurante por quilo** | A long hot buffet under sneeze-guards with stainless trays of rice, two bean pots, salads, pasta, stews and grilled meats; large white plates; a scale at the end of the line (display blurred); simple tables with placemats, office workers at noon. |

| **Feira livre (street market)** | A closed street on a weekday morning, stalls under blue, yellow or striped tarps, piles of fruit, the pastel stand with its frying vats at the end of the row. |
| **Churrasco em casa** | A covered back terrace (área de churrasqueira) with a built-in brick grill and chimney, skewers over charcoal, a wooden cutting board, a long table with a plastic or cotton cloth; Sunday afternoon. |
| **Churrascaria rodízio** | A large hall, servers carrying long skewers of meat to the table and carving onto plates, a big salad and side buffet, a two-sided card by each plate. |
| **Almoço de família at home** | A table with a patterned plastic or cotton cloth, rice and beans in their pots or serving bowls, a salad bowl, farofa, a meat platter; a 2 L bottle in the midground. |

## TRUSTED CONTENT

### HERO PRODUCT SLOT

Every scene carries one TCCC hero product, **named by the brief**. This
file uses `south-africa.md`'s generalised slot; since 2026-09-27 that is
the project-wide rule (the brief dictates the SKU, never the region — see
`DECISIONS.md` and `country-file-schema.md` §5.4). **If a brief names no
product, ask for one.** Nothing below is a default.

**Template:**
> [HERO PRODUCT]: {brand and variant exactly as named on pack}, in
> {format and size}, {dominant pack colour and material cue},
> {negated lookalikes}. {Position: standing upright on the surface,
> label facing camera or turned slightly}. Pack text will be composited
> in post.

**Rules:**
- **Name the variant; negate the closest lookalike** (Original vs. Sem
  Açúcar; Fanta Guaraná vs. Kuat vs. a competitor guaraná). An unspecified
  "Coca-Cola can" rendered as the wrong variant in 2 of 3 generations
  [HIGH — first-party test, `coca-cola-guidelines.md` §1].
- **Brazil is a 350 mL-can market — neither 330 mL nor 355 mL.**
  Brazilian retail lists Coca-Cola Original as a **350 mL lata** at every
  major retailer (Carrefour, Extra, Amazon.com.br, Mercado Livre, Tenda,
  Coca-Cola Andina's own Rio store). One listing gives the can as
  **6.6 × 6.62 × 12.22 cm**. [HIGH for 350 mL as the standard can size —
  six-plus independent retail listings; MEDIUM for 12.22 cm × 6.6 cm —
  one retail listing, consistent with a 12 oz-class can body]
  **This contradicts `coca-cola-guidelines.md` §4.3's rule "default to the
  330 mL can for any non-US market"** — logged in the GAP LOG; not edited
  here. For scale, **treat the Brazilian 350 mL can as 12.2 cm tall and
  6.6 cm wide** — practically the same silhouette as the US 355 mL can,
  and ~7 mm taller than the European 330 mL can.
- **Formats confirmed current in Brazil this pass** (reference for whoever
  writes the brief — never a default):
  - **Cans**: 350 mL (standard); **310 mL sleek** (taller, slimmer);
    **220 mL sleek mini** (replaced the old 250 mL mini can in 2017,
    also sold for Sem Açúcar, Kuat, Fanta Uva, Fanta Laranja and Sprite).
    [HIGH for 350 and 310 mL — multiple retailer listings (Pão de Açúcar,
    Carrefour, Bretas); HIGH for 220 mL — Exame (2017) and current
    Atacadão/Nova Era listings]
  - **Returnable glass** (vidro retornável): **200 mL, 290 mL, 1 L and
    1.25 L**. [MEDIUM-HIGH — Coca-Cola Brasil's own "Retornáveis" page
    (via search) lists the sizes; 1 L glass confirmed by separate retail
    listings] Dimensions not found — the budget ran out before the 290 mL
    height could be searched.
  - **Returnable PET — the "garrafa universal" (RefPET)**: **1.5 L and
    2 L**, a standardised clear PET bottle shared across Coca-Cola,
    Coca-Cola Sem Açúcar, Fanta Uva, Fanta Laranja, Fanta Guaraná, Sprite
    and Guaraná Jesus, with a **removable paper label**, reused up to 25
    times. [HIGH — ABIR, Propmark, Projeto Colabora, Ellen MacArthur
    Foundation, Recicla Sampa (via search)] Returnables (RefPET + glass)
    are reported as roughly **20% of Coca-Cola Brasil's portfolio** [MEDIUM
    — one trade article]. Whether the universal bottle keeps a contour
    silhouette was **not confirmed** [LOW] — do not describe its shape
    until it is.
  - **One-way PET**: 600 mL, 1 L, 1.5 L, 2 L and larger are sold [MEDIUM —
    not independently re-checked this pass; uncontested but no listing was
    read].
- **Which formats fit which setting** (reference only, not a default):
  - lanchonete, padaria, feira, street: a 350 mL can, a 290 mL glass
    bottle, a 600 mL PET or a 220 mL mini can
  - por quilo, PF, office lunch: a 350 mL can or 600 mL PET
  - family almoço, Sunday churrasco, birthday table, feijoada: a **2 L**
    (universal returnable or one-way PET) or a 1 L returnable glass bottle
    in the midground, one filled glass per place
  - restaurant, churrascaria: a can or glass bottle, optionally poured
    over ice into plain unbranded glassware
- **Use the chosen format's real dimensions as the scale anchor** (see
  SCALE REFERENCE).
- **One hero product per scene** unless the brief asks for several.

### ICONIC BEVERAGES (documented context — staging rules follow)

This section documents the real Brazilian drinks landscape, including
alcohol, and restricts only what is staged.

**TCCC Brazil portfolio — partly verified this pass.** Coca-Cola Brasil
describes a portfolio of 140+ products; brands confirmed as TCCC in
Brazil include **Coca-Cola, Fanta (Laranja, Uva, Guaraná), Kuat, Sprite,
Schweppes, Del Valle, Powerade, Crystal (water), Guaraná Jesus, Matte
Leão / Leão (teas)**, and regional guaraná brands (Taí, Simba, Charrua,
Tuchaua, Guarapan). [HIGH for the named brands — Coca-Cola Brasil's brand
page and Meio & Mensagem (via search)]

- **The competitor trap**: **Guaraná Antarctica** (Ambev) is the dominant
  guaraná soda and a very strong image prior (green can/bottle). A generic
  "guaraná" in a prompt will drift to it. Always name Fanta Guaraná or
  Kuat, and negate Antarctica. [EDITORIAL; Antarctica/Ambev ownership
  MEDIUM — not independently re-checked]

**Non-alcoholic context (not staged unless the brief names a companion):**
- **Caldo de cana** (fresh-pressed sugarcane juice, pale green-gold, in a
  tall plastic cup) — the pastel's partner at the feira. [HIGH — Mercure
  São Paulo guide, putzkifome (via search)]
- **Mate de galão** on Rio's beaches — iced mate from the vendor's two
  metal tanks, often mixed with lemonade, with **Biscoito Globo**; the
  pairing was declared cultural heritage of Rio in 2012. [HIGH — Cariocaria,
  Clube Candeias, Viajar em Família (via search)]
- **Chimarrão** (hot yerba mate in a gourd with a metal straw) — zone 4,
  all day, all year. [HIGH — multiple gaúcho cuisine sources]
- **Cafezinho** (small strong coffee), **pingado / média** (coffee with
  milk in a small glass at the padaria), **sucos** (fresh fruit juices),
  **água de coco** in the green husk. [MEDIUM — not independently
  re-checked this pass]

**Alcohol context (never staged):**
- Beer (cerveja, chope), cachaça, caipirinha, quentão (hot spiced
  cachaça drink at festas juninas), vinho quente.
- **Coca-Cola as a mixer is a named Brazilian drink: the "Samba em
  Berlim"** — cachaça and Coca-Cola over ice with lime, the Brazilian
  Cuba Libre, said to have been named by Brazilian soldiers (pracinhas)
  in World War II. [HIGH that the drink and name exist — Mapa da Cachaça,
  TudoGostoso, Dicionário Informal (via search); origin story MEDIUM]
  **Never stage a TCCC product in a tall glass with lime beside a cachaça
  bottle**, and never beside a caipirinha.
- **Beer is the default prior for churrasco, boteco and beach scenes.**
  Exclude it explicitly ("no beer bottles, no beer sleeves, no
  caipirinha") in those prompts.

### GENERAL NORMS

**Meal clock and light.**

| Occasion | Typical time | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Café da manhã (out of scope) | ~06:00–09:00 | LOW-MEDIUM — not independently re-checked |
| **Almoço** (main meal) | **~11:30–14:00, peak around 12:00–13:00** | MEDIUM — pt.wikipedia "Almoço" (via search) calls lunch the main meal for most Brazilians; a nutrition piece puts it at 12:00–13:00; work-schedule sources tie it to the commercial day |
| Café da tarde / lanche | ~15:30–17:30 | LOW-MEDIUM — not independently re-checked |
| **Jantar / janta** | ~19:00–21:00 | LOW-MEDIUM — no time study found; direction consistent with a lighter evening meal (one survey: 88% would switch dinner to something lighter — tier 4) |

- **§5.2 contrast**: Brazil eats lunch **two hours earlier than Mexico
  and Spain** (14:00–16:00 there). A Brazilian lunch scene is late-morning
  to early-afternoon light, and a por quilo at 12:15 is packed. [MEDIUM]
- **Janta** at home is often **the same arroz-and-feijão meal reheated**,
  or a lanche (bread, a sandwich, a pizza on weekends) — not a separate
  cuisine. [LOW-MEDIUM — not independently re-checked]
- **Light**: Brazil runs from ~5°N to ~33°S and abolished daylight saving
  in 2019, so most of the country sees sunset around **17:30–18:45** all
  year; jantar is almost always after dark, under interior light.
  [LOW-MEDIUM — model knowledge, not independently re-checked this pass]

**The daily plate (the most important norm in this file).**
- **Arroz e feijão every day.** White long-grain rice, loose and
  separate ("soltinho"), and beans stewed in their own dark broth (caldo)
  — carioca (brown-beige) in zones 1 and 3, black in zones 2 and 4;
  elsewhere mixed, with feijão-de-corda / feijão-verde in the Northeast.
  [HIGH for the daily base and the carioca/black split — see FILE ROLE
  sources]
- **Beans go on or beside the rice with their broth**, which soaks the
  edge of the rice — a lunch plate with dry beans in a separate dish
  reads foreign. Whether the beans go on top of the rice or beside it is
  a household preference (a long-running joke among Brazilians)
  [EDITORIAL; not searched].
- **Farofa** — manioc flour toasted in butter or fat, often with bacon,
  onion, egg or banana — is sprinkled at the table from a bowl or pot.
  Texture: dry, sandy, crumbly, pale gold to toasted beige, crunchy.
  Packaged ready farofa (e.g. Yoki) is sold everywhere and described
  as "for churrasco and feijoada". [HIGH for farofa as the churrasco and
  feijoada companion — Yoki listings at Pão de Açúcar and Carrefour, the
  feijoada sources; texture MEDIUM]
- **Salad is simple**: lettuce leaves, tomato slices, onion, sometimes
  cucumber or grated carrot, dressed at the table with oil, vinegar and
  salt. [MEDIUM — not independently re-checked]
- **Vinagrete** — finely diced tomato, onion and green pepper in vinegar
  and oil — with churrasco and many plates; **molho de pimenta**
  (small bottles or a jar of malagueta chillies in oil) on many tables.
  [MEDIUM — vinagrete named in the churrasco sources]
- **Cutlery**: fork and knife, Continental layout; spoons for beans at
  home. Rice and beans are eaten with a fork. [LOW-MEDIUM — not
  independently re-checked]
- **Serving at home**: food comes to the table **in its pots or in serving
  bowls**, and each person serves themselves; plates are loaded, not
  composed. [MEDIUM — not independently re-checked]

**The most common image-model failures for Brazilian food**: (1) **Mexican
or Tex-Mex conflation** — tortillas, refried beans, cheese-covered plates,
Spanish signage; (2) **the "Brazilian steakhouse" cliché** — every meal a
rodízio of skewers; (3) **açaí as the only "Brazilian food"**, always as a
sweet bowl; (4) **beans as a separate side dish** rather than on the rice.
Negate the one the scene invites. [EDITORIAL]

### VISUAL & PLATING NORMS

- **Palette**: bright white rice against brown-beige (carioca) or
  purple-black (black) beans; pale-gold farofa; deep-green couve;
  golden-brown fried salgados; the orange-red of dendê (zone 5); the
  yellow of tucupi (zone 7); orange slices on a feijoada table.
  [EDITORIAL synthesis of the dish entries]
- **Plating is abundant and informal.** The PF and the por quilo plate are
  full to the rim, components side by side and touching; home plates are
  self-served from pots. Composed fine-dining plating reads as a
  restaurant scene, not everyday Brazil. [MEDIUM — consistent with the PF
  and por quilo sources]
- **Beans with broth**: always show the broth — beans that look dry or
  mashed read as another country. [EDITORIAL]
- **Fried food is golden, not brown**: salgados and pastéis are sold
  fresh from the fryer; greasy dark or pale soggy ones read wrong.
  [EDITORIAL]
- **Colour grading**: neutral, true to the zone's light — soft grey-white
  São Paulo overcast, hard bright Rio and Northeast sun, humid Amazon
  haze. Avoid a blanket saturated "tropical" or warm cast [EDITORIAL; the
  equivalent documented "yellow filter" concern is sourced in `mexico.md`].

### ENVIRONMENT & STAGING SCENES

#### General environmental norms

- **"At home" means a house for most Brazilians.** IBGE's 2022 Census:
  **84.8% of the population lives in houses, 12.5% in apartments**, and
  2.4% in houses in a vila or condominium; apartment living rose from 8.5%
  (2010) to 12.5% (2022). **Only three of Brazil's 5,568 municipalities
  have more people in apartments than in houses: Santos (63.45%),
  Balneário Camboriú (57.22%) and São Caetano do Sul (50.77%).**
  [CONFIDENCE: HIGH — IBGE Educa, Correio Braziliense, IstoÉ Dinheiro,
  Exame, Jovem Pan, ABRAINC] [SOURCE:
  [IBGE Educa — Características dos domicílios, Censo 2022](https://educa.ibge.gov.br/jovens/conheca-o-brasil/populacao/22064-caracteristicas-dos-domicilios-censo-2022.html);
  [Correio Braziliense — 84% moram em casa](https://www.correiobraziliense.com.br/brasil/2024/02/6807513-censo-2022-84-dos-brasileiros-moram-em-casa-125-em-apartamento.html);
  [Exame — apenas três municípios têm mais apartamentos](https://exame.com/brasil/censo-2022-apenas-tres-municipios-tem-mais-apartamentos-do-que-casas-no-brasil/)]
  **§5.2 contrast**: close to Mexico (73.2% single house) and the opposite
  of Spain (65.3% in flats). **But** apartment living is concentrated in
  the big coastal and southeastern cities, where many briefs will be set;
  a middle-class São Paulo, Rio or Curitiba scene in an apartment is a
  real, co-equal register. The share of São Paulo capital specifically
  was not found this pass. [EDITORIAL on the staging default; statistics
  HIGH]
- **Interior markers (pick one or two per scene)**: ceramic or porcelain
  tile floors; white or pale painted walls; a compact kitchen with a gas
  stove and a pressure cooker (panela de pressão — beans are cooked in
  one); a dining table with a patterned plastic or cotton cloth; a
  clay water filter or a water-purifier on the counter; potted plants; a
  ceiling fan in the warm zones; a TV on in the background (screen
  blurred). [MEDIUM — uncontested general knowledge, not individually
  re-checked this pass]
- **Exterior markers**: houses behind walls and gates (muro and portão)
  with grilles; flat concrete-slab roofs with a blue or grey water tank;
  painted façades; in apartment towers, a small varanda (balcony),
  sometimes with a built-in grill ("varanda gourmet"). [MEDIUM — not
  individually re-checked]
- **Gen Z lens (§5.3)**: young adults commonly live with parents; shared
  flats (repúblicas) are the student register in university cities.
  [LOW-MEDIUM — not searched this pass; flagged in the GAP LOG] Stage a
  young adult at the family table, or in a small shared-flat kitchen with
  a laptop, plants and a fan — neither chaotic nor sterile.
- **Caricature avoidance [EDITORIAL]**:
  - **Carnival-as-everyday**: feathers, sequins, samba dancers, drums in
    an ordinary meal scene. Carnival is authentic only for an explicit
    Carnival brief (see FESTIVALS).
  - **Rio-as-Brazil**: every scene on Copacabana with Sugarloaf behind.
    Brazil's default in this file is São Paulo. Never name or depict a
    specific real landmark (Christ the Redeemer, Sugarloaf cable car,
    Maracanã) — describe the generic category ("forested granite hills
    over a beach city") per §7.5.
  - **Favela or danger framing**: hillside shacks, graffiti and poverty as
    the default "authentic" Brazil. Real working-class neighbourhoods are
    ordinary, tidy and lived-in.
  - **Amazon exoticism**: jungle, parrots, indigenous people as props.
    Zone 7 scenes are cities and markets; never stage Indigenous people
    as decoration.
  - **Hispanic conflation**: Spanish words, sombreros, tacos, Mexican
    tiles, "fiesta" styling — Brazil is Portuguese-speaking.
  - **Football everywhere**: shirts, flags, balls in every scene — only
    for an explicit match-day brief, with no readable crest or sponsor.
  - The ordinary baseline is a tidy, bright, lived-in home or a busy,
    clean lanchonete, padaria or por quilo restaurant.

#### Scenario: Casual lunch at home — 1 person

A kitchen or dining table, ~12:00–13:30, daylight from a window with a
grille or a balcony door; a plastic cloth. One plate served from the pots
on the stove: rice, beans with broth, a piece of meat (a thin steak, a
chicken thigh, a sausage), a spoon of farofa, a few tomato slices. Hero
(from the brief; formats that fit): a 350 mL can or 600 mL PET beside the
plate. Gen Z: the same plate in the family kitchen, or a marmita reheated
at a shared-flat table. [EDITORIAL; dish MEDIUM]

#### Scenario: Casual lunch at home — 2 people

Two plates at a small table; rice and beans still in their pots on
trivets or in two bowls; a shared salad bowl; a small bowl of farofa.
Or a Saturday strogonoff for two. Hero (from the brief; formats that
fit): two cans, or a 1–1.5 L bottle and two glasses. [EDITORIAL]

#### Scenario: Casual lunch at home — 3 people

The family almoço: a parent and two teenagers or three adults; rice,
beans, salad, farofa and a meat platter in the centre, each serving
themselves; or a feijoada on a Saturday. Hero (from the brief; formats
that fit): a 2 L bottle or a 1 L returnable glass bottle in the
midground, a filled glass at each place. The table gets crowded — leave
it abundant but not cluttered with props. [EDITORIAL]

#### Scenario: Dinner at home, indoors

Janta is **lighter and after dark** (~19:00–21:00): lunch's rice and
beans reheated with an egg; a sandwich of pão francês; soup in the
southern winter; or a delivery pizza on a weekend night. Warm interior
light, casual table. [LOW-MEDIUM — see GENERAL NORMS]

#### Scenario: Meal outdoors at home

- **The Sunday churrasco** — nationwide, strongest in zone 4. A covered
  back terrace or a varanda with a built-in brick grill; skewers of beef,
  sausage and chicken hearts over charcoal; a wooden board of sliced
  meat; farofa, vinagrete, rice, potato salad and garlic bread on a long
  table. Weekend afternoon. [HIGH for the home churrasco as the weekend
  and family ritual — Seara, Prezunic, TudoGostoso (Dia dos Pais) (via
  search); composition MEDIUM] See the churrasco entry.
- **Elsewhere**: a quintal (backyard) with a plastic table and chairs under
  a fruit tree; the almoço carried out. [EDITORIAL]
- Hero (from the brief; formats that fit): a 2 L bottle on the table with
  glasses, or cans in a cooler (keep branding blurred). **No beer** — the
  churrasco is the scene where beer is most expected (rule 3).

#### Scenario: Meal on the go — 1 person

The lanchonete and street register. A coxinha on a small plate or a paper
napkin on a steel counter in front of the estufa; a pastel on its paper at
a feira stand; a pão de queijo at a padaria counter; an acarajé on paper at
a Salvador tabuleiro (zone 5); a marmitex on a park bench or office desk.
Hero (from the brief; formats that fit): a 350 mL can, a 290 mL glass
bottle or a 600 mL PET beside it. **§5.2 contrast**: Brazil's grab-and-go is
**counter-based** — people stand or sit at a lanchonete counter — rather
than Mexico's stand-on-every-corner street stalls or the US drive-thru.
[EDITORIAL; not a measured claim]

#### Scenario: Away from home — 1 person at a restaurant/café

A **por quilo** restaurant at 12:30: one person at a small table with a
large white plate built from the buffet. Or a PF at a neighbourhood
restaurant counter. Hero (from the brief; formats that fit): a can or a
600 mL PET. [HIGH for por quilo as an everyday lunch institution — see
its entry]

#### Scenario: Away from home — 2–3 people

A churrascaria rodízio table; a boteco table on the pavement with shared
porções (bolinhos, pastéis, mandioca frita) — **beer excluded explicitly**;
a Saturday feijoada at a restaurant; a Belém market stall with tacacá in
the late afternoon (zone 7). Plain white plastic folding tables and chairs
are authentic for botecos and beaches; branded ones carry legible logos —
**stage unbranded furniture** [EDITORIAL]. Hero (from the brief; formats
that fit): cans or glass bottles, or a 1–2 L bottle with glasses.

   capixaba in a black clay pot.
   → catalog: Feijoada; Biscoito Globo beach; Açaí; Moqueca (capixaba).
3. **Minas Gerais & Centre-West** — Hill towns, wood stoves, cheese and
   pork. Pão de queijo, feijão tropeiro, tutu, frango com quiabo, torresmo,
   doce de leite; the comida por quilo was invented in Belo Horizonte.
   → catalog: Feijão tropeiro; Pão de queijo; Por quilo.
4. **South** — Pampas, Serra Gaúcha vineyards, immigrant towns, real
   winters. Churrasco as ritual (sal grosso, espeto), arroz carreteiro,
   galeto al primo canto with polenta, chimarrão everywhere (never staged
   beside the hero). Black beans.
   → catalog: Churrasco; Churrascaria; Arroz carreteiro; Galeto.
5. **Bahia** — Pelourinho colour, Atlantic beaches, dendê. Acarajé,
   abará, vatapá, caruru, moqueca baiana, bobó de camarão. Religious
   sensitivity (Candomblé). **Spinout candidate.**
   → catalog: Acarajé; Moqueca (baiana).
6. **Northeast** — Coast and sertão. Carne de sol, queijo coalho,
   manteiga de garrafa, baião de dois, cuscuz, tapioca, macaxeira;
   São João; Guaraná Jesus in Maranhão.
   → catalog: Baião de dois; Cuscuz; Tapioca; Festa junina.
7. **North / Amazon** — River ports, markets, humid heat. Açaí with fried
   fish, tacacá, pato no tucupi, maniçoba, river fish (tambaqui, pirarucu,
   filhote), farinha d'água. **Spinout candidate.**
   → catalog: Açaí (paraense); Tacacá.

### A. National core

#### Prato feito (PF) — the everyday lunch plate

- **Category**: Everyday lunch, Monday–Friday; restaurant counters,
  lanchonetes, botecos, marmitex; the same logic at home.
- **Lineage**: Brazilian. A popular story traces it to meals for railway
  workers under British railway companies, as an adaptation of "fish and
  chips" [LOW — one tier-4 blog; do not repeat as fact].
- **Form**: a plate **made up in the kitchen** (not self-served) with
  **rice, beans, a meat, fried potatoes and a simple salad**; the meat
  options are a thin steak (bife, often **bife acebolado** under soft
  onions), a breaded steak (bife à milanesa), breaded fish, a pork chop,
  grilled chicken; sides can swap fries for pasta, breaded aubergine or
  mashed potato; farofa is common. [HIGH — Homem na Cozinha, Renato
  Fontes, Seara, Bom Gourmet "PF em 6 cidades" (via search)]
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **the bean colour follows the zone** (carioca in
  1 and 3; black in 2 and 4); the São Paulo **weekly calendar** sets the
  day's PF (virado Monday, bife à rolê Tuesday, feijoada Wednesday, pasta
  Thursday, fish Friday — see Virado à paulista) [HIGH — Guia da Semana,
  São Paulo Secreto, Terra, Viva a Cidade]; regional PFs add couve,
  a fried egg, macaxeira (Northeast) or vinagrete. **Default when
  unspecified**: bife acebolado with carioca beans, fries, lettuce-tomato
  salad and a spoon of farofa, zone 1. [EDITORIAL]
- **Format (§4.4)**: plated (restaurant or home) or **marmitex** (the same
  food packed flat into an aluminium tray with a card lid, compartments
  merged). Give each its own composition (below).
- **Vessel & venue**: an oval or round white porcelain or melamine plate
  ~26–30 cm, **full to the rim**; a steel counter or a small table with a
  paper placemat; cutlery wrapped in a paper napkin.
- **Texture & finish**: rice white, loose and fluffy; beans glossy in their
  broth, the broth seeping into the rice edge; steak thin, browned, a
  little juice; onions soft, translucent, gold-edged, not crisp; fries
  golden, straight-cut, some slightly limp from sitting on the plate;
  lettuce bright; farofa dry and sandy.
- **Model failure**: a US steak dinner (thick steak, baked potato);
  Mexican rice-and-refried-beans; beans served dry in a ramekin; a
  composed fine-dining plate; a Cuban/Puerto Rican moros plate (mixed
  rice and beans — rule out by keeping rice white and beans separate on
  top or beside).
- **Confidence**: HIGH for the components; MEDIUM for the plate layout.
- **Sources**: [Homem na Cozinha — Prato Feito](https://homemnacozinha.com/prato-feito-descubra-os-segredos-e-vantagens-desse-classico/);
  [Bom Gourmet — PF em 6 cidades](https://bomgourmet.com/bomgourmet/produtos-ingredientes/prato-feito-em-cidades-brasileiras/) (via search);
  [Terra — prato-feito do dia em São Paulo](https://www.terra.com.br/vida-e-estilo/degusta/prato-feito-do-dia-quais-sao-os-pratos-servidos-em-cada-dia-da-semana-em-sao-paulo,796c7a1ec3aa1d234762268fad8067efkilmbl6s.html).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one plated PF, bife acebolado.
  - **What dominates**: **rice and beans together are half the plate.**
    Rice ~30% of the visible surface; beans ~15–20% (on or against the
    rice); steak with onions ~20%; fries ~15%; salad ~10–15%; farofa ~5%.
    No component is a garnish; everything is a real portion. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count (plate) | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | White rice | a mound ~12–14 cm across, ~4 cm high (about 1½ cups cooked) | 1 mound | Bright white, loose separate grains, faint gloss | One side of the plate, a gently domed heap |
    | Beans (carioca) | ~1 ladle (~150 mL); beans ~1–1.5 cm each | 1 ladle | Light-brown beige whole beans in thick brown broth | Ladled **over one edge of the rice** or right beside it; broth staining the rice edge brown |
    | Bife (thin steak) | ~12–15 cm long, **~1 cm thick** — about as long as the can is tall, far thinner than the can is wide | 1 | Browned, slightly irregular edges, a little pan juice | Opposite the rice, lying flat |
    | Onions (acebolado) | Rings/half-rings ~0.5 cm wide | a loose tangle, ~½ onion | Soft, translucent, golden edges, glossy | **Draped over the steak**, covering half to two-thirds of it |
    | Batata frita | Straight-cut sticks ~7–9 cm long, ~1 cm thick | ~12–20 | Golden, matte, a few slightly bent | A heap beside the steak, touching it |
    | Salad | 2–3 lettuce leaves ~8–10 cm; 2–3 tomato slices ~5–6 cm (smaller than the can's width); a few raw onion rings | as listed | Bright green, red, white; undressed or a sheen of oil | A small cluster at the plate edge |
    | Farofa | ~2 tablespoons | 1 small heap | Pale gold, dry, sandy, crumbly | Beside the beans or sprinkled over them |

  - **Arrangement**: components in **adjacent heaps that touch**, filling
    the plate to the rim with almost no bare plate showing; no stacking
    except onions on the steak and beans over the rice edge.
  - **Vessel fill and depth**: 90–100% of the plate surface covered;
    rice and fries are the tallest (~4 cm); a rim sliver shows at most.
  - **Served portion vs. whole dish**: the plate *is* the portion.
    **Marmitex version**: the same components pressed flat into a
    ~20 × 15 cm foil tray, rice and beans taking half, steak and fries
    overlapping the other half, salad usually omitted or in a separate
    small cup, card lid folded back beside it.
  - **State cues**: light steam off the rice and beans; bean broth glossy;
    onion sheen; fries matte-golden, not glistening.
  - **Absent on purpose**: tortillas, refried/mashed beans, melted cheese,
    herb garnish sprigs, sauce swooshes, a baked potato, a side ramekin of
    beans, lemon on the steak.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A full white oval plate, food touching edge to
    edge: a domed heap of fluffy separate white rice with a ladle of whole
    light-brown beans in thick brown broth spilling over its edge; beside
    it a thin browned steak, about as long as the can is tall and far
    thinner, draped with soft golden onion rings; a heap of golden
    straight-cut fries; two lettuce leaves and three small tomato slices;
    a spoon of dry sandy toasted flour. No tortillas, no cheese."

#### Comida por quilo — the pay-by-weight buffet plate

- **Category**: Everyday weekday lunch; offices, city centres, shopping
  districts, nationwide.
- **Lineage**: Brazilian invention — **the first por quilo restaurant was
  "Isto é aQuilo", opened in Belo Horizonte in 1984 by chef Fred da Mata
  Machado**; it spread nationwide within a few years and reached Portugal
  in the late 1990s. An alternative Santos claim exists; the industry
  consensus is Belo Horizonte. [HIGH — pt.wikipedia, Abrasel, Estado de
  Minas, Revista Encontro (via search)]
- **Form**: a long heated (and a chilled) buffet counter of stainless
  trays; the diner builds a plate and **it is weighed at the end** before
  paying; many have a separate grill station where meat is carved onto the
  plate (charged by weight too). Typical spread: white rice, brown rice,
  two bean pots, salads, pasta, a lasagna, stews, fried and grilled
  meats, farofa, vegetables, a dessert counter. [HIGH for the system;
  MEDIUM for the spread — uncontested, not item-by-item searched]
- **Variants (§4.6)**: everyday office por quilo (default); upscale
  por quilo with sushi and a larger grill; **churrasco por quilo** where
  the grill dominates. [EDITORIAL]
- **Vessel & venue**: a large white plate ~28–30 cm; plastic trays at
  some; small tables with placemats; the buffet and the (blurred) scale in
  the background.
- **Texture & finish**: a **mosaic** of many small portions — every item
  a distinct colour and texture next to the next; nothing sauced over
  everything.
- **Model failure**: a Chinese buffet or a US salad bar; a hotel breakfast
  buffet; a single dish on the plate; a legible price-per-kilo board or
  scale display (rule 1).
- **Confidence**: HIGH for the format; EDITORIAL for plate composition.
- **Sources**: [Abrasel — comida a quilo, onde foi inventada](https://abrasel.com.br/noticias/noticias/comida-a-quilo-voce-sabe-onde-foi-inventada/);
  [Estado de Minas — comida a quilo completa 40 anos](https://www.em.com.br/degusta/2024/09/6951299-comida-a-quilo-completa-40-anos-voce-sabia-que-essa-ideia-surgiu-em-bh.html);
  [pt.wikipedia — Comida por quilo](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comida_por_quilo) (via search).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one diner's plate.
  - **What dominates**: **rice-and-beans still anchor the plate** (~30%
    together), then salad (~25%), one or two hot dishes (~25%), a piece of
    grilled meat (~15%), small extras (~5%). Six to nine distinct small
    heaps. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | White rice | ~10 cm heap, ~3 cm high | 1 | White, loose | One sector |
    | Beans | ~½ ladle | 1 | Carioca or black with broth, per zone | Beside or on the rice |
    | Salad leaves + raw veg | lettuce/rocket pile ~10 cm; grated carrot ~2 tbsp; 3–4 cherry tomatoes or tomato slices; a few beet cubes | 3–5 items | Bright green, orange, red, magenta — the most colourful sector | Opposite the rice, in small separate piles |
    | Hot dish (e.g. lasagna square, strogonoff, stewed chicken) | lasagna ~7 × 7 cm (a little wider than the can); stew ~1 serving spoon | 1–2 | Varies; one sauced item at most | Between rice and salad |
    | Grilled meat | 2–3 slices ~8 × 4 cm × 1.5 cm or 1 chicken thigh | 1–3 | Browned, charred edges | On top of the edge of the rice or a clear sector |
    | Farofa / potato salad / fried item | 1–2 tbsp; 1 small fried item | 1–2 | Pale gold; creamy white | Filling the gaps |

  - **Arrangement**: sector-by-sector around the plate, items **touching
    but distinct**; nothing stacked except beans on rice and meat on rice
    edge.
  - **Vessel fill and depth**: 85–100% covered; low (2–4 cm), a little rim
    visible.
  - **Served portion vs. whole dish**: the plate is the portion; the
    buffet trays behind are the "whole dish" and should read as a blurred
    stainless row.
  - **State cues**: steam on hot items; salad crisp and wet-bright;
    grilled meat just carved (pink centre on beef).
  - **Absent on purpose**: a legible scale display or price board; a
    buffet of one cuisine only; chopsticks; plates piled high like a
    mountain.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A large white plate built from a lunch buffet,
    a mosaic of small separate heaps touching: fluffy white rice with a
    half-ladle of beans in broth, a pile of bright lettuce with grated
    carrot, beet cubes and tomato slices, a square of lasagna a little
    wider than the can, three slices of grilled beef with charred edges,
    and a spoon of dry toasted flour. Stainless buffet trays blurred
    behind; no readable signs or scale display."

#### Feijoada completa (Wednesday and Saturday)

- **Category**: Weekly special — **Wednesdays and Saturdays** at
  restaurants and bars, Saturday lunch at home; long, heavy, social.
  [HIGH — Comércio São Paulo, CNN Brasil guide, GoWhere (via search)]
- **Lineage**: Brazilian, from Portuguese stew traditions (the often-told
  "slave-kitchen invention" story is contested by food historians
  [LOW-MEDIUM — not re-checked this pass]; do not repeat it in copy).
- **Form**: **black beans** stewed with salted and smoked pork and beef —
  carne seca, paio, linguiça, costela, lombo, bacon, and in the
  "traditional" version pé, rabo and orelha (trotter, tail, ear). A
  **feijoada completa** comes with **five accompaniments: white rice,
  couve refogada, farofa, torresmo and orange** (sliced or in segments);
  also molho de pimenta, and in some restaurants bisteca, banana à
  milanesa and vinagrete. [HIGH — São Paulo restaurant guides naming the
  same five, Bolinha, Bar Original, Pateo (via search)]
- **Variants (§4.6)**: **carioca feijoada** — black beans, the Rio
  standard (default); São Paulo restaurant feijoada — also black beans
  despite carioca everyday beans; "light" feijoada without the offal cuts;
  Northeastern feijoada sometimes with carioca or mulatinho beans and
  vegetables [LOW-MEDIUM — not re-checked]. **Default when unspecified**:
  black-bean feijoada completa with the five sides, Saturday lunch.
  [EDITORIAL]
- **Format (§4.4)**: served (a) **in its pot** (clay or cast iron) with
  meats **separated** into small bowls in restaurants, or all together;
  (b) plated.
- **Vessel & venue**: a dark clay or cast-iron pot ~20–30 cm; small bowls
  for couve, farofa, torresmo; orange on a small plate; white rice in a
  bowl; at home the pot on a trivet at the table.
- **Texture & finish**: beans **glossy purple-black**, broth thick and
  dark, meat pieces rosy-brown to dark; couve very fine and bright green;
  farofa dry and sandy; torresmo puffed, blistered, golden, crunchy;
  orange slices bright, wet, peeled.
- **Model failure**: black bean soup with toppings; Cuban black beans;
  cassoulet with white beans; chili; beans puréed; orange juice instead of
  orange slices.
- **Sources**: [Comércio São Paulo — o ritual de quarta e sábado](https://comerciosaopaulo.com.br/melhor-feijoada-em-sao-paulo-o-ritual-de-quarta-e-sabado/);
  [CNN Brasil — guia da feijoada em São Paulo](https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/viagemegastronomia/gastronomia/guia-da-feijoada-em-sao-paulo-onde-comer-em-mais-de-20-enderecos/).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one plated portion (and the pot).
  - **What dominates**: on the plate, **rice and black beans together are
    ~60%** (rice ~30%, beans-with-meat ~30–35%); couve ~15%; farofa ~10%;
    orange ~5–10%; torresmo pieces accents. **Meat is a minority of the
    beans ladle** — 3–5 small pieces among many beans. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count (plate) | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | White rice | ~12 cm heap | 1 | White, loose | One side |
    | Black beans + broth | 1½–2 ladles | 1 | Glossy purple-black whole beans in thick dark broth | Over and beside the rice, broth running into it |
    | Meats | chunks ~2–4 cm (carne seca shreds, paio/linguiça coins ~2–3 cm and ~1 cm thick, a rib bone ~6–8 cm) | 3–5 pieces | Rosy-brown to dark, glossy with bean broth | **Half-sunk in the beans**, not piled on top |
    | Couve refogada | ribbons 2–3 mm wide; heap ~8 cm | 1 heap | Deep glossy green, slightly wilted, garlic flecks | Beside the beans |
    | Farofa | 2–3 tbsp | 1 heap or a sprinkle | Pale gold, sandy | Sprinkled over the beans or beside |
    | Torresmo | pieces ~3–5 cm (about half the can's width to its width) | 2–3 | Puffed, blistered golden skin, fat layer below | At the plate edge |
    | Orange | rounds ~7 cm, peeled, ~0.5–1 cm thick (about the can's width) | 2–3 slices or 4–6 segments | Bright orange, juicy | On the plate rim or a side saucer |

  - **Arrangement (pot)**: in the pot the beans fill to ~2–3 cm below the
    rim, a few meat pieces breaking the surface; in restaurants, meats may
    be lifted into a separate steel dish, each cut in its own pile.
  - **Vessel fill and depth**: plate 90–100% covered; beans pool ~1 cm deep
    on the plate.
  - **Served portion vs. whole dish**: the pot and its side bowls stay on
    the table; each person builds a plate. Show one built plate in front,
    pot and bowls behind.
  - **State cues**: steam from pot and beans; bean broth glossy and
    thick; torresmo crisp; couve bright, not grey.
  - **Absent on purpose**: tortilla chips, sour cream, cheese, coriander,
    avocado, white or brown beans, puréed beans; **caipirinha and beer**
    (the default drinks at a restaurant feijoada — rule 3).
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A plate of fluffy white rice with a generous
    ladle of glossy whole black beans in thick dark broth spilling over
    it, a few small pieces of sausage and dried beef half-sunk among the
    beans; beside them a heap of very fine glossy dark-green collard
    ribbons, a sprinkle of dry sandy toasted flour, two pieces of puffed
    golden pork crackling and two peeled orange slices about the width of
    the can. A dark clay pot of the beans behind."

#### Churrasco em casa — the home barbecue

- **Category**: Weekend and family ritual — Sundays, Father's Day,
  birthdays; strongest in zone 4, nationwide.
- **Lineage**: Southern Brazilian gaúcho tradition, now national.
  Distinct from Argentine/Uruguayan asado: Brazilian churrasco is
  **skewer-based** and seasoned with **coarse salt (sal grosso)**; the
  Uruguay file documents the contrast. [HIGH for the skewer and sal grosso
  distinction — `uruguay.md` sources, Seara (via search)]
- **Form**: charcoal in a built-in brick grill (churrasqueira de
  alvenaria) or a portable one; skewers or a grate; **picanha** with its
  fat cap, **linguiça** (fresh pork sausage), **coração de frango**
  (chicken hearts on skewers), chicken wings and thighs, pork ribs;
  **pão de alho** (garlic bread) and **queijo coalho** on skewers; sides:
  **farofa, vinagrete, white rice, maionese (potato salad), green salad**.
  [HIGH for picanha, linguiça, hearts, pão de alho, queijo coalho,
  vinagrete, farofa, rice — Seara, Prezunic, Yoki (via search); built-in
  brick grill MEDIUM — not re-checked]
- **Variants (§4.6)**: gaúcho (zone 4) — big cuts, costela fogo de chão
  (whole rib racks by a ground fire), more restraint on sides [MEDIUM —
  not re-checked]; everyday urban churrasco on an apartment varanda with a
  small grill (default in zones 1–2); Northeastern with queijo coalho and
  carne de sol. **Default when unspecified**: a São Paulo backyard or
  varanda churrasco, picanha slices on a board. [EDITORIAL]
- **Format (§4.4)**: meat **sliced on a board and passed around**; people
  eat from small plates, often standing; the "table" is a board of meat
  plus bowls of sides.
- **Vessel & venue**: wooden board ~30–45 cm; bowls of farofa and
  vinagrete; a long table; small plates; the grill smoking in the
  background.
- **Texture & finish**: picanha — thick slices with a **white-gold fat
  cap**, crusty with visible salt crystals, **pink-to-red centre**;
  linguiça glossy brown, split; hearts dark and shiny, slightly charred;
  pão de alho golden with bubbling cream-coloured filling; queijo coalho
  golden-brown on its faces, holding its shape.
- **Model failure**: US barbecue (sauce-glazed ribs, brisket, burgers,
  corn on the cob with butter); neat crosshatch grill marks; well-done grey
  steak; kebabs with vegetables between the meat; **beer** (rule 3).
- **Sources**: [Seara — churrasco em casa](https://www.seara.com.br/materia/10-dicas-para-mandar-bem-no-churrasco-em-casa/);
  [Prezunic — churrasco no domingo](https://blog.prezunic.com.br/institucional/dicas-de-churrasco-para-aproveitar-o-domingo-ao-lado-do-seu-pai/);
  [TudoGostoso — churrasco de Dia dos Pais](https://www.tudogostoso.com.br/noticias/churrasco-de-dia-dos-pais-a22206.htm).
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — the board and one plate.
  - **What dominates**: **the board of meat** (~50% of the table's food
    area): picanha slices dominate the board (~60% of it), sausage pieces
    ~25%, hearts ~15%. On a plate: meat ~45%, farofa ~15%, vinagrete ~15%,
    rice ~15%, a piece of pão de alho ~10%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count (board / plate) | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Picanha slices | ~10–12 cm long, **~1.5–2 cm thick** (thinner than the can is wide), each with a ~1 cm fat cap along one edge | 8–12 / 2–3 | Crusty salted exterior, white-gold fat cap, pink-red centre, juice on the board | Fanned in a row on the board, fat caps all on one side |
    | Linguiça | links ~12–15 cm, cut into ~3–4 cm pieces | 6–10 pieces / 2 | Glossy brown, split skin, charred spots | A heap at one end of the board |
    | Coração de frango | ~2–3 cm each | 10–20 / 4–6 | Dark red-brown, shiny, slightly charred | On a skewer or loose in a small heap |
    | Pão de alho | ~10–12 cm mini-baguette (a little shorter than the can is tall) | 3–6 / 1 | Golden crust, split, cream-yellow garlic filling bubbling | Side plate |
    | Queijo coalho | bar ~8–10 cm × 2 cm × 2 cm on a stick | 2–4 / 0–1 | Golden-brown faces, white inside, holding its shape | Side plate |
    | Farofa | 3–4 tbsp per plate | 1 bowl | Pale gold, sandy | Bowl on the table; heap on the plate |
    | Vinagrete | 3 tbsp per plate; dice ~5 mm | 1 bowl | Red, white and green dice glistening in vinegar | Bowl; spooned onto the plate |
    | Rice / potato salad | small heaps ~8 cm | bowls | White; creamy white with potato cubes | Bowls on the table |

  - **Arrangement**: board centred, sides in bowls around it, small
    plates stacked; the grill with skewers out of focus behind.
  - **Vessel fill and depth**: board ~60–70% covered, juice pooling in its
    groove; plates half-full (people go back for more).
  - **Served portion vs. whole dish**: a skewer is carved onto the board,
    then onto plates; a portion is 2–3 slices plus sides.
  - **State cues**: smoke haze from the grill behind; meat juices glossy;
    visible coarse salt crystals on the crust.
  - **Absent on purpose**: barbecue sauce, grill-mark crosshatch,
    burgers, corn cobs with butter, vegetable kebabs, beer bottles in
    insulated sleeves, caipirinhas.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A worn wooden cutting board on an outdoor
    table with a fanned row of thick picanha beef slices, each thinner
    than the can is wide, with a white-gold fat cap on one edge, a crusty
    coarse-salt exterior and a pink juicy centre; a small heap of split
    glossy sausage pieces and dark chicken hearts at one end. Around it,
    bowls of dry toasted flour and diced tomato-onion relish, garlic bread
    split and bubbling. A brick barbecue with skewers smoking softly
    behind. No beer, no sauce."

#### Churrascaria rodízio — restaurant barbecue

- **Category**: Special occasion, celebrations, visitors; nationwide.
- **Form**: **rodízio** (also "espeto corrido") — servers (passadores)
  carry long skewers of meat through the room and **carve directly onto
  the diner's plate**; a two-sided card (green = send meat, red = pause)
  sits by each plate, a system reported from the 1970s; **picanha folded
  in a C on the skewer and sliced thin at the table** is the signature
  cut; a large buffet of salads and hot sides. [HIGH — Wikipedia
  "Churrascaria" (via search), O Casarão, Devour Destinations, Churrascaria
  Palace]
- **Vessel & venue**: large white plates, a steel side plate, heavy
  cutlery, cloth napkins; a big hall with wood and stone, white-cloth
  tables. The card usually has printed words — **keep it blank or
  unreadable**, colour only.
- **Texture & finish**: as the home churrasco, but slices **thin and
  curled** (carved off the skewer); the skewer's meat shows a dark salted
  crust and a fat cap.
- **Model failure**: Fogo de Chão-style US steakhouse clichés with gaucho
  costumes; a hand holding the skewer in frame (rule 5) — show the skewer
  resting or out of focus; the card with readable text.

#### Pastel de feira

- **Category**: Street/market snack; weekday and weekend mornings at the
  feira livre; also pastelarias.
- **Lineage**: Brazilian, traced to **Japanese immigrants in Santos/São
  Paulo in the 1940s** adapting spring-roll and gyoza doughs; the dough
  uses **cachaça** (evaporates in the fryer, making the bubbles).
  [MEDIUM — putzkifome, Café Doce Encontro and Wikipedia (via search)
  agree on the story; origin claims are hard to prove]
- **Form**: a **flat rectangle** (sometimes half-moon) of thin wheat dough,
  sealed, deep-fried to order; fillings: carne (seasoned ground beef),
  queijo (mozzarella), frango, palmito, pizza (cheese, tomato, oregano),
  camarão. Served on a sheet of paper or a paper napkin; vinegar-pepper
  sauce and ketchup/mustard squeeze bottles on the counter. Partner:
  **caldo de cana** (never staged — rule 4). [HIGH for pastel + caldo de
  cana at the feira; MEDIUM for fillings]

  (~8–10 cm); boteco porção of mini pastéis. [EDITORIAL]

- **Category**: Street food, late afternoon and evening, Salvador and the

- **Lineage**: **Afro-Brazilian**, of West African origin; a fritter of
  **peeled black-eyed pea (feijão-fradinho) paste with onion and salt,
  deep-fried in dendê (red palm oil)**; sold from the **tabuleiro** by
  the **baiana de acarajé**. [HIGH — pt.wikipedia "Acarajé" (via search),
  Aviva, Vai Se Food] The baianas' craft is recognised as national
  intangible heritage (IPHAN) and acarajé is also a Candomblé offering
  food [MEDIUM — not independently re-checked this pass].
- **Form**: the fritter **split open and filled with vatapá** (a thick
  paste of bread, shrimp, peanut/cashew, coconut milk and dendê), **caruru**
  (okra stewed with dried shrimp and dendê), **camarão seco** (dried
  smoked shrimp), **vinagrete** (often green tomato) and **pimenta**
  (hot pepper sauce) — the customer chooses "quente" (with pepper) or
  "frio". [HIGH — pt.wikipedia, SEGS, Vai Se Food (via search)]
- **Hard staging rules (see file-wide rule 7)**: stage it as the street
  food it is — on paper on the tabuleiro's edge or a small table. **Never**
  with offerings, candles, beads, at a terreiro or in a ritual. The baiana
  may appear softly in the background as the vendor, never as a costume
  prop or caricature. [EDITORIAL]
- **Texture & finish**: fritter **deep reddish-brown to orange-bronze
  from the dendê, crisp craggy surface**, dense pale-orange crumb; vatapá
  **thick, smooth, yellow-orange paste**; caruru dark olive-green-brown
  with okra rings; dried shrimp **whole, bright coral-orange, curled,
  shells on**, laid across the top.
- **Model failure**: a falafel pita; an Indian bhaji; a burger; shrimp
  pale grey-pink (fresh) instead of dried coral.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one acarajé "completo".
  - **What dominates**: **the fritter ~50%** of the silhouette; fillings
    bulging from the split ~35%; shrimp on top ~15%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Fritter | **~10–12 cm long, ~6–7 cm wide, ~4 cm tall** — about the can's width, a little shorter than its height [LOW — not searched] | 1 | Reddish-bronze, crisp, craggy, oil-sheened | Split lengthwise like a bun, lying on paper |
    | Vatapá | ~2 tbsp | 1 | Thick smooth yellow-orange paste | Spread inside the split, bulging |
    | Caruru | ~1 tbsp | 1 | Dark olive-green with okra rings | Beside the vatapá inside |
    | Vinagrete | ~1 tbsp, dice ~5 mm | 1 | Green-tomato and onion dice | On top of the fillings |
    | Camarão seco | whole dried shrimp ~4–6 cm | 3–5 | Bright coral-orange, curled, shells on | Laid across the top in a row |
    | Paper | ~20 cm square | 1 | White, orange-oil spotted | Under it |

  - **Arrangement**: split fritter overflowing, shrimp lined up on top.
  - **Vessel fill and depth**: on paper or a small plate; the tabuleiro's
    dendê pot blurred behind.
  - **Served portion vs. whole dish**: one per person.
  - **State cues**: fresh from the oil — sheen, a faint steam.
  - **Absent on purpose**: pita, lettuce, yoghurt sauce, a bun, ritual
    objects, beads, candles.
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "On a square of white paper, one oval
    reddish-bronze fritter about as wide as the can and a little shorter
    than it is tall, crisp and craggy with an orange oil sheen, split
    lengthwise and stuffed with a thick smooth yellow-orange paste and a
    dark green okra stew, topped with finely diced green tomato and onion
    and a row of four whole dried coral-orange shrimp. A pot of red-orange
    oil blurred behind. No pita, no lettuce."

#### Tacacá (zone 7, Belém)

- **Category**: Street food, **late afternoon (~17:00–19:00)**, Belém and
  the Amazon cities. [HIGH — Sabor na Rua ("se come em cuia às 18h"),
  Prática, DOL (via search)]
- **Lineage**: Indigenous Amazonian.
- **Form**: a hot, sour broth of **tucupi** (yellow fermented manioc
  juice) with **goma** (translucent tapioca starch gel), **jambu** leaves
  (a tingling, mouth-numbing herb) and **dried shrimp**, served in a
  **cuia** (gourd bowl) set in a straw or fibre ring; sold by
  **tacacazeiras** from pavement stalls. [HIGH — same sources]
- **Texture & finish**: broth **clear-to-cloudy bright yellow**, thin;
  goma **translucent, glassy, stringy blobs**; jambu **dark green leaves
  and stems, wilted**; shrimp coral-orange; cuia exterior matte brown,
  interior dark-lacquered black.
- **Model failure**: a Thai curry soup; ramen; a coconut-shell bowl
  (shaggy) instead of a smooth gourd.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one cuia.
  - **What dominates**: **the yellow broth ~50%** of the surface; goma
    ~20%; jambu ~20%; shrimp ~10%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Cuia | ~12–16 cm across, ~6–7 cm deep — about twice the can's width | 1 | Smooth gourd, matte brown outside, glossy black inside | In a woven fibre ring on the stall counter |
    | Tucupi broth | fills to ~1–2 cm below rim | 1 | Bright yellow, thin, steaming | Base |

    | Camarão seco | ~4–6 cm | 3–5 | Coral-orange, curled | On top |

  - **Arrangement**: shrimp and jambu around the rim; goma in the middle.
  - **Vessel fill and depth**: nearly full.
  - **Served portion vs. whole dish**: one cuia per person; the vendor's
    big aluminium pots behind.
  - **State cues**: steam, humid evening light.
  - **Absent on purpose**: noodles, coconut milk, chilli oil, lime wedges,
    chopsticks, a spoon standing in it (it is sipped; a toothpick is used
    for the shrimp).
  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A smooth round gourd bowl about twice the width

    noodles, no coconut milk."

#### Tapioca (zone 6; also Morning Module)

- **Category**: Snack, breakfast and late-afternoon; street tapioqueiras
  in the Northeast (Olinda, Fortaleza); now nationwide. [MEDIUM — not
  independently re-checked this pass]

- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one folded tapioca.

#### Moqueca — baiana (zone 5) and capixaba (zone 2, Espírito Santo)

- **Category**: Weekend, restaurant and family meal on the coast.
- **Form-changing (§4.2), offered as choices (§4.6)**:
  - **Moqueca baiana**: fish and/or shrimp stewed with **dendê and coconut
    milk**, onion, tomato, peppers, coriander — **thick, rich, deep orange**;
    served with white rice, **pirão** (manioc porridge made with the fish
    broth) and farofa de dendê. [HIGH — Prática, Bem Paraná, Unilever FS,
    Estado de Minas (via search); sides MEDIUM]
  - **Moqueca capixaba**: fish stewed with **olive oil, urucum (annatto),
    tomato, onion and coriander — no dendê, no coconut milk**; lighter,
    red-orange and clearer; **always in the black handmade clay pot
    (panela de barro) of the paneleiras de Goiabeiras**, Vitória. [HIGH —
    same sources]
  **Default when unspecified**: baiana (the more widely known), but ask
  if the brief names Espírito Santo. [EDITORIAL]
- **Vessel**: the clay pot **brought bubbling to the table** on a wooden
  trivet; baiana often also in clay (colour varies) [LOW-MEDIUM].
- **Model failure**: Thai or Indian curry; bouillabaisse; paella.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — the pot at the table.
  - **What dominates**: **the sauce ~50%** of the pot's surface; fish steaks
    ~30%; pepper/onion/tomato rings ~15%; coriander ~5%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count (pot / portion) | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|

    | Sauce | fills to ~2 cm below rim | — | Baiana: deep orange, creamy, oil beads on top; capixaba: red-orange, thinner, clearer | Base |

    | Coriander | chopped | a handful | Bright green | Scattered over |

  - **Arrangement**: fish evenly spaced, rings layered on top (a moqueca is
    assembled in layers, not stirred).
  - **Served portion vs. whole dish**: spooned onto a plate over white rice
    — **one fish steak, a spoon of sauce, rice, and a spoon of pale
    beige-gold pirão**; farofa on the side.
  - **State cues**: bubbling at the edges, steam, oil sheen (baiana).
  - **Absent on purpose**: mussels and clams heaped (not typical), lemon
    halves, noodles, naan.
  - **Prompt-ready line (baiana)**: "A wide, low clay pot about four cans

### D. Sweets and party table

#### Ceia de Natal (24 December, night)

- **Category**: Christmas Eve supper, late at night, in summer.
- **Form**: roast **turkey or chester** (a branded large chicken — never
  show the brand) or **tender** (glazed ham with cloves and pineapple),
  **arroz à grega** (white rice with raisins, carrot, peas, corn),
  **farofa** (often with dried fruit or bacon), **salpicão** (shredded
  chicken salad with mayonnaise, carrot, corn, raisins, apple, often topped
  with batata palha), bacalhau, lentils; desserts **rabanada** (fried
  sweet bread with cinnamon sugar) and **panetone**. [HIGH — Ministério do
  Turismo, Terra, Unit, Blablacar (via search)]
- **Model failure**: a winter snow-scene Christmas; US cranberry sauce and
  pumpkin pie; Mexican bacalao a la vizcaína.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — the table.
  - **What dominates**: **the roast bird or ham** (centrepiece ~30%), rice
    ~20%, farofa ~15%, salpicão ~15%, sweets ~20%. [EDITORIAL]
  - **Component table**:

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |

  - **Absent on purpose**: snow, fireplaces with stockings, cranberry

#### Cuscuz nordestino (zone 6; breakfast and janta)

- **Category**: **Everyday breakfast in the Northeast, also eaten at
  janta** and as a side. [HIGH — Seara, Cozinha Técnica, Amigo Foods,
  Michelin Guide Brasil title (via search)] Because it is a real **janta**
  food, it may be staged in the in-scope dinner scenario for zone 6.
- **Form**: **flocão** (pre-cooked flaked corn) moistened with salted
  water and **steamed in a cuscuzeira** into a soft, fluffy cake; turned
  out whole; served with butter, fried or scrambled egg, queijo coalho,
  carne de sol. [HIGH — same sources]
- **Model failure**: North African couscous (small semolina grains);
  polenta; cornbread.
- **Composition & proportions (§4.7)** — one plate.
  - **What dominates**: **the yellow corn cake ~55%**; egg ~20%; cheese or
    meat ~20%; butter ~5%. [EDITORIAL]

    | Component | Real size | Count | Look | Where it sits |
    |---|---|---|---|---|

  - **Prompt-ready line**: "A squat dome of steamed bright-yellow flaked
